# Usuzumi Ink (薄墨)

**Usuzumi** (薄墨) means "thin" or "pale" ink. It is sumi diluted until it turns soft grey instead of deep black.[^1]

---

## Two Traditions

### 1. Condolence Etiquette

In Japan, the name on a **kōden envelope** (香典袋, the envelope for money given at a funeral) is traditionally written in usuzumi.[^3][^4][^5][^6] People usually give two reasons for this:[^2]

- **Tears** fell into the inkstone and thinned the ink.
- **Haste:** the news came so suddenly that there was no time to grind the ink to full strength.

Either way, the pale grey reads as grief that the hand couldn't hide. Using it for happy occasions is considered a faux pas.

### 2. Ink Painting and Calligraphy

Usuzumi is one of the main tonal registers of **sumi-e** (ink painting). Painters build space with it:

- distant mountains
- mist and water
- the sense of air between forms

Deep black (_nōboku_, 濃墨)[^11] is kept for what comes forward. Because pale ink is mostly water, it bleeds and spreads on the paper. That makes each stroke hard to control and impossible to correct, which is why the tradition treats it as **an ink that carries emotion**.[^2]

---

## A Related Name: Usuzumi-zakura (淡墨桜)

The **Usuzumi-zakura** at Neodani in Motosu, Gifu, is a cherry tree said to be more than 1,500 years old.[^7][^8][^9] Its name uses a different first character, 淡 ("faint"). Its blossoms open pale pink, fade to white, and are said to turn a faint ink-grey as they fall.

---

## Notes for Shinsei

- **Grief and impermanence in the material itself.** The ink shows its meaning through how dilute it is. It's a quieter counterpart to gold and indigo (see [[Gold and Indigo in Sacred Art]]).
- **Layering.** Several washes of pale ink over one another give depth without hard edges.
- **Ink tone.** Blue-grey **seiboku** (青墨) ink sticks[^10] dilute to a cool usuzumi, which would sit naturally next to indigo.
