# Ink and Indigo

## What Sumi Is Made Of

Three things: soot, glue, and a fragrance to cover the glue.

- **Soot.** Either _shōen_ (松煙), from burning resinous pine, or _yūen_ (油煙), lamp soot collected from burning oil. Pine soot dominated the early centuries; lamp soot came later and gives the deeper black, and became the standard.
- **Glue.** _Nikawa_ (膠), extracted by boiling the hide of an animal — cow or deer.
- **Fragrance.** Musk, borneol, or plum-blossom essence, added to mask the smell of the glue.[^1]

So the black is carbon, and the body holding it is animal. This is worth knowing for a practice concerned with the subtle body: the mark is soot suspended in hide.

## How It Is Made

Ink-making reached Japan in 610, transmitted by the Korean monk Damjing. When the capital moved to Nara around 700 the craft followed, and has continued there for some 1,300 years; Nara now accounts for about 95% of Japanese sumi production.

The Nara turn came in the Muromachi period, when a monk at Kōfuku-ji tried making ink from the soot of votive oil lamps — temple lamps, temple demand, and forest resources in one place.

Production: soot kneaded with heated glue, worked by hand and foot, pressed into wooden moulds, then buried in wood ash in a temperature-controlled room so the moisture leaves slowly over ten to thirty days. It is made only in the cold months, because heat spoils the glue.[^1]

Aged sticks — _kogaboku_ (古墨, old ink) — are valued for how the glue changes with time; the older stick gives a softer, more diffuse mark.[^2]

## The Colour Inside the Black

Sumi is not one black. Inks fall into two families:

- **Blue-black** — _seiboku_ (青墨), also _aozumi_. Cool, receding.
- **Reddish or brown-black** — _chaboku_ (茶墨). Warm, advancing.

The crucial working fact: **the difference widens as the ink is diluted.** The lighter the wash, the more the underlying colour declares itself. Paper weight and surface then change how it blots and how that colour reads.[^2]

One honest caveat: sources disagree about how much of the blueness is native to the soot and how much comes from indigo or other pigment added to the ink. Test your own sticks on your own papers before committing to an account of it.[^2]

## The Tonal Doctrine

Classical ink painting holds that all five colours can be depicted with black ink alone. The Japanese term for the operating principle is _nōtan_ (濃淡) — written with the characters for dark and pale — and refers to the range of shades a master can produce within a single stroke.

The corollary is equally old: the untouched paper counts as much as the mark. Empty space is not background.[^3]

Both propositions matter to a monochrome series that intends to reach colour later. The tradition's claim is that it never left.

## Indigo — Ai

- **The plant.** _Ai_ (藍), an annual of the genus _Persicaria_, native to southern China and Indochina, in Japan by the late seventh century. It grows to about 80 cm and flowers white or reddish in autumn.
- **Sukumo.** Leaves are harvested, cut, and fermented into _sukumo_ (蒅), a composted mass, which is then combined with lye and lime to make the dye liquor.
- **The living vat.** The dye is cultivated, not mixed. Bacteria from the fermentation do the work, and the blue appears only on oxidation in air. Dyers feed the vat sake, _mizuame_ syrup and other nourishment; when conditions are right, cobalt bubbles called _ai no hana_ (藍の花, indigo flowers) rise to the surface and the vat is ready.
- **History.** Indigo reached Awa province in the Heian period. Demand rose sharply in the Sengoku era, when samurai wore it under armour for its beauty and for antimicrobial properties that reduced chafing rashes. The trade made Tokushima the tenth most populous city in Japan by 1889.
- **Shades.** Named across a full range, from the palest _kamenozoki_ (瓶覗, "a glance into the jar") to _kachiiro_ (勝色), a blue so deep it reads as black.[^4]

That last point is the one to hold: **the indigo scale ends where sumi begins.** _Kachiiro_ and _seiboku_ meet at the same place from opposite directions.

## Where Ink and Indigo Meet

Indigo-dyed paper written in gold and silver — the decorated sutras of the late Heian period, the _Chūsonji-kyō_ and _Jingoji-kyō_ among them. The full account, with academic references, is in [[Gold on Indigo]].[^5]

This is the historical form of blue and gold together in Japan, and it is a writing practice rather than a painting one: text laid in light onto a dyed night.

Tibet offers the ink-side version. Its "blue-black" sutra paper was usually coated with soot and hide glue rather than dyed with indigo, then burnished and written in gold — a sumi ground carrying gold script. See [[Gold and Indigo in Sacred Art]], §3.5.

## What Follows for the Work

1. **The blue in the current work has two possible sources** — the soot of a _seiboku_ stick, or indigo added to the ink. These behave differently in dilution and on different papers. Worth establishing which one is in use before the series fixes its palette.
2. **Dilution is the first colour operation.** No pigment is required to begin dividing light; a single stick diluted across a gradient already does it.
3. **Two sooted inks on one sheet** — a _seiboku_ beside a _chaboku_ — is the second operation, still monochrome by any ordinary description, and already chromatic.
4. **Indigo-dyed paper inverts the scheme.** Ink on white is light subtracted; gold on indigo is light added to darkness. The series currently works the first way. The second is available and carries eight centuries of precedent.
5. **The material is animal and vegetable.** Soot, hide glue, fermented leaf, live bacteria. Nothing in this palette is inert, which may matter to how the work is framed.
