# Gold and Indigo in Sacred Art

_紺と金: light written on darkness_

---

## Abstract

Gold written on a deep blue ground is one of the most widespread combinations in the sacred book arts. It appears in Japanese _konshi kindei_ (紺紙金泥) sutras, Goryeo sutra transcriptions, Tibetan _mthing shog_ manuscripts, the Umayyad and early ʿAbbāsid "gold on blue" tradition that produced the Blue Qur'an, and, in a closely related purple form, the imperial codices of Byzantium and Carolingian Europe. This companion to [[Gold in Spiritual Arts and Scriptures]] asks why the _pairing_ mattered, not just the gold. It argues that the combination rests on three foundations. The first is **mineral**: the image of lapis lazuli, a blue stone flecked with gold, which scripture repeatedly uses as the ground of heaven and the Buddha-lands. The second is **optical**: a dark ground makes gold look as if it gives off its own light. The third is **theological**: sacred speech is pictured as light appearing out of darkness. The study ends with notes for the Shinsei scrolls.

---

## 1. Introduction: A Pairing, Not Two Colours

The earlier essay argued that gold's beauty is relational: it glows only in relation to shadow.[^1] Indigo is the most deliberate form that shadow has taken in sacred art. Instead of relying on a dim temple or a candlelit mosque, makers dyed the darkness into the page itself. That way the gold would shine wherever the book was opened.

Three separate kinds of evidence meet in this pairing:

1. **Scriptural imagery.** Buddhist and biblical texts describe heaven and the Buddha-lands as a blue, lapis-like ground marked with gold.
2. **Optics.** Gold against a dark, cool ground gains in brilliance and warmth.
3. **Devotional economy.** Both materials were costly and labour-intensive, so combining them made the copy an offering.

## 2. The Stone Behind the Pairing: Lapis Lazuli

### 2.1 A blue stone with gold inside it

The Kyoto National Museum's explanation of _konshikyō_ (紺紙経, indigo-paper sutras) makes the mineral link explicit. The combination of dark blue and gold represents two of the Buddhist "seven treasures" (七宝): gold, and _ruri_ (琉璃), the deep blue stone identified with lapis lazuli. The museum points out that lapis naturally contains glittering specks of pyrite. The stone itself is, in effect, a _konshi kindei_ page, and the museum suggests it as the likely visual source of the style.[^2] The same text notes that the Buddha's speech is called _konku_ (金口, the "golden mouth"). It also notes that the _Lotus Sutra_ describes Pure Land ground made of lapis, so the colour scheme "may represent the pure world and the Buddha."[^3]

This clarifies a point left open in the earlier essay. There I treated the lapis-and-gold reading of indigo sutras as my own interpretation. The Kyoto National Museum presents a closely related reading, cautiously but directly.[^4]

### 2.2 Lapis grounds in Buddhist scripture

The textual basis is clear in two Mahāyāna sutras:

- **The _Lotus Sutra_, chapter 3.** The future land of Śāriputra has ground "made of lapis lazuli," with roads "marked off by ropes of gold."[^5]
- **The _Medicine Buddha Sutra_.** The Medicine Master of Lapis Lazuli Light (Yakushi Rurikō Nyorai, 薬師瑠璃光如来) vows that "my body will be translucent inside and out, like lapis lazuli, with brightness and flawless purity." His land's "ground is made of lapis lazuli; the boundaries are demarcated with golden cords."[^6]

A _konshi kindei_ page repeats this design closely. It is a blue ground with lines of gold running across it, and the lines are the Buddha's words, not roads.

### 2.3 Lapis as heaven elsewhere

The same mineral imagery appears outside Buddhism.

- **Egypt.** The _Book of the Heavenly Cow_ describes the aged sun god with "his flesh of gold, and his hair of genuine lapis lazuli."[^7]
- **Hebrew Bible.** At Sinai the elders see beneath God's feet "the like of a paved work of sapphire stone, and the like of the very heaven for clearness" (Exodus 24:10).[^8] Biblical scholars commonly take the Hebrew _sappir_ to be lapis lazuli rather than the modern sapphire, which would make this another blue, heavenly floor.
- **Medieval Europe.** Lapis was ground into ultramarine, which Cennino Cennini called "a color illustrious, beautiful, and most perfect, beyond all other colors."[^9] Michel Pastoureau traces a twelfth-century shift in which the Christian God came to be associated with light, and that light was pictured as blue. Suger's blue glass at Saint-Denis and the Virgin's blue mantle followed.[^10] The gold-and-ultramarine Madonna of late medieval painting is the Western counterpart of the Asian gold-and-indigo sutra.

## 3. East Asia: Writing on Indigo

### 3.1 Nara Japan: purple before indigo

The first great state project of gold-lettered sutras in Japan used **purple** paper, not indigo. In his provincial-temple edict of 741, Emperor Shōmu declared: 朕又別擬写金字金光明最勝王経、毎塔各令置一部 ("We further intend to copy the _Sutra of Golden Light_ in gold letters, and to place one set in each pagoda").[^11] The surviving _Kokubunji-kyō_ (国分寺経) at Nara National Museum, completed in 746, is written in gold on purple paper (紫紙金字). According to the Agency for Cultural Affairs, the imperial gold-character scriptorium produced 71 sets for the purpose.[^12]

The technique is also documented. Gold powder was dissolved in animal glue and water, and after drying the letters were burnished with a boar's tusk, "transforming its appearance almost instantly into that of gold leaf."[^13] A record of 751 plans for 35 boar tusks to burnish 1,400 sheets.[^14]

### 3.2 Heian Japan: indigo becomes standard

Indigo paper was used from the Nara period onward. By the late Heian period (eleventh to twelfth centuries), _konshikyō_ commissioned by aristocrats had become one of the defining forms of decorated sutra.[^15] Copying in gold on indigo was regarded as a great act of devotion and a way to earn merit for oneself, one's family or one's ancestors.[^16]

The most ambitious example is the **Chūson-ji Sutras** (中尊寺経). Fujiwara no Kiyohira commissioned them between 1117 and 1126 for the same temple as the Golden Hall discussed in the earlier essay. The entire Buddhist canon was written on indigo paper with **lines alternating between gold and silver**. The Kyoto National Museum calls a full canon in alternating gold and silver almost unprecedented anywhere in Japan, China or Korea. The canon ran to almost 5,400 scrolls; about 4,500 survive, more than 4,200 of them now on Mount Kōya.[^17] The Jingo-ji canon, written in gold on indigo under imperial patronage in the twelfth century, is a parallel monument.[^18] Both sets, the _Heike Nōkyō_ and the jeweled pagoda mandalas are covered in detail in [[Gold on Indigo]].

### 3.3 Goryeo Korea

Korea produced some of the finest gold- and silver-on-indigo sutras in East Asia. Goryeo _sagyeong_ (寫經) were usually written on mulberry paper dyed deep indigo, with gold-painted frontispieces of the Buddha in paradise. King Chungnyeol (r. 1274–1308) set up two court scriptoria, the _Geumjawon_ (金字院, Gold Letters) and _Eunjawon_ (銀字院, Silver Letters). They were so admired that Korean sutra-writers were sent to the Yuan court.[^19]

### 3.4 China

Blue-and-gold sutras go back to Tang China (618–907). Meher McArthur identifies a _Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra_ of the ninth or tenth century as the earliest known Chinese example.[^20]

### 3.5 Tibet: the "blue" made of soot

Tibet adds a useful complication. Its luxury manuscripts are written in gold and silver on _mthing shog_ (blue-black paper), and Michela Clemente documents such volumes from the twelfth century onward, often framed with double lines, one gold and one silver.[^21] Conservation study at the Library of Congress shows how the dark ground was made. Sheets were laminated, coated with a pigment of **soot mixed with yak-hide glue**, and burnished with shells or stone beads.[^22] The "blue" of Tibetan blue-black paper, then, was usually **ink-black** rather than indigo. The aesthetic target was a dark, lustrous field for gold, and makers reached it with whatever dark material they had. The Qing _Dragon Kanjur_ of 1669, commissioned by Grand Empress Dowager Xiaozhuang, carried this Tibetan format into the imperial court: 108 volumes in gold ink on "Tibetan Blue Paper."[^23]

## 4. The Mediterranean and Islamic Worlds

### 4.1 Purple codices and Jerome's objection

Late antique and Byzantine luxury Bibles were written in gold and silver on purple-dyed parchment. Examples include the sixth-century Rossano Gospels and the Gothic Codex Argenteus, and Carolingian and Ottonian courts later revived the practice (for instance, the Godescalc Evangelistary, 781–783).[^24] Purple carried imperial associations.

As with gold in general, the pairing drew criticism. Around 384, Jerome wrote: "Parchments are dyed purple, gold is melted into lettering, manuscripts are decked with jewels, while Christ lies at the door naked and dying." In his prologue to Job he set "gold and silver letters on purple skins" against "our poor pages and copies which are less remarkable for beauty than for accuracy."[^25] Jerome's complaint anticipates Bernard of Clairvaux by eight centuries. It also adds a scholar's objection: splendour can distract from textual accuracy.

### 4.2 The Umayyad gold-on-blue tradition

The Islamic tradition is the most carefully studied case of gold on blue. Alain George shows that the mosaic inscription of the Dome of the Rock (692) is in "gold against a dark green ground which verges on dark blue." Arabic sources describe the Umayyad mosque inscriptions at Damascus and Medina as "written in gold on dark blue."[^26] Gold letters on blue were thus an established monumental language for sacred text before the Blue Qur'an was made.

### 4.3 The Blue Qur'an: contrast and light

The Blue Qur'an is written in gold Kufic script on indigo-dyed parchment, with silver ornament.[^27] Its date and place are still debated. The Metropolitan Museum attributes it to Tunisia, perhaps Qairawan, in the later ninth to mid-tenth century, and connects its palette to Byzantine purple manuscripts.[^28] George instead argues that it is "in all likelihood, an early ʿAbbāsid manuscript made for a patron of the highest rank."[^29]

George's aesthetic argument matters more here than the dating. He distinguishes the Blue Qur'an from purple Bibles. Where their reddish tones are harmonious, the Qur'an's "relatively stable dark blue hue" is "fundamentally defined by the strong contrast" with the gold. Its makers could have imitated a purple effect with vegetable dyes, "but they did not."[^30] He ties this contrast to the Qur'an's own opposition between _nūr_ (light) and _ẓulumāt_ (darknesses): "the Qur'an represents a descent of divine light on earth." He also argues that the visual effect was designed for "the kind of atmosphere created by candlelight."[^31]

The Blue Qur'an thus puts into words what the East Asian sources show: **the page represents scripture as light appearing in darkness.** The Johannine line "the light shineth in darkness" (John 1:5) expresses the same idea in a Christian register.[^32]

## 5. A Note on India: The Dark God in Gold

South Asian devotional art applies the pairing to the body rather than the page. The name _Kṛṣṇa_ means "dark," and Krishna is conventionally shown dark blue or black, dressed in the _pītāmbara_, the "bright golden-yellow garment" that also serves as an epithet of Vishnu.[^33] The pairing is the same: a dark ground carrying golden light, with the deity's body in place of the sutra page.

---

## 6. Aesthetic Analysis

### 6.1 Value: the page as night

The most basic effect is contrast of lightness. On white paper, gold ink looks like a pale yellow-brown and nearly disappears. On indigo, the same gold becomes the brightest thing in view and **seems to shine by itself**. This is the effect Procopius, Tanizaki and George each describe in their own settings.[^34] Indigo carries the darkness of the temple interior into the book.

### 6.2 Hue: complementary heightening

Blue and yellow-orange sit opposite each other on the colour circle. Michel-Eugène Chevreul's law of simultaneous contrast describes how adjacent complementary colours intensify each other.[^35] The cool indigo pushes the gold toward greater warmth, and the gold makes the indigo look deeper. George's point that the Blue Qur'an is "defined by the strong contrast" is an observation about this effect.[^36]

### 6.3 Depth and surface

Indigo _absorbs_ light and reads as depth: night, sky, sea, the lapis floor of heaven. Burnished gold _reflects_ light and reads as surface and presence. The pairing sets an infinite background against a present foreground. In Buddhist terms it resembles the Pure Land ground with the Buddha's word upon it. In Islamic terms it is _nūr_ against _ẓulumāt_.

### 6.4 Labour as offering

Both materials took time and skill. Indigo required repeated dyeing, and gold ink had to be ground, mixed with glue, applied and then burnished letter by letter with a boar's tusk. The Nara record of 35 tusks for 1,400 sheets is a reminder that **the shine was a result of labour**.[^37] In the devotional logic of _shakyō_ (写経, sutra copying) and of Tibetan merit-making, that labour was the point.[^38]

### 6.5 Silver as a third term

The Chūson-ji canon, the Goryeo scriptoria and the Tibetan double frames all set **silver alongside gold**.[^39] The sources describe what was done but mostly do not say why. One tempting reading, which is my own and not from the sources, is that alternating gold and silver lines on a night-blue ground suggest sun and moon crossing the sky, like an alternating breath of warm and cool light.

### 6.6 Purple and indigo: harmony versus contrast

George's comparison suggests two distinct aesthetics. **Purple grounds** (Byzantine codices, Nara _shishi kinji_) are regal and harmonious, drawing on imperial associations. **Indigo grounds** (Heian _konshikyō_, the Blue Qur'an, Goryeo _sagyeong_) are contrastive and nocturnal, closer to lapis and the night sky.[^40] Japan used both, with purple prominent in the Nara state project and indigo defining the late Heian decorated sutra. That sequence is at least suggestive of a move from imperial display toward a more contemplative image of the Pure Land.

## 7. Notes Toward Shinsei

These are offered as possibilities for the scroll work, not conclusions.

- **Sumi as the dark ground.** The Tibetan case shows the "blue" field can be made of soot (on sumi and indigo as materials, see [[Ink and Indigo]]). For a sumi-e painter, this suggests gold on a sumi-washed ground rather than on dyed paper, with the ink's darkness becoming the "night" for the gold.
- **Night sakura.** Sakura, the core visual focus of Shinsei, painted in _kindei_ or silver on indigo or sumi would bring together the Pure Land image (a lapis ground with golden lines) and the Japanese love of _yozakura_ (夜桜, cherry blossoms at night).
- **Burnishing as practice.** Polishing each stroke with a tusk or agate burnisher turns the finishing of gold into its own slow, attentive act. That fits Shinsei's view of the painting as a residue of practice.
- **Alternation as breath.** The Chūson-ji rhythm of gold line and silver line could be carried into non-textual marks: warm and cool strokes alternating like inhalation and exhalation.
- **One lamp.** George's point about candlelight suggests showing indigo-and-gold scrolls in low, single-source light, as they were made to be seen.
- **Direct experience.** Some temples have offered _konshi kindei shakyō_ sessions for visitors, for example Hase-dera in Kamakura.[^41] Doing it yourself may teach more about the medium than any text.
