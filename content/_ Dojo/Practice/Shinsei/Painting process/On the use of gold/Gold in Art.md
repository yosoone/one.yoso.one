# Gold in Art

## Why Gold Is Not a Colour

Gold is reflectance, not pigment. A pigment absorbs most wavelengths and returns one; gold returns whatever it is given, directionally, so the work changes as the light changes and as the viewer moves. This is the reason gold can sit beside _sumi_ (墨, ink) without breaking a monochrome scheme — it adds no hue, it adds behaviour.

The tradition names this function directly. In Japanese Buddhist painting gold is the sacred colour of the deities and stands for the rays of light that illuminate the world.[^1]

## Japanese Techniques

### Kinpaku — gold leaf

_Kinpaku_ (金箔) is gold beaten to roughly one ten-thousandth of a millimetre. Kanazawa in Ishikawa Prefecture has made it since 1593 and produces almost all of Japan's supply, with around sixty craftspeople still working.

Pure gold is alloyed with small quantities of silver and copper at 1300°C — a standard mix of 94.438% gold, 4.901% silver, 0.661% copper — then beaten in two stages: _zumiuchi_ down to one-thousandth of a millimetre, then _hakuuchi_ to final thinness under a bar striking 700 times a minute. The _hakuuchi_ paper that wraps the gold is itself treated with persimmon tannin and egg, and it determines the final quality.[^2]

### Kirikane — cut gold

_Kirikane_ (截金, also written 切金) is leaf cut into lines, lozenges and triangles and laid as drawing rather than as ground. Two or more leaves are heated over ash and bonded to build thickness, cut with a bamboo blade on a deerskin pad, and fixed with seaweed or hide glue.

It came from Tang China; the oldest surviving Japanese example is the Tamamushi Shrine at Hōryū-ji. Its golden age was the eleventh century, continuing into the thirteenth and fourteenth before declining with Buddhist patronage. It survives as an Important Intangible Cultural Property, with Eri Sayoko recognised as a Living National Treasure in the technique.[^3]

### Kindei and leaf from behind

Gold also works as paint — powdered gold, _kindei_ (金泥) — and as leaf applied to the reverse of the silk. The Kōfuku-ji mandala uses both at once: leaf pressed from behind so the surface holds an iridescence that comes from within, and gold paint on the front for the fine outlines.[^1]

Worth noting as a structural idea: the glow arrives from behind the image, not on top of it.

### Screens and the Rinpa line

Gold-ground screens of the Azuchi-Momoyama period and the Rinpa school extend the same logic to the whole field — the ground itself becomes light, and the image floats on it without a described space.[^4]

## Gold With Indigo

Gold and silver script on indigo-dyed paper is a developed tradition in its own right — the decorated sutras of the late Heian period, and the jeweled pagoda mandalas where the text composes the image. The Japanese sets and their materials are in [[Gold on Indigo]]; the same pairing across Asia, the Islamic world and Byzantium is in [[Gold and Indigo in Sacred Art]].

The short version: deep blue as the ground of the spiritual realm, gold as light written onto it — nearly a description of the current direction of the work.

## Western Gilding

For contrast, and because the craft is remarkably close. The meaning of the Western gold ground — transcendent light, placeless heaven — is covered in [[Gold in Spiritual Arts and Scriptures]] (§2.3 and §8.3); this section keeps to method.

**Technique.** A reddish clay ground called bole was laid first — it gave the gold depth and prevented the greenish cast that leaf shows over white. Water gilding laid the leaves onto that bole and burnished them with agate or a dog's or wolf's tooth to a mirror finish. Mordant gilding left the gold unburnished and duller; shell gold, powdered and bound, served for small details; punches and line tools worked patterns into haloes.

**History in brief.** Gold backgrounds emerged from Roman mosaic around the first century and were taken up by early Christian and Byzantine art. Italian panel painting carried the gold ground from the thirteenth century onward, holding longest in conservative centres such as Siena and Venice. Naturalistic space killed it — Early Netherlandish painting dropped gold grounds within a few years of the mid-1420s, and Lorenzo Monaco (d. 1424) is called the final gasp of gold-ground brilliance in Florence. It continued unbroken in Orthodox icon painting, and returned in Klimt's golden phase, 1898–1911.[^4]

## What Follows for the Work

1. **Gold does not break monochrome.** It contributes reflectance, not hue. A black-and-gold work is still tonally one system.
2. **Consider gold from behind.** The Kōfuku-ji method — leaf under the surface, paint only for the line — puts the light inside the work rather than on it. On paper rather than silk this needs testing, but the principle is worth attempting.
3. **The blue-and-gold pairing is inherited, not invented.** The current softer blue-black sumi with gold sits directly in the Jingo-ji lineage. Knowing that is useful for a wall text and for a student; it also means the pairing carries meaning already and does not need explaining twice.
4. **Cut gold is drawing, not gilding.** _Kirikane_ is the technique to study if the gold is to be a line rather than a field — which is what a mark-based practice would want.
