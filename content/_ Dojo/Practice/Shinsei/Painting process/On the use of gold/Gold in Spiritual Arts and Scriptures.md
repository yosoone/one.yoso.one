# Gold in Spiritual Arts and Scriptures

_An aesthetic survey across traditions_

---

## Abstract

Gold shows up in the sacred art and scripture of almost every literate religious tradition. Its meanings differ in detail, but they come back again and again to a small set of aesthetic qualities, each rooted in what the metal physically does. It **gathers and returns light** rather than making its own. It **does not tarnish or decay**. It **is purified by fire**. It can be **beaten into a surface so thin that it seems to have no depth**. And it **is rare and costly**, which makes it an offering. This essay follows those five qualities through texts and objects: the Pure Land sutras and the Vedic Brāhmaṇas, the Book of Revelation and the Qur'an, Byzantine mosaics, gold-on-indigo sutras and the Golden Hall of Chūson-ji. It then looks at the traditions' own doubts about gold, from the golden calf to Bernard of Clairvaux to Tanizaki's praise of shadow. It closes with some notes on what this history might offer the Shinsei scroll work.

---

## 1. Introduction: A Metal That Behaves Like a Metaphor

Most sacred materials carry meaning by convention. Gold is unusual because its physical behaviour already works like a metaphor. It does not rust or corrode. Fire refines it without destroying it. It can be hammered into leaf and drawn into thread. Its warm reflectance changes with every shift in the light around it. Scriptures and artists did not have to add much to this. Mostly they pointed at what the metal already does.

Annabel Teh Gallop, co-curator of the British Library's 2022 exhibition _Gold_, put the cross-cultural pattern plainly: "With its reflective properties, gold was ideal for representing sacred light, and figures might be set against golden backgrounds to suggest an expanse of heavenly light."[^1] The exhibition placed Burmese Buddhist gold plates (fifth to sixth century) next to Byzantine gospel canon tables (sixth to seventh century), the Golden Haggadah (c. 1320) and gilded Qur'ans. Its point was that very different faiths arrived at the same material answer.[^2]

The sections below take the five qualities one at a time and then turn to the counter-tradition of restraint.

---

## 2. Luminosity: Light That Seems to Come From Within

### 2.1 Byzantium: the dissolving interior

The earliest sustained aesthetic writing about gold in sacred space is Byzantine. Describing Justinian's Hagia Sophia in the 550s, Procopius wrote that "its interior is not illuminated from without by the sun, but that the radiance comes into being within it, such an abundance of light bathes this shrine." He also noted that "the whole ceiling is overlaid with pure gold, which adds glory to the beauty, yet the light reflected from the stones prevails, shining out in rivalry with the gold."[^3]

Two ideas in Procopius run through the rest of this history. The first is **light that seems to come from inside the building**. Gold makes a room look self-luminous, as if the building gave off light rather than received it. The second is **rivalry**. Gold is never the only source of splendour. Its beauty comes from its interplay with marble, glass, candle and shadow.

Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite, the most influential theologian of sacred imagery, spelled out what the metals mean. In _The Celestial Hierarchy_ he glosses electrum, an alloy of gold and silver, as combining "the incorruptible, priceless, unfailing, and unpolluted radiance of gold" with "the gleam, the gloss, the splendor, and the heavenly glow of silver."[^4] In one sentence, gold's radiance is tied to incorruptibility, which is the second quality discussed below.

### 2.2 The performative surface

Recent scholarship has moved attention from what gold _symbolises_ to what it _does_ in front of a viewer. In _The Sensual Icon_, Bissera Pentcheva argues that Byzantine metal-relief icons were performative objects, not static pictures. "The sun's rays or flickering candlelight, stirred by drafts of air and human breath, animated the rich surfaces." The aim was _poikilia_, a shimmering variety of appearance, and the ideal was the _empsychos_ or "in-spirited" image, not a lifelike one.[^5] Liz James's _Light and Colour in Byzantine Art_ likewise shows that the Byzantines judged colour by brightness and lustre more than by hue, which helps explain why gold held first place.[^6]

This is the key aesthetic point. **A gold surface is never finished.** It changes as the viewer moves, as a lamp flickers, as the day passes. A gold icon or screen is less an image than an event.

### 2.3 The gold ground as non-space

The same logic lies behind the _gold ground_: figures set against an unbroken field of gold rather than a landscape. Otto Pächt observed that "medieval gold ground was always interpreted as a symbol of transcendental light," and that the gold of Byzantine mosaics suggested "eternal cosmic space."[^7] The gold ground does not show a place. It cancels place, lifting the holy figure out of the measurable world.

Japan developed a parallel form in the _kinji_ (金地, gold-ground) folding screens of the Momoyama period (1568–1600). Gold leaf there served both as a flat, placeless field and as a practical reflector in dim castle and temple rooms. Rinpa painters later made the gold field their signature.[^8] (Techniques: [[Gold in Art]].)

### 2.4 Tanizaki: gold needs darkness

The most penetrating modern account of gold's luminosity is Japanese. In 『陰翳礼讃』(_In'ei raisan_, In Praise of Shadows, 1933), Tanizaki Jun'ichirō argues that gold only makes sense in the dark:

> How, in such a dark place, gold draws so much light to itself is a mystery to me. But I see why in ancient times statues of the Buddha were gilt with gold and why gold leaf covered the walls of the homes of the nobility.[^9]

Tanizaki insists that "their use of gold leaf and gold dust was not mere extravagance. Its reflective properties were put to use as a source of illumination. Silver and other metals quickly lose their gloss, but gold retains its brilliance indefinitely to light the darkness of the room."[^10] He also admires how, in brocade and lacquer, "the bold patterns remain for the most part hidden in darkness; only occasionally does a bit of gold or silver gleam forth."[^11]

Tanizaki and Procopius describe the same thing from opposite sides. Procopius sees gold turning a room into light. Tanizaki sees gold as the one thing that stays visible when the room goes dark. Either way, **gold's beauty depends on its relation to shadow**.

## 3. Incorruptibility: The Metal of Immortality

### 3.1 Vedic India

The Brāhmaṇa literature states the equation directly. In the fire-altar ritual of the _Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa_, the sacrificer hangs a round gold plate (_rukma_) around his neck, its knobs worn outward as the sun's rays. The text explains: "gold is light, and he (the sun) is the light; gold is immortality, and he is immortality."[^12] Gold, light and _amṛta_ (deathlessness) are treated as one substance in three forms. The Ṛgveda's cosmogonic hymn 10.121 names the first principle _Hiraṇyagarbha_, the "Golden Embryo," which arose at the beginning of creation (_hiraṇyagarbhaḥ samavartatāgre_).[^13]

### 3.2 Daoist China

Ge Hong's alchemical treatise _Baopuzi neipian_ (抱朴子内篇, early fourth century) builds a theory of immortality on the same property. In the chapter "Gold and Cinnabar" (金丹) he writes: 黄金入火，百炼不消，埋之，毕天不朽。服此二物，炼人身体，故能令人不老不死. That is: "Gold put into fire is not consumed though refined a hundred times; buried, it does not decay until the end of heaven. Ingesting these two substances refines the body, and so can make one neither age nor die."[^14] The metal's durability is thought to pass into the body that takes it in. Mircea Eliade's comparative study of alchemy reads such traditions as treating gold as the _perfected_ or fully matured metal, the end point toward which all matter is slowly moving.[^15]

### 3.3 Egypt

Egyptian religious texts describe the gods' bodies in precious materials. The _Book of the Heavenly Cow_, found in New Kingdom royal tombs including those of Seti I and Ramesses II, describes the aged sun god Re with "his bones being of silver, his flesh of gold, and his hair of genuine lapis lazuli."[^16] Gold here is not a picture of divinity. It is what divine flesh is made of. This helps explain the gold funerary masks and gilded shrines of Egyptian royal burial, including the gilded shrine of Tutankhamun, which carries part of the same text.[^17]

### 3.4 Aesthetic consequence

Incorruptibility gives gold an aesthetic of **permanence against time**. A gilded image claims to stand outside decay. That claim fits naturally with funerary settings, such as the Egyptian tomb or the Konjikidō at Chūson-ji (§5.3), where gold surrounds the dead.

## 4. Refinement: Purity Through Fire

### 4.1 Pali Buddhism: the pliant, radiant mind

The _Upakkilesa Sutta_ (AN 5.23) gives one of Buddhism's most exact metallurgical analogies. Gold mixed with iron, copper, tin, lead or silver "is neither pliant nor malleable nor luminous, but brittle & not rightly fit for work." Once freed of these impurities, it "is pliant, malleable, & luminous." In the same way, the mind freed of the five hindrances (sensual desire, ill will, sloth and drowsiness, restlessness and anxiety, and uncertainty) becomes "pliant, malleable, & luminous."[^18]

For an artist, this passage is significant. **The same three words describe both good material and a cultivated mind: pliant, workable, radiant.** Gold is used here as a model of the meditative process itself, not as a symbol of a finished state.

### 4.2 The biblical refiner's fire

The Hebrew Bible and New Testament use the same image of purification by ordeal. "When he hath tried me, I shall come forth as gold" (Job 23:10). The Lord "shall purify the sons of Levi, and purge them as gold and silver" (Malachi 3:3). Faith is "much more precious than of gold that perisheth, though it be tried with fire" (1 Peter 1:7).[^19] In each case gold's brilliance is _earned_. It appears only after something has been burned away.

### 4.3 Aesthetic consequence

Refinement links gold's appearance to a **history of purification**. Glitter alone does not make gold beautiful. Its beauty is the visible result of subtraction. This fits closely with contemplative art, where the finished object is a residue of practice.

## 5. The Golden Body

### 5.1 The Buddha's skin

Among the thirty-two marks (_lakkhaṇa_) of a Great Man in the _Lakkhaṇa Sutta_ (DN 30) is golden skin: _suvaṇṇavaṇṇo hoti kañcanasannibhattaco_, "golden-coloured, with skin like burnished gold."[^20] The _Mahāparinibbāna Sutta_ (DN 16) turns this mark into a scene. When a lay follower offers the dying Buddha a pair of cloth-of-gold robes, Ānanda sees that against the Tathāgata's body the gold cloth looks dull.[^21] The point is that **the enlightened body outshines gold itself**. Gold is the highest earthly standard, and awakening goes beyond it. This is the textual basis for gilded Buddha images across Asia, and for the Japanese term _konjiki_ (金色, golden colour) used for the Buddha's body.

### 5.2 Gold as flesh in Egypt and India

The Egyptian "flesh of gold" (§3.3) and the Vedic Golden Embryo (§3.1) show that the golden body is not only a Buddhist idea. In all three traditions, **gold is the colour of a body that has passed beyond ordinary mortality**.

### 5.3 The Konjikidō at Chūson-ji

Japan's most complete realisation of the golden body in architecture is the _Konjikidō_ (金色堂, Golden Hall) at Chūson-ji in Hiraizumi, raised in 1124 under Fujiwara no Kiyohira. The hall gives form to the Pure Land ideal. Its central altar holds Kiyohira's remains, surrounded by eleven Buddhist images covered in gold.[^22] The dead patron is placed inside a small golden paradise, a pairing of incorruptible metal with the hope of rebirth. The _Book of the Heavenly Cow_ would have recognised the idea.

## 6. The Golden Ground of Paradise

### 6.1 Pure Land and Lotus Sutra

Mahāyāna scriptures describe awakened realms in precious materials. The _Amitābha Sutra_ (translated into Chinese by Kumārajīva in 402) describes ponds of the seven jewels whose "beds ... are covered entirely with golden sand," and states simply: "The ground is made of gold."[^23] In the _Lotus Sutra_, the future land of Śāriputra has ground "made of lapis lazuli" with roads "marked off by ropes of gold."[^24]

The second image is worth noting because of how closely it matches the look of _konshi kindei_ (紺紙金泥) sutras, which are written in gold paint on deep blue indigo paper (§7.2). The sutras themselves do not make the connection. The Kyoto National Museum, however, reads the indigo-and-gold scheme as representing lapis lazuli and gold, two of the seven treasures, and notes the _Lotus Sutra_'s lapis Pure Land ground. It frames this cautiously ("may represent"), so the link is a **supported interpretation** rather than a settled fact. See [[Gold and Indigo in Sacred Art]] for the full discussion.

### 6.2 The New Jerusalem

The Book of Revelation gives Christianity's most striking golden paradise: "the city was pure gold, like unto clear glass," and "the street of the city was pure gold, as it were transparent glass" (Revelation 21:18, 21).[^25] Aesthetically, the image is paradoxical: **transparent gold**, a material that cannot exist on earth. It describes gold's radiance with its opacity and weight removed, matter turned into light. That is exactly the effect Procopius and Pentcheva describe in Byzantine interiors.

### 6.3 The Qur'an and deferral

The Qur'an also places gold in paradise. The righteous are "adorned with bracelets of gold" (18:31) and served from vessels of gold (43:71).[^26] A well-known hadith draws the ethical conclusion: "Do not wear silk or Dibaja, and do not drink in silver or golden vessels ... for such things are for the unbelievers in this worldly life and for us in the Hereafter."[^27] Gold becomes an aesthetic of **deferral**. Its full enjoyment is postponed to the next world, and this sharpens its meaning in this one. The restriction applies to personal use and luxury, not to the adornment of scripture, which Islamic book arts carried to great heights (§7.1).

## 7. Writing the Word in Gold

### 7.1 Chrysography in the Islamic and Byzantine worlds

Writing sacred text in gold (chrysography) is where scripture and gold meet most directly. The so-called Blue Qur'an, probably made in Tunisia between the later ninth and mid-tenth century, is written in gold and silver Kufic script on indigo-dyed parchment. The Metropolitan Museum notes that "its palette is thought to refer to the purple‑dyed, gilded manuscripts made in the neighboring Byzantine empire."[^28] Here gold does three things. It marks the text as precious. It makes the page shine. And it places the manuscript in a wider Mediterranean language of sacred luxury.

### 7.2 Gold on indigo in East Asia

The same pairing of gold on dark blue appears independently in East Asia. Blue-and-gold sutras were produced in Tang China (618–907). In Japan, _konshi kindei_ transcriptions became, in Meher McArthur's words, "great acts of devotion and a means to earn merit," often commissioned by nobles or warriors for themselves, their families or their ancestors.[^29] Copying scripture in gold made the act of devotion materially costly and visually lasting. The pairing has its own notes: [[Gold on Indigo]] for the Japanese sets and [[Gold and Indigo in Sacred Art]] for the wider tradition.

### 7.3 The Heike Nōkyō and _kirikane_

The high point of this tradition is the _Heike Nōkyō_ (平家納経), the set of sutras Taira no Kiyomori dedicated at Itsukushima Shrine in 1164. The texts use "gold, silver, verdigris and indigo," and the frontispieces, endpapers and both sides of the scrolls "are abundantly decorated ... in gold and silver."[^30] It is an unusually complete example of Buddhist scripture dedicated at a Shinto shrine, and of gold serving both piety and the display of power. (Details of the set: [[Gold on Indigo]].)

Japanese Buddhist art also refined _kirikane_ (截金, cut gold): leaf cut into fine lines and laid onto painted or sculpted surfaces, a technique that came from Tang China and flourished in the eleventh century.[^31] (Method: [[Gold in Art]].) Aesthetically, _kirikane_ turns gold from a _field_ into a _line_: a thread of light that follows the folds of a deity's robe without flattening the figure into a gold ground.

## 8. The Counter-Tradition: Doubt, Restraint and Repair

A survey that only praised gold would miss something the traditions themselves knew. Gold is dangerous because it is beautiful.

### 8.1 The golden calf

The same book of Exodus that orders the Ark and lampstand to be made of "pure gold" (Exodus 25:11, 31) also tells of the golden calf, worshipped with the cry "These be thy gods, O Israel" (Exodus 32:4).[^32] The metal is the same in both. What differs is the orientation. Gold serving presence is holy. Gold taken as presence is an idol.

### 8.2 Suger and Bernard

Twelfth-century France staged the classic debate. Abbot Suger of Saint-Denis defended gold as a way of lifting the mind. His inscription on the gilded doors tells viewers to "marvel not at the gold and the expense but at the craftsmanship," and declares that "the dull mind rises to truth through that which is material." Elsewhere he describes being "transported from this inferior to that higher world in an anagogical manner" by contemplating the gems and gold of the altar.[^33]

Bernard of Clairvaux answered in his _Apologia_: "Oh vanity of vanities, yet no more vain than insane! The church is resplendent in her walls and wanting in her poor. She dresses her stones in gold and lets her sons go naked."[^34] Bernard does not deny gold's beauty. He denies that its beauty is innocent. For him, gold's pull on the eye competes with inner attention and with care for the poor.

### 8.3 Alberti and the painter's skill

In the Renaissance the objection became artistic rather than moral. Leon Battista Alberti wrote in _On Painting_ (1435–36): "There are some who use much gold in their _istoria_. They think it gives majesty. I do not praise it." A painter, he argued, should imitate the gleam of gold with colour, since real gold makes planes shine where they should be dark and look dark where they should shine.[^35] This objection explains part of the decline of the gold ground in Western painting. It also shows, by contrast, what the gold ground had been for: **refusing illusion**.

### 8.4 Tanizaki and kintsugi: gold in its place

Japanese aesthetics offers a third response, neither acceptance nor refusal but **placement**. Tanizaki's gold is beautiful because it stays mostly hidden (§2.4). The later practice of _kintsugi_ (金継ぎ), mending broken ceramics with lacquer and gold, carries this further. Gold marks the history of damage instead of covering it, as the essays in Christy Bartlett's _Flickwerk: The Aesthetics of Mended Japanese Ceramics_ explore.[^36] Here gold's traditional claim to incorruptibility is turned around. It honours what broke.

_Diagram: the same readings as a cycle — matter is perceived, perception is read, reading becomes practice, and practice works the matter again. The shadow side short-circuits the cycle back onto the metal._

| Quality | Physical basis | Representative sources | Aesthetic effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Luminosity** | High, warm reflectance | Procopius; Pseudo-Dionysius; Pentcheva; Tanizaki | Light that seems to come from within; a surface that changes with every viewing |
| **Incorruptibility** | Does not tarnish or corrode | _Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa_; _Baopuzi_; _Heavenly Cow_ | Permanence against time; funerary and eschatological settings |
| **Refinement** | Purified by fire | AN 5.23; Job; Malachi; 1 Peter | Radiance earned by subtraction; a model of cultivation |
| **Placelessness** | Can be beaten into a depthless leaf | Gold grounds; _kinji_ screens; Revelation's "transparent gold" | Cancels measurable space; the ground of paradise |
| **Preciousness** | Rarity, cost | Suger; chrysography; _Heike Nōkyō_ | Offering, merit, reverence for the Word |
| _Tension_ | Attraction to the eye | Exodus 32; Bernard; Alberti; hadith | The idol, vanity, distraction; hence restraint, deferral, placement |

Across these traditions, gold's aesthetic is **relational**. It needs darkness in order to glow (Tanizaki), rival materials in order to shine (Procopius), fire in order to be purified (AN 5.23), and a right orientation in order to be holy rather than idolatrous (Exodus, Bernard).

## 10. Notes Toward Shinsei

![[_ Dojo/Practice/Shinsei/Painting process/On the use of gold/The Cycle of Practice.svg]]

_Diagram: the same cycle turned inward — mind in practice at the centre, five movements around it, each mirrored by something gold does._

These are offered as open questions, not conclusions.

- **Sumi and gold as complements.** Ink soaks into paper. Gold reflects light off it. On a scroll, _sumi_ is the trace of the hand going _in_, and gold is light coming _back out_. Tanizaki's claim that gold needs darkness suggests the two are partners, not opposites.
- **Pliant, workable, radiant.** The _Upakkilesa Sutta_ uses the same three words for purified gold and for the purified mind. That fits Shinsei's principle that the painting is a residue of inward practice. Gold used sparingly could stand for that result rather than for decoration.
- **Line versus field.** A full gold ground (_kinji_) lifts the image out of place. A thread of _kirikane_ or _kindei_ (金泥, gold paint) keeps the image in the world while picking out a single line of light. Each choice carries a different theology.
- **Suger's direction and Shinsei's.** Suger valued gold for what it _does to the viewer_, lifting the "dull mind" upward. That outward-facing aim is close to the directionality Shinsei distinguishes itself from. A Shinsei use of gold might sit closer to Tanizaki: light kept mostly in shadow, noticed rather than displayed.
- **Changing light.** Pentcheva's performative surface suggests hanging gold-bearing scrolls where daylight or candlelight moves, so the work changes over the day, like a practice.
