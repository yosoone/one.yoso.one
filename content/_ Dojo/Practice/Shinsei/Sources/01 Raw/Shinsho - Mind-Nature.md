# Shinshō (心性): Mind-Nature from the Luminous Mind to the Painted Trace

> **Abstract.** This essay follows the term 心性 (Japanese _shinshō_, Chinese _xinxing_) from early Indian teachings on the "luminous mind," through Chinese Buddhist and Confucian thought, into Japanese debates over original enlightenment. Throughout that history, one question keeps coming back: ==is mind-nature a stable essence hidden beneath the defilements, or the open, dependently arising activity that practice reveals==? The essay ends by asking what this question means for a painting practice that takes 心性 as its name.[^1]

---

## 1. The Word and Its Two Readings

The compound is made of 心 (_shin_: mind, heart; Sanskrit _citta_) and 性 (_shō_ or _sei_: nature, inborn character). Japanese dictionaries give it two readings, and each carries a different world.[^2]

- **Shinshō (しんしょう)** uses the older _go-on_ reading of 性 that Buddhist vocabulary keeps. The _Nihon Kokugo Daijiten_ defines this Buddhist sense as "the unchanging substance of the mind" (不変な心の本体).[^2]
- **Shinsei (しんせい)** is the everyday modern reading. The _Daijisen_ defines it as a mental disposition or "mentality" (メンタリティー), as in "the Japanese   ```*shinsei*."[^2]

The _Digital Dictionary of Buddhism_ glosses the Buddhist term as "mind-nature." It gives the Sanskrit equivalent _citta-dharmatā_ and links the term to the _tathāgatagarbha_ mind (如來藏心) and to "the mind that is pure in its own nature" (自性清淨心).[^3]

So the dictionary definition already leans one way: ==mind-nature as something \*unchanging\*==. The rest of this essay argues that the tradition never fully settled that point. The most useful way to understand 心性 is as a long argument over whether the mind's ground is a thing or a way of being.

## 2. Indian Roots: The Luminous Mind

The oldest source for the idea is a short passage in the Pali _Aṅguttara Nikāya_:

> "Luminous, monks, is the mind. And it is defiled by incoming defilements."[^4]

The passage goes on to say that the uninstructed person does not see this, and so, for them, "there is no development of the mind." The well-instructed disciple does see it, and so can develop the mind.[^4] Two things matter here:

1. **Defilement comes from outside.** It is "incoming" (_āgantuka_), not part of the mind's nature.
2. **The claim serves practice.** Luminosity is taught so that cultivation makes sense, not as metaphysics for its own sake.

The Mahāsāṃghika school turned this into doctrine. Vasumitra's treatise on the early schools, in Xuanzang's Chinese translation, reports their view: "==The nature of mind is originally pure; it is called impure because it is stained by adventitious afflictions==" (心性本淨，客隨煩惱之所雜染，說為不淨).[^5] The word 心性 appears here, together with the image of "guest dust" (客塵) settling on a clean surface. That image would become central in East Asia.

The Mahāyāna _Perfection of Wisdom in Eight Thousand Lines_ takes the idea further. Its first chapter says _tathā hi tac cittam acittam, prakṛtiś cittasya prabhāsvarā_: "that mind is no-mind, for the nature (_prakṛti_) of mind is luminous."[^6] Here luminosity is joined to emptiness. The mind is bright _because_ it is not a thing. Later texts such as the _Ratnagotravibhāga_ went the other way and developed _cittaprakṛti_ ("mind-nature") into a positive account of buddha-nature. William Grosnick has shown that this Indian model of an unmoved, pure nature beneath deluded thought is a real precedent for the Chinese _Awakening of Faith_.[^7]

From its Indian sources, then, 心性 inherits two possibilities: **luminosity as emptiness** and **luminosity as a pure ground**.

## 3. China: Mind and Nature in Two Traditions

### 3.1 A Confucian Inheritance

Buddhist translators did not bring _xin_ and _xing_ into an empty field. Centuries earlier, Mencius had written:

> "==He who has exhausted all his mental constitution knows his nature==. Knowing his nature, he knows Heaven. To preserve one's mental constitution, and nourish one's nature, is the way to serve Heaven."[^8]

The Chinese reads 盡其心者，知其性也. For Mencius, working fully with the heart-mind is how one knows one's nature, and nature is to be _nourished_ (養), like a plant. Chinese readers heard 心性 against this background: nature as something given and good, which cultivation brings to completion.

### 3.2 The _Awakening of Faith_

The _Awakening of Faith in the Mahāyāna_, traditionally said to be translated by Paramārtha, became the central text for East Asian thinking about mind-nature. It describes the One Mind under two aspects, suchness and arising-and-ceasing, and says: "The mind as suchness is the essence of the teaching of the one great, all-encompassing Dharma-realm; that is to say, mind-nature neither arises nor ceases" (心性不生不滅).[^9] It then defines awakening as "the mind's essence being free from thoughts" (心體離念).[^9]

This is the clearest statement of the "pure ground" model. ==An unmoving mind-nature stands beneath moving thoughts== (_nian_ 念), as the ocean stands beneath its waves.[^7]

Tiantai took a different route. Zhiyi (538–597) did not place mind-nature _beneath_ phenomena. He taught that a single moment of mind already contains "three thousand" realms of existence (一念三千). Buddhist dictionaries accordingly describe ==Tiantai mind-nature as including all the dharmas of the ten realms, from hell to buddhahood==.[^3] ==On this view, mind-nature is not a clean surface under the dust. It holds delusion and awakening together==. This "inclusive" reading would matter greatly in Japan, where Tendai carried Tiantai's teaching forward.

### 3.3 Chan: Seeing the Nature

Chan made seeing this nature the whole point of practice. In the _Platform Sūtra_, the Fifth Patriarch warns: "If one does not recognize the fundamental mind, studying the Dharma is of no benefit." Huineng's awakening is expressed as the realization that "the self-natures are fundamentally and naturally pure."[^10] The slogan _jianxing chengfo_ (見性成佛, "seeing the nature, one becomes a buddha") comes from this line of thought.

Guifeng Zongmi (780–841), who was both a Huayan patriarch and a Chan master of the Heze line, gave the idea its most systematic form. He sorted the Chan schools of his time into three tenets (禪三宗):[^11]

1. **Stopping delusion and cultivating the mind** (息妄修心宗)
2. **Eliminating everything, relying on nothing** (泯絕無寄宗)
3. **Directly revealing the mind-nature** (直顯心性宗)

He ranked the third highest. For this tenet, all phenomena, whether existent or empty, "are only the true nature" (皆唯真性), and the core of that nature is a "knowing that is empty and calm" (空寂之知). Zongmi's best-known phrase says: "The single word _knowing_ is the gate of all wonders" (知之一字，眾妙之門).[^11][^12] ==For Zongmi, mind-nature is not a blank ground. It is awareness itself, luminous because it \*knows\*.==

### 3.4 The Neo-Confucian Answer

In the Song dynasty, Confucian thinkers took the vocabulary back and built a "learning of the mind and nature" (心性之學). Cheng Yi taught that "the original nature is just \[a matter of] _li_" (principle).[^13] Zhu Xi adopted Zhang Zai's formula that "the mind unites nature and feelings" (心統性情).[^14] This moved _xing_ away from Buddhist emptiness and toward cosmic moral order. The same two characters now named a different metaphysics.

## 4. Japan: Original Enlightenment and Its Critics

### 4.1 _Hongaku_

In medieval Japan, especially in Tendai, the pure-ground model became original-enlightenment (本覚, _hongaku_) thought. Jacqueline Stone's study describes this as a doctrine in which enlightenment is "neither a goal to be achieved nor a potential to be realized but the true status of all things."[^15] If mind-nature is already awake, and awake in everything, it is no longer clear what practice is _for_.

### 4.2 Dōgen's Refusal

Dōgen (1200–1253) took up this problem directly. In _Bendōwa_ (1231), a questioner offers a comforting teaching:

> "Do not grieve over life and death. There is an instantaneous means for separating from life and death. It is to understand the principle that mind-nature is permanent."[^16]

The questioner's word is _shinshō_ (心性). Dōgen's reply is blunt: "The idea you have just mentioned is not Buddha-dharma at all, but the fallacious view of Senika" (いまいふところの見、またく仏法にあらず、先尼外道が見なり).[^16][^17] Senika was a non-Buddhist who held that a conscious self survives the body. Dōgen's objection is not to mind-nature as such. He objects to _splitting_ a permanent mind from a perishing body. He goes on:

> "In the Buddha-dharma it has always been taught that body and mind are one (身心一如), and that nature and form are not two (性相不二)… In the teaching that speaks of permanence, all dharmas are permanent; body and mind are not divided."[^17]

For Dōgen, mind-nature is not hidden behind form. It _is_ form, lived through a body in practice. The same text insists that practice and realization are one (修証一等), so seated practice does not _reach_ the nature. It _enacts_ it.[^18]

### 4.3 Critical Buddhism

In the 1980s and 1990s, the Komazawa scholars Hakamaya Noriaki and Matsumoto Shirō pushed Dōgen's suspicion much further. Their "Critical Buddhism" (批判仏教) argued that _tathāgatagarbha_ and _hongaku_ doctrines reintroduce a substantial self (_ātman_) that Buddhism rejects, and that they "deny language and thinking in favor of an ineffable and nonconceptual mysticism."[^19] They also claimed that affirming every phenomenon as already enlightened had helped legitimate social discrimination in Japan.

Stone has answered that immanentalist doctrine has served both oppressive and progressive ends, and that nothing in it fixes its social use.[^20] The debate, collected in _Pruning the Bodhi Tree_, is still the sharpest modern test of what 心性 can responsibly claim.[^21]

## 5. Shinshō and the Shinsei Series

The painting series takes this word and reads it _shinsei_, deliberately keeping its homophones in play: 新生 (new birth), 神聖 (the sacred), 真性 (true nature), 神性 (divinity). It defines its subject as "original mind."[^22] The history above suggests four ways this definition can be both rich and careful.

1. **Luminosity is uncovered, not added.** In the _Aṅguttara_ image, brightness belongs to the mind and defilement is a visitor. In painting terms, the unpainted washi may carry the role of mind-nature, while ink and water are "guest dust" that settles and clears. This is an interpretive analogy, not a traditional teaching, but it matches the soft-edged, layered register the series already uses.
2. **The painting is not a container for a permanent mind.** Dōgen's reply to the questioner warns against making mind-nature an object that survives apart from body and act. That supports the series' own principle that the painting is a _residue_ of practice, not the practice itself.[^22] The trace records one moment in which body and mind were not divided. It does not store a mind.
3. **Resonance as knowing.** Zongmi places the core of mind-nature in "knowing." If a viewer responds to the work, that response can be read as the viewer's own knowing waking up, not as an effect the painter produced. This fits the series' inward direction, where resonance is a consequence and not a goal.[^22]
4. **Hold the term lightly.** Critical Buddhism is a reminder that "original mind" can harden into a hidden essence. Presenting 心性 as a way of practising, rather than a substance the art transmits, keeps the work nearer to the _Perfection of Wisdom_ reading: luminous _because_ empty.

---

## 6. Conclusion

心性 does not name one fixed doctrine. It names a question that Buddhist and Confucian thinkers have kept open for more than two thousand years:

- The _Aṅguttara_ made the mind's luminosity the basis of practice.
- The _Perfection of Wisdom_ made it empty.
- The _Awakening of Faith_ made it an unmoving ground.
- Tiantai made it hold every realm at once.
- Zongmi made it knowing.
- Hongaku made it the true status of all things.
- Dōgen refused to separate it from body and form.

For a practice that paints under this name, the most faithful stance may be Dōgen's: mind-nature is neither behind the brushstroke nor stored inside it. It is fully present in the stroke, and it is gone once the stroke is made.
