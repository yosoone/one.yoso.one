# Emotion and Buddhist Awareness for Shinsei

Working notes connecting Shinsei's soft-edged, reiki-aligned language to how Buddhist thought accounts for emotion — material to draw from for the series and its accompanying text. Complements [[What Shapes Our Emotions]], which surveys emotion more broadly across biological, appraisal, conditioning, and cultural layers; this note goes deeper on the Buddhist layer specifically.

## The feeling-tone that starts everything: vedana

In the five aggregates (skandhas), emotion begins earlier than most Western accounts place it: with _vedana_, the raw feeling-tone (pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral) that arises the instant a sense contacts an object. Full emotions — anger, longing, joy — are _sankhara_, mental formations that build on top of that initial tone through habit, memory, and interpretation. This two-layer structure gives a useful visual metaphor: vedana as the first wash of color meeting the canvas, sankhara as the shapes and edges that accumulate around it. Soft edges could read as formations still close to their source, not yet hardened into fixed shapes.

Source: the Samyutta Nikaya's analysis of the aggregates; also Rupert Gethin, _The Foundations of Buddhism_ (Oxford University Press, 1998).

## Dependent origination — emotion as a chain, not a thing

The twelve links of dependent origination (_pratityasamutpada_) trace a causal sequence — contact gives rise to feeling, feeling gives rise to craving, craving gives rise to clinging. Emotion, in this frame, is a moving process held together by conditions, comparable to weather systems forming and dissolving rather than objects with fixed borders. This lines up closely with Shinsei's soft-edge language: a form whose boundary is a gradient rather than a line is already picturing a mental state mid-process, still conditioned, still in motion.

## The three poisons and their transformation

Attachment, aversion, and confusion (_raga_, _dvesha_, _moha_) are the root afflictive emotions (_kleshas_), and the tradition frames the path as their gradual transformation into wisdom and warmth rather than their suppression. Chögyam Trungpa's teaching is especially useful for a painter: he described afflictive emotions as raw energy that becomes workable and even beautiful once seen clearly. That reframes the emotional charge behind a painting as material to work with directly, the way reiki treats energy as something to channel.

Source: Chögyam Trungpa, _Cutting Through Spiritual Materialism_ (Shambhala, 1973).

## The ocean and its waves

Thich Nhat Hanh's recurring image — each emotion as a wave, awareness as the ocean that holds every wave without being consumed by any one of them — offers a direct compositional idea: passages of turbulence (sharper value shifts, denser mark-making) sitting inside a larger field of calm, unbroken ground. The ground stays continuous and steady while the waves rise and fall across it, which mirrors Shinsei's grounding in inner awareness and peace even where a passage carries more intensity.

Source: Thich Nhat Hanh, _No Mud, No Lotus_ (Parallax Press, 2014).

## Stored seeds: the alaya-vijnana

Yogacara philosophy describes a storehouse consciousness (_alaya-vijnana_) that holds the seeds (_bija_) of every past action and emotional pattern, ready to ripen again when conditions allow. This gives the reiki alignment in Shinsei a precise philosophical parallel: both frameworks treat present emotional experience as the surfacing of stored energy rather than something invented fresh in the moment. A layered painting technique — earlier passages showing through later ones — could literalize this idea of past seeds still active beneath the visible surface.

Source: William S. Waldron, _The Buddhist Unconscious: The Alaya-vijnana in the Context of Indian Buddhist Thought_ (Routledge, 2003).

## Cultivated emotional states: the four immeasurables

Alongside the afflictive emotions, Buddhism names four states worth deliberately cultivating (_brahmaviharas_): loving-kindness (_metta_), compassion (_karuna_), sympathetic joy (_mudita_), and equanimity (_upekkha_). These give Shinsei a positive emotional vocabulary to aim toward — a palette and mark-language associated with warmth, spaciousness, shared joy, and level ground could each anchor a distinct passage or panel within the series.

## A scientific bridge

Lisa Feldman Barrett's theory of constructed emotion (_How Emotions Are Made_, Houghton Mifflin Harcourt, 2017) argues that emotions are built in the moment from interoceptive sensation and learned concepts, rather than triggered as fixed, hardwired reactions — this maps closely onto the vedana-to-sankhara sequence above.

Richard Davidson's work at the Center for Healthy Minds, developed partly through the Mind and Life Institute's dialogues with the Dalai Lama, has shown measurable changes in emotional regulation and neural patterns of well-being through sustained meditation practice (Davidson & Lutz, "Buddha's Brain: Neuroplasticity and Meditation," _IEEE Signal Processing Magazine_, 2008). This gives the reiki and meditation side of the practice an academic anchor alongside the philosophical one.

## Questions to carry into the studio

Where in a piece is the vedana — the first feeling-tone before form settles in?
Which passages are waves, and which is the ocean holding them?
Does a layer beneath the surface still read as an active seed, or has it fully resolved into the visible layer?
Which of the four immeasurables is this particular canvas cultivating?
