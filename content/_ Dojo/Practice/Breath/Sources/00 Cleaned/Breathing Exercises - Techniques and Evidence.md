# Breathing Exercises: Techniques and Evidence

The sourced counterpart to [[_ Dojo/Practice/Breath/Sources/01 Raw/Breath|Breath (raw)]]. That file's technique descriptions are broadly sound; its sources are hospital blogs, commercial wellness sites and one supplement retailer, and its detox section is wrong in ways worth correcting rather than softening. This note keeps what survives and says plainly what doesn't.

Sourced under [[Source Standards]]. Claims resting on a source confirmed to exist but not read at the point of the claim are marked **in bold**.[^11]

## 1. What the controlled evidence supports

Three studies carry most of the weight here.

**Breathwork versus no breathwork.** Fincham and colleagues pooled 12 randomised controlled trials with 785 adults and found a small-to-medium effect on subjective stress, with comparable effects for anxiety and depression. Most included trials carried moderate risk of bias, and the authors explicitly warn against a miscalibration between hype and evidence.[^1] That is the honest headline: a real but modest effect, on self-reported measures, from a literature that is not yet methodologically strong.

**Which technique.** Balban and colleagues ran the comparison that matters for this note — 108 participants, five minutes a day for 28 days, randomised across four arms: cyclic sighing (prolonged exhalations), box breathing (equal inhale, hold, exhale, hold), cyclic hyperventilation with retention, and mindfulness meditation. Breathwork beat meditation on mood improvement and respiratory-rate reduction, and **exhale-focused cyclic sighing was the strongest of the four.**[^2]

Two findings from that trial deserve to travel further than they have. First, **the trial found no significant change in heart rate variability or resting heart rate in any group**, cyclic sighing included. Claims that these practices "improve your HRV" are not supported by it. Second, box breathing and cyclic hyperventilation with retention — the two techniques most heavily marketed — were the ones that did _less_ well than simply lengthening the exhale.

**Why slow breathing works.** Zaccaro and colleagues systematically reviewed the psychophysiology of slow breathing, defined as under 10 breaths per minute, in healthy subjects, and found consistent links between increased parasympathetic activity, central nervous system changes, EEG theta power, and emotional control and wellbeing.[^3] The mechanism is autonomic and cortical, not respiratory-chemical.

**Pranayama specifically.** Novaes and colleagues randomised 30 healthy young adults to four weeks of Bhastrika pranayama or control, with fMRI at baseline and after. Anxiety and negative affect fell significantly, and activity changed in the amygdala, anterior cingulate, anterior insula and prefrontal cortex, with reduced resting-state connectivity involving the anterior insula and lateral prefrontal cortex. The authors describe their own imaging analyses as preliminary and exploratory.[^4] This is the study the raw note referred to as "a 2020 randomized controlled trial"; 30 participants is small, and it should be cited with that caveat attached.

## 2. Technique by technique

**Diaphragmatic breathing.** The description in the raw note is fine. **The claim that the diaphragm performs about 80 per cent of the work of breathing is widely repeated but was not traced to a respiratory-physiology source**; it is plausible for quiet breathing and should be checked before use.

**Pursed-lip breathing.** The one technique here with a genuine clinical literature, and the evidence is more mixed than the raw note suggests. It creates variable expiratory resistance by constricting the lips, slowing expiration. A randomised cross-over trial in 25 COPD patients found it did not reduce dynamic hyperinflation induced by a six-minute walk test, though it affected an activities-of-daily-living task differently.[^5] **A systematic review of stable COPD concluded that patients respond unequally, with moderate-to-severe COPD most likely to benefit, and called for higher-quality studies to separate responders from non-responders; some dyspnoea-management guidelines do not specifically endorse it.**[^6] Useful for some people with obstructive disease; not a general-purpose technique.

**4-7-8 breathing.** Popularised by Andrew Weil rather than derived from a trial. It contains a seven-second retention, which places it in the territory covered by [[Breath Retention in Contemplative Practice]]. No controlled evidence specific to the 4-7-8 ratio was found. Its plausible mechanism is simply that it produces a long exhale and a low breath rate, which is what Zaccaro's review and Balban's trial both support.[^3][^2]

**Box breathing.** Tested directly by Balban and found less effective than cyclic sighing on mood and respiratory rate.[^2] Still beneficial relative to baseline; just not the best available option.

**Cyclic sighing.** The best-supported five-minute practice currently available, and absent from the raw note. Double inhale, extended exhale, five minutes daily.[^2]

**Alternate nostril breathing (nadi shodhana).** The raw note's "balance energy flow" and "nadis" framing is traditional description, which is legitimate as description but is not a physiological claim. No trial-level evidence specific to it was traced for this note.

**Kapalabhati and Bhastrika.** Both are forms of voluntary hyperventilation. The raw note's claim that Kapalabhati "purges the body of toxins" is tradition stated as physiology — see section 4. Bhastrika has the Novaes trial behind it for anxiety and affect.[^4] Both carry the cautions in section 5, which the raw note does not give.

**Dirga, Ujjayi, Bhramari.** Described accurately in the raw note as traditional techniques. No controlled evidence traced; treat the benefit claims as traditional until sourced.

## 3. On the word _prāṇāyāma_

The raw note glosses _āyāma_ as "stretching." That is one reading. The compound is also standardly analysed as extension _or_ restraint of the breath, and some lexicographers gloss _prāṇāyāma_ as suspension of the breath — which matters, because the difference is precisely the difference between lengthening the breath and holding it. **Neither gloss was checked in Monier-Williams or another scholar-edited Sanskrit dictionary for this note.** Given that the whole retention question turns on it, it is worth settling properly.

For the historical development of retention within haṭha yoga, and why the circulating translations of the _Haṭhapradīpikā_ should not be trusted, see [[Breath Retention in Contemplative Practice]], which carries the philological sources.

## 4. The detox claims

This section of the raw note should not move forward. Its sources are a supplement retailer and four wellness blogs, and its central claims are false or unsupportable.

**"Around 70 per cent of the body's toxins are discharged through breathing."** No physiological basis. Elimination is not apportioned across organs as a percentage of "toxins" in any clinical or physiological source. What the lungs excrete is carbon dioxide — a normal metabolic end-product of respiration, not a toxin in the sense the sentence implies. The figure originates in supplement marketing.

**"The lungs are the pumps of the lymphatic system."** False. Lymph is propelled by a combination of extrinsic forces and the intrinsic, active contraction of lymphatic muscle cells in the collecting vessels, with one-way valves preventing backflow. At rest, roughly one-third of lymph transport in the human lower extremities comes from skeletal muscle compression and about two-thirds from intrinsic pumping.[^7] Breathing movements appear in the physiology as _one_ extrinsic source among several — alongside blood vessel pulsations, gastrointestinal muscle contraction and skeletal muscle contraction.[^7] There is no sense in which the lungs are the pump.

**"Oxygen stimulates the creation of more white blood cells."** Unsupported. Leukopoiesis is not driven by breathing more deeply.

**"Toxins build up due to inactivity and shallow breathing."** Unsupported.

**"Kapalabhati purges the body of toxins."** A traditional claim. Keep it as what the tradition says; do not restate it as physiology.

The honest version of this section is short: breathing eliminates CO₂, and that is a real and important function, but it is gas exchange rather than detoxification, and no breathing practice accelerates hepatic or renal clearance of anything.

## 5. Safety, which the raw note omits

Kapalabhati, Bhastrika and cyclic hyperventilation are hyperventilation techniques. Lowering arterial CO₂ delays the urge to breathe, which is the mechanism behind hypoxic blackout when hyperventilation precedes a breath-hold.[^8] Breath-holding drownings occur in otherwise healthy people without visible distress.[^9] None of these should be practised in or near water, and cyclic hyperventilation with retention drives oxygen saturation far below clinical norms during the hold.[^10]

4-7-8 contains a retention and should be treated accordingly. Cardiovascular disease, seizure disorders and pregnancy are reasons to seek medical input first.

## 6. What is still open

- Does any trial isolate alternate nostril breathing, Ujjayi or Bhramari from general slow breathing?
- Is the diaphragm's share of quiet ventilation actually around 80 per cent, and in which source?
- What does Monier-Williams give for _āyāma_?
- Does the HRV null result in Balban replicate, or was 28 days simply too short?
