# Breath Retention in Contemplative Practice

Sourced under [[Source Standards]]. Claims resting on a source that was confirmed to exist but not read at the point of the claim are marked **in bold**. See [[#Verification status]] for what was checked and how.[^29]

## 1. Three things called "holding the breath"

The phrase covers at least three practices with different mechanisms and different risk profiles. Most confusion in contemporary breathwork comes from collapsing them.

1. **Retention as energetic containment** — the breath held in or out to concentrate a subtle substance in the body. Indian _kumbhaka_ (कुम्भक, breath retention), Daoist _biqi_ (閉氣, breath retention), Tibetan _gtum mo_ (inner heat) vase breath.
2. **Retention as suspension of mental movement** — breath stillness taken as index or cause of mental stillness, sometimes arising spontaneously rather than being forced.
3. **Retention as physiological stressor** — the freediving and cyclic-hyperventilation register, where the hold is a hypoxic and hypercapnic load deliberately applied.

## 2. Indian yoga: retention as the whole practice

In haṭha yoga, retention is not an accessory to breathing practice. Mallinson and Singleton identify pure breath-retention, unaccompanied by inhalation or exhalation, as the culminating _prāṇāyāma_ (प्राणायाम, breath control) of the later haṭha texts, and give the _Dattātreyayogaśāstra_ account of _kevala kumbhaka_ as their example.[^1] **This is taken from a work citing _Roots of Yoga_, not from the book itself.**

Birch dates the _Dattātreyayogaśāstra_ to the twelfth or thirteenth century and places it as the text that reconciled the rivalry between Haṭha and Rājayoga into a hierarchy of four yogas — Mantra, Laya, Haṭha and Rāja. Svātmārāma later dismantled that hierarchy in the _Haṭhapradīpikā_, melding the two systems and asserting their mutual dependence, and in doing so created a complete system he called Haṭhayoga.[^2] The rivalry itself turned on effort: the _Amanaskayoga_ argued that Rājayoga was superior because its methods were effortless, where Haṭhayoga required exertion and was superfluous.[^2] That dispute is the tradition's own version of the question this note is asking.

Anything doctrinal drawn from the _Haṭhapradīpikā_ should now go through the critical edition rather than through the circulating translations, which are edited from at best nine manuscripts from a single region of India where at least 250 survive.[^3] **The specific verses on retention purifying the channels and on the breath being tamed by degrees were not checked against the critical edition for this note**; they appear in the popular translations and should be confirmed at _Haṭhapradīpikā Online_ before being quoted.

## 3. Buddhism: the tradition that declined

The most useful finding for a Buddhist frame is that early Buddhism tried breath retention and rejected it.

In the _Mahāsaccaka Sutta_ the Bodhisatta recounts attempting the breathless absorption among his austerities: cutting off the breath through mouth and nose produced winds roaring from the ears like a blacksmith's bellows, and cutting off the ears as well produced a sensation of being drilled into the skull. Energy and mindfulness stayed sharp, but the body was disturbed rather than tranquil, because he had pushed too hard in painful striving.[^4] The episode belongs to the practices he abandoned before awakening.

Polak's _Reexamining Jhāna_ treats the description of the breathless meditation alongside the other methods of meditation criticised in the Suttapiṭaka, and examines the authenticity of the bodhisatta discourses.[^5] **This comes from the volume's contents list; the argument was not read, so none of Polak's conclusions is asserted here.** Anālayo's comparative work sets the Pali account beside its Chinese parallels.[^6]

What replaces retention is observational. The _Ānāpānasati Sutta_ gives 16 steps grouped into four tetrads, using awareness of the breath as object;[^7] none of the 16 instructs retention.

## 4. The Vajrayana exception: vase breath

Tibetan _gtum mo_ (inner heat) does retain, through vase breathing — inhalation held low in the abdomen with isometric contraction. Kozhevnikov and colleagues measured expert practitioners in monasteries of eastern Tibet and recorded reliable rises in axillary temperature into the slight-to-moderate fever range, up to 38.3 °C, during the Forceful Breath form, with the magnitude of the rise correlating with increases in EEG alpha power. They separate a somatic component, the thermogenesis produced by the breathing itself, from a neurocognitive one, the visualisation that sustains the elevation; without visualisation neither meditators nor untrained controls could sustain it long, and increases stayed within the normal range.[^8] **Benson's earlier _Nature_ measurements of _g tum-mo_ practitioners are cited throughout this literature but were not consulted directly.**[^9]

So the legend holds up partially, and the retention does real thermogenic work — but inseparably from the visualisation and the lineage context.

## 5. Daoist inner alchemy: retention, then its supersession

_Taixi_ (胎息, embryonic breathing) names the target state: breathing as an embryo does, without nose or mouth. The associated techniques include _xingqi_ (行氣, circulating breath), _biqi_ (閉氣, breath retention), _fuqi_ (服氣, ingesting breath) and _taishi_ (胎食, swallowing saliva).[^10]

The decisive move is historical. Tang internal alchemists reframed embryonic breathing away from _waiqi_ (外氣, the external qi of air, inhaled and retained) toward _neiqi_ (內氣, the internal qi of the organs), so that adepts circulated and remoulded visceral energy rather than holding air.[^10] **This shift is described in Despeux's encyclopedia entry, which was confirmed bibliographically but not read.** The primary material sits in the Tang _Taishang yangsheng taixi qijing_.[^11] Kohn and Sakade's volume is the standing English collection on the wider technique family.[^12]

Retention of air, in other words, was progressively reinterpreted as retention of qi, and the literal breath-hold became a beginner's scaffold.

## 6. Japan: Hakuin and the injury case

Hakuin Ekaku's 『夜船閑話』(_Yasenkanna_, Idle Talk on a Night Boat, 1757) is a documented account of contemplative overexertion and its repair. Hakuin describes _zenbyō_ (禅病, Zen sickness) contracted through severe practice, and the cure he learned from the hermit Hakuyū: _naikan_ (内観, inner contemplation), settling the mind on the _tanden_ (丹田, the cinnabar field below the navel), with the _nanso_ (軟酥, soft butter) visualisation of warm butter melting down from the crown into the lower body. Hakuin describes the technique as concentrating _ki_ (氣, vital energy) in the lower body, and the text served for two and a half centuries both as a cure for Zen sickness and as a regimen for preserving health under the rigours of training.[^13]

**A tension noted in the secondary literature — that _ki_ must be stored in the _tanden_ yet also circulated through labour lest it stagnate — was found in an article abstract whose journal, volume and pages are unconfirmed.**[^14] If it holds, it is the same storage-versus-circulation problem the Tang Daoist reframing addresses.

## 7. Eastern Christianity: the minor case

Hesychast prayer attaches the Jesus Prayer to the rhythm of the breath. The earliest developed Greek description is Nicephorus the Hesychast's _On Vigilance and the Guarding of the Heart_, from the late thirteenth century;[^15] a closely similar text, the _Method of Holy Prayer and Attentiveness_ attributed to Symeon the New Theologian but almost certainly not his, was edited with a French translation by Hausherr.[^16]

**The specific claim that Nicephorus prescribed a brief suspension of the breath after inhalation, between the two halves of the prayer, was found only in devotional writing and is not confirmed from the Greek or from Hausherr.** It should be checked against _Patrologia Graeca_ 147.945–66 before use.

Ware's assessment of the physical techniques is deflationary and is the benchmark: Gregory Palamas regarded them as theologically defensible but secondary and suited mainly to beginners, and for the hesychast masters the essential thing was the inner invocation rather than control of the breath.[^17] **Ware's chapter was read as a reproduction on a personal teaching site, not in print.** A sourcing warning travels with it: the Kadloubovsky and Palmer English Philokalia renders Theophan the Recluse's nineteenth-century Russian rather than the original Greek, with resulting inaccuracies; Gouillard's _Petite Philocalie de la Prière du Coeur_ is closer to the Greek.[^17]

## 8. What happens physiologically

**The diving response.** Breath-hold produces peripheral vasoconstriction with rising arterial pressure, bradycardia, reduced cardiac output, increased cerebral and myocardial blood flow, splenic contraction and preserved oxygen delivery to brain and heart.[^18] The response is shared across diving mammals and its neural control is now partly mapped.[^19]

**The spleen as an oxygen reserve.** Repeated apneas produce a rapid fall in spleen volume of around 20 per cent, largely complete during the first hold, with no recovery between holds and only partial recovery an hour later — consistent with active contraction rather than passive collapse, and explaining why successive attempts lengthen.[^20] A reduction of 18 to 35 per cent in spleen volume raises haematocrit by two to six per cent, and contraction begins within about three seconds of apnea onset.[^21]

**The struggle phase.** The involuntary diaphragmatic contractions that begin partway into a hold are functional rather than a failure: the increasingly negative intrathoracic pressure swings significantly drive the rise in left-ventricular stroke volume, while introducing marked respiratory variability into cerebral blood flow.[^22]

**What ends a hold is carbon dioxide, not oxygen.** This is the hinge of section 10 and of the safety section.

## 9. Experimental evidence

**Cyclic hyperventilation with retention.** Kox and colleagues trained volunteers in a protocol of hyperventilation cycles followed by breath retention and tested them against intravenous endotoxin. The trained group showed large increases in epinephrine release, increased anti-inflammatory mediators and a dampened pro-inflammatory cytokine response. The authors are explicit that the design cannot isolate which component produced the effect, and attribute the epinephrine rise to both the hyperventilation phase and the hypoxia of retention.[^23] The intervention also included cold exposure and meditation training, and there were 12 subjects per arm.

**Breathwork in general.** Fincham and colleagues found a small-to-medium effect on subjective stress across 12 randomised controlled trials with 785 adults, with comparable effects for anxiety and depression; most included studies carried moderate risk of bias, and the authors urge caution against a miscalibration between hype and evidence.[^24] None of these trials isolates retention as a variable.

There is, as of this note, no body of controlled evidence on breath retention as a practice in its own right. Claims made for _kumbhaka_ specifically rest on tradition, not on trials.

## 10. Air hunger as interoceptive exposure

Klein's suffocation false alarm hypothesis proposes that a physiological misreading by a suffocation monitor misfires an evolved alarm, producing sudden respiratory distress, brief hyperventilation, panic and the urge to flee, with carbon dioxide hypersensitivity as the sign of a deranged monitor.[^25] The amygdala is itself a chemosensor, detecting CO₂ and acidosis through acid-sensing ion channels and eliciting fear behaviour.[^26] Feinstein and colleagues complicate the picture usefully: amygdala lesion patients panic more on CO₂ inhalation, clinically anxious patients have smaller amygdalae alongside pronounced CO₂ hypersensitivity, and stimulation of the amygdala induces apnea outside the patient's awareness — suggesting the amygdala inhibits CO₂-evoked fear and can itself trigger apnea.[^27]

The research-relevant reading: a retention is a titrated interoceptive exposure. Sitting with air hunger without acting on it is structurally the same procedure as clinical interoceptive exposure. That gives the traditional claim — that retention produces equanimity — a mechanism which does not require subtle-body ontology, and which does not refute one either.

## 11. Safety

The most empirically settled part of the literature.

- **Never hyperventilate before a hold.** Pre-hold hyperventilation lowers arterial CO₂ and delays the cerebral signal to breathe, allowing oxygen to fall to critical levels before respiratory drive triggers.[^28]
- **Never practise retention in or near water.** Breath-holding drownings in otherwise healthy people occur without visible distress, and the mechanism is well understood.[^28] Mortality in breath-hold diving falls mainly on less well trained recreational divers and competitive spearfishers who succumb to hypoxia.[^18]
- **Cyclic hyperventilation protocols are a stressor, not a relaxation practice.** Oxygen saturation during the retention phase falls far below clinical norms.[^23]

Cardiovascular disease, seizure disorders and pregnancy are reasons to treat all of the above as contraindicated without medical input.

## 12. Where the traditions diverge

| Question | Haṭha yoga | Early Buddhism | Daoist neidan | Hesychasm |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Is retention the goal? | Yes — _kevala kumbhaka_ | No — explicitly abandoned | Transitional; superseded by _neiqi_ | No — secondary to the invocation |
| Forced or spontaneous? | Forced, then effortless | Spontaneous stilling only | Forced early, effortless later | Prayer-linked, minor |
| The tradition's own warning | Haṭha requires exertion and was called superfluous | Body disturbed, not tranquil | Stagnation of _ki_ | Suited to beginners only |

Four unrelated lineages converge on the same structure: forced retention is an early-stage technique that the mature form of each tradition supersedes. That convergence is a stronger finding than any single tradition's claims, and it is the point most consistently missing from contemporary breathwork literature.

## 13. Open questions

- Does the critical edition of the _Haṭhapradīpikā_ change the retention verses in any material way?
- Is the Nicephorus suspension-after-inhalation detail present in the Greek at all, or is it a modern devotional accretion?
- Is there any controlled study isolating retention from the hyperventilation that usually precedes it in modern protocols?
- Does the _zenbyō_ literature describe anything recognisable as a breath-practice injury, or is the causation purely ascetic overexertion?
