# Breathing Exercises

Working collection. Originally assembled from AI answers sourced to hospital blogs, commercial wellness sites and a supplement retailer; reformatted and re-sourced on 20 September 2026.[^14] Technique descriptions are kept; unsupported claims are marked or removed, and the reasoning is in [[Breathing Exercises - Techniques and Evidence]].

Breathing exercises are widely taught for stress reduction, relaxation and control of psychophysiological states. Slow breathing — under 10 breaths per minute — has consistent links to increased parasympathetic activity, changes in EEG theta power, and emotional control and wellbeing in healthy subjects.[^1] Across 12 randomised controlled trials with 785 adults, breathwork showed a small-to-medium effect on subjective stress, with comparable effects on anxiety and depression; most trials carried moderate risk of bias.[^3]

## General techniques

### 1. Diaphragmatic (belly) breathing

One hand on the chest, the other on the belly. Inhale through the nose so the belly expands while the chest stays still, then exhale slowly through the mouth.

> **Unsourced.** The often-quoted figure that the diaphragm does about 80% of the work of breathing was not traced to a respiratory-physiology source. Plausible for quiet breathing; check before repeating.

### 2. Pursed-lip breathing

In through the nose, out through pursed lips for at least twice as long — a variable expiratory resistance created by constricting the lips.

The evidence is more mixed than usually presented. A randomised cross-over trial in 25 COPD patients found it did **not** reduce dynamic hyperinflation induced by a six-minute walk test.[^5] **A systematic review of stable COPD found responders and non-responders, with moderate-to-severe COPD most likely to benefit.**[^6] A clinical technique for obstructive disease, not a general-purpose one.

### 3. 4-7-8 breathing

Inhale through the nose for four, hold for seven, exhale through the mouth for eight. Four cycles.

Popularised by Andrew Weil rather than derived from a trial. It contains a seven-second retention — see [[Breath Retention in Contemplative Practice]] for the safety considerations. Its plausible mechanism is simply a long exhale at a low breath rate.[^1]

### 4. Box breathing

Equal counts of inhale, hold, exhale, hold — typically four seconds each.

Tested head-to-head by Balban and found **less** effective than cyclic sighing on mood and respiratory rate.[^2] Still better than baseline; not the best available option.

### 5. Cyclic sighing

Double inhale, extended exhale. Five minutes daily.

**The best-supported short practice currently available.** In a randomised trial of 108 participants over 28 days, exhale-focused cyclic sighing beat box breathing, cyclic hyperventilation with retention, and mindfulness meditation on mood improvement and respiratory-rate reduction.[^2]

### 6. Alternate nostril breathing (nadi shodhana)

Alternating inhalation and exhalation through each nostril. Traditionally described as balancing energy flow through the _nadis_, the channels said to circulate energy. That is tradition speaking, not physiology; no trial-level evidence specific to the technique was traced.

## Key claims worth keeping

Regular slow breathing is associated with parasympathetic activation and improved emotional regulation.[^1] What it does **not** reliably do: the Balban trial found no significant change in heart rate variability or resting heart rate in any arm, cyclic sighing included.[^2] Claims that breathwork improves HRV are not supported by it.

# Yogic breathing (pranayama)

Pranayama is one of the eight limbs of yoga in Patanjali's _Yoga Sūtras_. _Prāṇa_ is life force or vital energy; **_āyāma_ is usually glossed as "stretching" or extension, but is also read as restraint, and some lexicography gives _prāṇāyāma_ as suspension of the breath.** That distinction matters — it is the difference between lengthening the breath and holding it — and it has not been checked in a scholar-edited Sanskrit dictionary.

For the history of breath retention within haṭha yoga, and why the circulating translations of the _Haṭhapradīpikā_ are unreliable, see Birch[^11] and the critical edition.[^12]

## Key techniques

**1. Dirga (three-part breath).** Foundational for beginners; strengthens diaphragmatic breathing, breath awareness and lung capacity. Traditional description; no controlled evidence traced.

**2. Ujjayi (victorious / ocean breath).** Gently narrowing the throat to create resistance, producing a soft oceanic sound. Foundational in ashtanga and vinyasa. Traditional description.

**3. Nadi shodhana (alternate nostril).** As above.

**4. Kapalabhati (skull-shining breath).** Gentle inhale, forceful exhale. **Traditionally said to purge the body of toxins — this is tradition stated as physiology, and should not be repeated as a physiological claim.** See the retraction below. Kapalabhati is a form of voluntary hyperventilation; see safety.

**5. Bhastrika (bellows breath).** Forceful inhalation and exhalation, energising. Also voluntary hyperventilation; see safety.

**6. Bhramari (humming bee breath).** Long deep inhales with a humming exhalation. Traditional description.

## Evidence

Thirty healthy young adults were randomised to four weeks of Bhastrika or control, with fMRI at baseline and after. Anxiety and negative affect fell significantly, and activity changed in the amygdala, anterior cingulate, anterior insula and prefrontal cortex, with reduced resting-state connectivity involving anterior insula and lateral prefrontal cortex. The authors describe the imaging analyses as preliminary and exploratory.[^4]

This is the study previously cited here only as "a 2020 randomized controlled trial." Thirty participants is small; cite it with that attached.

> The "2019 study" on pranayama and lung efficiency in athletes, referenced in the original version of this note, could not be traced to a journal and has been removed.

## Getting started

Five to ten minutes is enough to notice a shift; longer sessions of 20 to 30 minutes deepen it. Consistency matters more than duration. The Balban protocol that produced measurable mood effects was five minutes a day for 28 days.[^2] Kapalabhati and Bhastrika should wait until the basics are comfortable and a qualified teacher is involved.

## Safety

Kapalabhati, Bhastrika and cyclic hyperventilation are hyperventilation techniques. Lowering arterial CO₂ delays the urge to breathe, which is the mechanism behind hypoxic blackout when hyperventilation precedes a breath-hold.[^8] Breath-holding drownings occur in otherwise healthy people without visible distress.[^9] **Never practise these in or near water.** Cyclic hyperventilation with retention drives oxygen saturation far below clinical norms during the hold.[^10] 4-7-8 contains a retention and should be treated accordingly. Cardiovascular disease, seizure disorders and pregnancy are reasons to seek medical input first.

# Breathing and detoxification — retracted

The section formerly here claimed that around 70% of the body's toxins are discharged through breathing, that the lungs are the pumps of the lymphatic system, that oxygen stimulates white blood cell production, and that toxins build up through shallow breathing. Its sources were a supplement retailer and four wellness blogs. **All four claims are false or unsupportable and have been removed.**

What is true:

**Gas exchange.** The lungs take in oxygen and excrete carbon dioxide, a normal metabolic end-product of respiration. That is real and important — but it is gas exchange, not detoxification, and CO₂ is not a "toxin" in the sense the original claim implied. No breathing practice accelerates hepatic or renal clearance.

**Lymph.** Lymph is propelled by the intrinsic, active contraction of lymphatic muscle cells in the collecting vessels, plus extrinsic forces, with one-way valves preventing backflow. At rest, roughly one-third of lymph transport in the human lower extremities comes from skeletal muscle compression and about two-thirds from intrinsic pumping.[^7] Breathing movements appear in the physiology as one extrinsic source among several, alongside vascular pulsation and gastrointestinal muscle contraction.[^7] The lungs are not the pump.

Full reasoning in [[Breathing Exercises - Techniques and Evidence]].
