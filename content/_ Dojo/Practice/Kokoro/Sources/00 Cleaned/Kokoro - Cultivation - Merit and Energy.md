# Cultivating _Kokoro_: Merit, Energy, and Self-Cultivation in Japanese Religious and Intellectual History

> **Revision note (13 September 2026).** Citations checked against the sources — Nishihira 2021, Kurita 2018, Ahn 2008, the Usui memorial title, the _gokai_ text and its derivation from Suzuki Bizan, Kuroda 1981, the 1871 _Self-Help_ translation, the 1872 Shugendō ban — and all held. One discrepancy flagged at footnote 47. Header links corrected to the vault's current filenames. No change to the argument or the text.

## Abstract

This essay examines how Japanese traditions have conceptualised the cultivation of _kokoro_ (心, heart-mind). It argues that two idioms recur across otherwise divergent Buddhist, Shinto, Confucian, and modern therapeutic contexts: **merit**, which accrues, and **energy**, which circulates. Within these idioms _kokoro_ functions at once as the field of cultivation, the register in which merit is recorded, and the medium through which power moves. The most doctrinally reflexive traditions subject each idiom to critique — Chan/Zen's refusal of calculable merit, Shinran's relocation of merit to Amida, Hakuin's diagnosis of a meditation that exhausts vital energy — yet these critiques relocate the idioms rather than abolish them. The late Meiji discourse of _shūyō_ (修養, self-cultivation) then reabsorbed both idioms into a nominally non-religious ethics of character and health, a synthesis condensed in the 1927 memorial to Usui Mikao, which is titled a monument to his _kudoku_ (功徳, merit). Drawing on conceptual history, the historiography of "practical" and "common" religion, and recent scholarship on psycho-spiritual therapies, the essay closes with hypotheses for reading Natsume Sōseki's 『こころ』(_Kokoro_, Heart, 1914).

**Keywords:** _kokoro_; _shūyō_; _shugyō_; _yōjō_; _kudoku_; _ekō_; _ki_; _kaji_; Shugendō; _seishin ryōhō_; Reiki; Natsume Sōseki

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The problem

Studies of _kokoro_ in Japanese thought usually proceed doctrinally, asking what the heart-mind _is_: consciousness-only, One Mind, Buddha-nature, the sincere heart of nativist poetics. Less attention has been paid to what practitioners understood themselves to be _doing_ to it, and to the vocabularies in which they described its transformation. This essay asks how Japanese traditions have articulated the cultivation of _kokoro_, and what conceptual work two recurrent idioms — merit and energy — perform in those articulations.

### 1.2 Argument

The essay advances three claims. First, across traditions, cultivation is described either as **accumulation** (merit gained, stored, transferred) or as **circulation** (energy gathered, directed, received), and _kokoro_ is positioned as field, register, and medium. Second, reflexive traditions critique these idioms from within, but the critiques typically relocate merit and energy rather than dispense with them. Third, the modern discourse of _shūyō_ functioned as a shared "grammar" that absorbed both idioms under the categories of character and health, with ambiguous political consequences.

### 1.3 Method and scope

The approach is conceptual-historical and attentive to emic vocabulary. It follows Nishihira Tadashi's proposal that the English word "cultivation," which has been used to render _shūyō_, _shugyō_, _yōjō_, and _keiko_ alike, can be read back as a "reverse import" that reveals their overlaps.[^1] Regarding efficacy, the essay adopts methodological agnosticism: it describes claims about merit, _ki_, and spiritual power without adjudicating them. Its scope is Japan, with Indian and Chinese antecedents introduced only where necessary. A limitation should be stated plainly: Japanese-language scholarship is accessed here largely through English-language syntheses and translations.

## 2. Terms and Historiography

### 2.1 The vocabulary of cultivation

Nishihira observes that _shūyō_ is neither a modern translation term nor a word used continuously since the Edo period, and that its scope is visible only when it is read against its neighbours.[^2] His distinctions are useful as a working typology. _Shūyō_ has historically been bound to politics — Neo-Confucian self-cultivation was the path to governance — whereas _shugyō_ (修行), _yōjō_ (養生), and _keiko_ (稽古) were rarely shaped directly by political power.[^3] Following Yuasa Yasuo, he characterises _shugyō_ as practice that imposes restrictions stricter than social norms and aims beyond ordinary health, premised on an awareness of human evil, whereas Neo-Confucian _shūyō_ presumes an original goodness and proceeds more gently.[^4] _Shūyō_ is expected to be useful in secular life; _shugyō_ may exceed or sacrifice that usefulness.[^5]

Janine Tasca Sawada's study of nineteenth-century Japan widens the frame. She treats personal cultivation as an interpretive system encompassing moral, ritual, physiological, and educational processes believed to produce well-being, and argues that it cuts across the conventional distinction between popular and elite religion.[^6] Stephen Covell's review identifies her central contribution as a shared "grammar" of cultivation beneath content that could differ dramatically.[^7]

### 2.2 Merit

_Kudoku_ renders Sanskrit terms for meritorious power and virtuous quality (_puṇya_, _guṇa_); _ekō_ (回向, _pariṇāmanā_) names the turning-over of merit to others.[^8] Two historiographical corrections frame its study. Gregory Schopen's epigraphic research showed that Indian monastics dedicated merit to deceased parents, undermining the older view that ancestor-directed merit-transfer was a Chinese accommodation.[^9] Ian Reader and George Tanabe argued that the pursuit of _genze riyaku_ (現世利益, this-worldly benefits), long relegated to "superstition," is scripturally supported, doctrinally integrated across most Buddhist sects, and central to a Japanese "common religion."[^10]

### 2.3 Energy

For _ki_ (気), Shigehisa Kuriyama's comparative history of medicine establishes the conceptual distance between East Asian conceptions of the body organised around _qi_ and Greek anatomical models.[^11] Yuasa's philosophical work treats _ki_ as the key to an Eastern theory of mind–body unity achieved through cultivation (§4.1).[^12] The term _rei_ (霊) requires particular caution. Justin Stein notes that no extant pre-war text adequately defines ==the \*rei\* of \*reiki\*, that rendering \*rei\* compounds solely as "spiritual" is misleading, and that contemporary therapists used \*reiki\* interchangeably with \*seiki\* (精気, vitality), \*prana\*, and "aura."[^13] For \*tama\* (魂, soul), the Kokugakuin glossary is the standard reference== (§4.6).

Categorical caution is also required for "Shinto." Kuroda Toshio's influential argument held that Shinto did not exist as an independent religion before modern nationalism and the early Meiji policy separating kami and buddhas; until then kami cults were embedded within an exoteric-esoteric Buddhist order.[^14] The "Shinto" idioms discussed below are therefore read as strands of a combinatory field rather than as a separate system.

### 2.4 Historiographical orientations

Two orientations structure the literature. The first is doctrinal and sectarian, privileging normative texts. The second is practice-centred. Gaynor Sekimori summarises the shift: scholars moved from "what should be" to "what is or was," recognising that practice rather than doctrine has defined Japanese religion.[^15] The shift also corrected earlier disdain. Sekimori records that Anesaki Masaharu, in lectures of 1913–15, dismissed _yamabushi_ as representatives of crude superstition.[^16] The present essay draws on both orientations, reading doctrinal refusals of merit and energy (§3.3–3.5) against the practices they addressed.

## 3. Economies of Merit

### 3.1 Transfer and intention

The Mahāyāna innovation that matters most for _kokoro_ is not the existence of merit but its transferability. _Ekō_ makes merit a function of **intention** as well as act: an offering's scope depends on the direction in which the dedicating mind turns it, and dedication verses (_ekōmon_, 回向文) conventionally close Japanese Buddhist liturgies.[^8] Merit thus becomes a relation between the heart-mind and other beings rather than a private store.

### 3.2 Benefit as common religion

Reader and Tanabe's argument (§2.2) has a direct consequence for the history of cultivation: for most Japanese practitioners, the cultivated _kokoro_ and the protected life were never separate goods. Benefits sought through prayer to buddhas and kami — health, safety, success — were understood as the ordinary fruits of sincere practice, and their pursuit was underwritten by scripture and promoted by monastic institutions.[^10]

### 3.3 Refusal: "No merit"

Against this economy stands Chan/Zen's most famous refusal. In the commentary to the first case of the 『碧巌録』(_Hekiganroku_, Blue Cliff Record), Emperor Wu of Liang enumerates his temples and ordinations and asks what merit he has earned; Bodhidharma answers that there is none (_mukudoku_, 無功徳).[^17] John McRae's historiography of Chan cautions that encounter dialogues of this kind are later literary productions rather than records of historical exchanges; their significance lies in the rhetorical position they construct.[^18] That position is precise: merit reckoned by a calculating mind is already forfeit, because the giver and the accountant cannot be the same heart.

### 3.4 Reversal: Shinran

Shinran reaches a structurally similar conclusion by a different route. The self-power mind aspiring to enlightenment cannot reach fulfilment,[^19] so he reverses the direction of _ekō_. Amida directs virtue to beings in two aspects: for their going forth to the Pure Land (_ōsō ekō_, 往相回向) and for their return to this world to guide others (_gensō ekō_, 還相回向).[^20] Merit is not abolished but relocated: it belongs to Amida, and the practitioner's _kokoro_ becomes the site of its reception.

### 3.5 Enactment: Dōgen

Dōgen, by contrast, retains merit-language. Fascicles of the 『正法眼蔵』(_Shōbōgenzō_, Treasury of the True Dharma Eye) such as "Kesa kudoku" (袈裟功徳, The Merit of the Robe) and "Kuyō shobutsu" (供養諸仏, Making Offerings to Buddhas) extol the merit of the robe and of offerings.[^21] Read alongside his insistence on the unity of practice and realisation, the effect is to relocate merit _inside_ practice: wearing the robe is not an investment toward awakening but its enactment.

### 3.6 Virtue and repayment

Shinto discourse lacks a doctrine of merit in the Buddhist sense but possesses _shintoku_ (神徳), a kami's particular power to confer blessings.[^22] In the nineteenth century Ninomiya Sontoku (1787–1856) developed an ethical economy of _hōtoku_ (報徳, repaying virtue). John A. Tucker reads Ninomiya's response to rural poverty through the _Great Learning_ and _Doctrine of the Mean_: not esoteric remedies but tireless labour undertaken for others, through which virtue is repaid with plenty.[^23] Eiji Takemura situates this ethic within Tokugawa perceptions of work alongside Ishida Baigan.[^24] The subsequent history is political. Thomas Havens's study of agrarian nationalism begins with Ninomiya, and the Home Ministry sponsored _hōtoku_ associations in rural communities in 1906, in the wider context of the state-led Local Improvement Movement analysed by Kenneth Pyle.[^25]

## 4. Idioms of Energy

### 4.1 _Ki_ and the lived body

Yuasa's _The Body, Self-Cultivation, and Ki-Energy_ examines _ki_ in acupuncture, Buddhist and Daoist meditation, and the martial arts, proposing a schematisation of the lived body to explain how these traditions achieve mind–body oneness. Yuasa also treats research on _ki_ as grounds for an epistemological critique of modern science.[^12] One need not accept the latter programme to adopt his phenomenological point: in these traditions _kokoro_ is not an observer of bodily energy but a mode of its circulation.

### 4.2 _Yōjō_: circulation as ethics

Kaibara Ekken's 『養生訓』(_Yōjōkun_, Precepts for Nourishing Life, 1713) defines nourishing life as the care of _ki_: internal _ki_ must not stagnate, true _ki_ is gathered in the body's centres through posture and quiet breathing, and the practitioner is to avoid anger, fear, excessive speech, and excessive desire.[^26] Tsujimoto Masashi, Nishihira reports, has reread the _Yōjōkun_ as a theory of _shūyō_ rather than a mere health manual, and Mary Evelyn Tucker's study places Ekken's programme within Neo-Confucian moral and spiritual cultivation.[^27] Kurita Hidehiko notes that the word _shūyō_ itself already appears in this literature.[^28] Nishihira adds a modern coda: _yōjō_ became Western-style hygiene only when disentangled from _shūyō_.[^29]

### 4.3 Hakuin: bringing the heart-mind down

Hakuin Ekaku (1686–1769) supplies the clearest case of cultivation that fails and is repaired through _ki_. In 『夜船閑話』(_Yasenkanna_, Idle Talk on a Night Boat) he narrates his "Zen sickness" (_zenbyō_, 禅病) and its cure through two methods attributed to the hermit Hakuyū: _naikan_ (内観), introspective contemplation centred on breath in the lower abdomen (_tanden_, 丹田), and _nanso no hō_ (軟酥の法), the visualisation of a fragrant ointment melting down through the body from the crown.[^30] Juhn Ahn shows that Hakuin, writing to a sick monk, prescribed the ointment method for depletion and for the exhaustion of the mind's vital energy, claiming that accumulated congestion would descend together with the mind as water flows downhill.[^31]

Two features matter for _kokoro_. First, the cure spatially **relocates** the heart-mind, drawing it down from chest and head into the belly. Second, Ahn argues that Hakuin's genuine Zen is "meditative work in the midst of activity" (_dōchū no kufū_, 動中の工夫), and that the malady of meditation is better read as anxiety about labour and exhaustion than about stagnation alone — a tension Hakuin shares with Ekken.[^32]

### 4.4 _Kaji_: energy as grace

Shingon offers a model in which power is received rather than generated. In 『即身成仏義』(_Sokushin jōbutsu gi_, The Meaning of Becoming a Buddha in This Very Body), Kūkai explains _[[kaji]]_ (加持, empowerment) through light and water: ==\*ka\* is the Buddha's sunlight appearing in the mind-water== of a being; _ji_ is the practitioner's mind-water sensing that light; when this is contemplated, the three mysteries of Buddha and practitioner unite.[^33] _Kokoro_ here is neither generator nor ledger but a reflecting surface whose stillness determines what it receives. Pamela Winfield's study traces how this doctrine of empowerment was applied to healing.[^34]

### 4.5 Shugendō: power verified by practice

Shugendō (修験道) resists definition, and Sekimori identifies definition as the field's central unresolved problem.[^35] Miyake Hitoshi treats it as a composite religion drawing on folk religion, mountain beliefs, shamanism and magic, kami cults, esoteric Buddhism, and Chinese divinatory traditions.[^36] Gorai Shigeru's rival interpretation, as Sekimori summarises it, understands Shugendō as a religion of practice, miracles, and faith that acquired theory and doctrine only after losing its miraculous power; Sekimori notes the attendant risk of romanticisation.[^37] Institutionally, Shugendō was banned by government decree in 1872, and the small minority of practitioners who entered Buddhist institutions were initially forbidden their distinctive robes and rituals.[^38] For the present argument, Shugendō is the clearest case in which cultivation is verified as _efficacy_ (_gen_, 験) and that efficacy is expended on behalf of a community.

### 4.6 _Tama_ and the purified heart

In the kami idiom, power is spirit (_tama_) rather than _ki_. The Kokugakuin glossary describes _chinkon_ (鎮魂, spirit-pacification) as a rite to keep the soul from leaving the body, and a fourfold spirit (_shikon_, 四魂) comprising a ruling aspect (_aramitama_), a harmonising aspect (_nigimitama_), a transforming aspect (_kushimitama_), and a blessing aspect (_sakimitama_).[^39] The corresponding virtue of the heart is _seimei_ (清明), purity and brightness of heart, called _akaki kiyoki kokoro_ (明き清き心) and understood as the inner dimension of purification (_harae_, 祓).[^40] Here cultivation proceeds less by accumulation than by **removal**. Sawada shows how this idiom was reworked as nineteenth-century personal cultivation in the purification rituals devised by Inoue Masakane (1790–1849).[^41] Stein notes that in the early twentieth century the Ōmoto movement's _chinkon kishin_ "democratised" spirit practices previously reserved for specialists such as _yamabushi_ and shrine maidens.[^42]

## 5. The Modern Synthesis: _Shūyō_ and Psycho-Spiritual Therapy

### 5.1 The formation of _shūyō_ discourse

Kurita argues that _shūyō_ emerged as a conceptual category with religious undertones, shaped by the debates over religion and education instigated by Inoue Tetsujirō, and that it came to transcend the boundary between "ethics" and "religion."[^43] Its first modern appearance is in Nakamura Masanao's 1871 translation of Samuel Smiles's _Self-Help_.[^44] Kurita distinguishes three phases up to about 1905 — a _shūyō_ of the independent mind, a _shūyō_ of the virtuous mind, and a phase in which Buddhists entered the discourse and practices such as zazen and introspection became methods of ethical-religious union — with the popular "_shūyō_ boom" following.[^45] Nishihira adds that late Meiji _shūyō_ was oriented primarily toward usefulness to the nation rather than the care of _ki_.[^29]

### 5.2 Zen, introspection, and quiet sitting

Several strands converged in this discourse. At Shaku Sōen's suggestion, his lay student Suzuki Daisetsu wrote 『静坐のすすめ』(_Seiza no susume_, A Recommendation for Quiet Sitting, 1899), which offered zazen for non-religious ends such as concentration, moral cultivation, and mental relaxation, justified by physiology and psychology and paralleled in Christian and Confucian practice.[^46] Sōen (1860–1919) was the Engakuji abbot under whom Sōseki sat in 1894–95.[^47] Katō Totsudō's 『冥想論』(_Meisōron_, On Meditation, 1905) grounded quiet sitting in psychology and physiology rather than in Hakuin's _Yasenkanna_, and recommended it for the sick.[^48]

Kiyozawa Manshi (1863–1903) made introspection (_naikan_) the core of _shūyō_. In his account, honest self-reflection exposes the limits of one's ethical capacity, and at that limit faith in the absolute infinite arises.[^49] Nishihira reads Kiyozawa's _shūyō_ as functionally closer to _shugyō_: a disciplined turn from self-power to other-power.[^50] Body-mind methods outside any religious institution followed, among them Okada Torajirō's (1872–1920) quiet-sitting method, Fujita Reisai's method of harmonising breath and mind, and Nitobe Inazō's recommendations for "silencing thought" (_mokushi_, 黙思) in 『修養』(_Shūyō_, 1911).[^51]

### 5.3 _Seishin ryōhō_ and Usui Reiki Ryōhō

^bcf396

==The same decades produced thousands of \*seishin ryōhō\* (精神療法, psycho-spiritual therapies) and \*reijutsu\* (霊術, spirit techniques). Stein describes practitioners who used embodied methods such as abdominal breathing to cultivate an invisible healing power transmitted through the hands, comparable to North American "mind cure."[^52] Hirano Naoko and Yoshinaga Shin'ichi have documented the metaphysical and transnational sources of these therapie==s.[^53]

Usui Reiki Ryōhō, systematised in 1920s Tokyo by Usui Mikao (1865–1926), exemplifies the modern synthesis of merit, energy, and _kokoro_. Four features stand out.

1. **Merit.** The memorial erected by Usui's society at Saihōji in 1927 is titled 『霊法肇祖臼井先生功徳之碑』(_Reihō chōso Usui sensei kudoku no hi_, Memorial of the Merit of Usui-sensei, Founder of the Spiritual Method).[^54] In a widely circulated English translation, its opening glosses the compound: virtue (_toku_) is what is realised through cultivation and training, and merit (_kō_) is the spreading and practice of a method of guidance and relief.[^55] The classical Buddhist pairing is thus redefined in the vocabulary of _shūyō_.
2. **Cultivation of _kokoro_.** Stein identifies a clear legacy of _shūyō_ in the memorial's description of the practice as correcting the heart-mind through innate mysterious abilities, and in the therapies' posited causal link between the state of _kokoro_ and bodily health.[^56]
3. **Precepts.** The five precepts (_gokai_, 五戒), attributed to Usui in a signed calligraphy, derive, according to Stein, from a recitation text by the earlier therapist Suzuki Bizan, whose _Kenzen no genri_ (1914) synthesised Neo-Confucian self-cultivation with Christian Science and New Thought.[^57] The calligraphy reads 招福の秘法　萬病の靈薬　今日丈けは　怒るな　心配すな　感謝して　業をはげめ　人に親切に — "the secret method of inviting happiness, the miraculous medicine for all illness: today only, do not anger, do not worry, be grateful, fulfil your work, be kind to others." The memorial instructs that the precepts be recited morning and evening in _seiza gasshō_ (静坐合掌), so as to engrave them in the heart-mind (_kokoro ni nen-zeshimu_).[^58] It is worth noting, as an observation rather than a claim of influence, that the first two prohibitions echo Ekken's _yōjō_ counsel against anger and fear (§4.2).
4. **_Rei_ and _kokoro_.** Stein observes that while several contemporary new religions, notably Ōmoto, replaced _kokoro_ with _rei_ as their central term, Usui Reiki Ryōhō retained an explicit relation between _reiki_ and _kokoro_.[^59] ==The ritual transmission called \*reiju\* has been argued to derive from esoteric Buddhist consecration (\*kanjō\*), although Stein is sceptical of Jojan Jonker's proposal of a more direct link to \*chinkon kishin\*.==[^60]

### 5.4 The politics of cultivation

Cultivation discourse was never politically neutral. Nishihira argues that Nitobe's _shūyō_ functioned to fix poorly educated young people in their stations and to turn rural youth and married women toward national duty.[^61] A recent study traces Katō Totsudō's reformulation of _shūyō_ in the Taishō era as "constitutional cultivation" (_rikkenteki shūyō_), responding to the legacy of the Meiji Emperor and to public preoccupation with _kokutai_.[^62] Tsutsui Kiyotada interprets popular _shūyō-shugi_ as a Japanese form of the "capitalist spirit."[^63] Usui Reiki Ryōhō's leadership was dominated for half a century by Imperial Navy officers, and recitation of the Meiji Emperor's poetry (_gyosei_) was foundational to its practice.[^64]

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 Field, register, medium

![[_ Dojo/Practice/Kokoro/Sources/01 Raw/Kokoro/Kokoro - Cultivation - Circular Model.svg]]

_Figure 2._ Circular model. _Kokoro_ at the centre; merit and energy as the two halves of the inner ring; traditions positioned on the side of their idiom, with internal critiques dashed; Meiji–Taishō _shūyō_ as the outer boundary through which the idioms pass into modern forms. Author's schema.

The survey supports the first claim. Where cultivation is imagined as accumulation, _kokoro_ is the **register** of merit and the origin of the intention that directs it (_ekō_). Where cultivation is imagined as circulation, _kokoro_ is the **medium** of _ki_, grace, or spirit — to be settled in the belly (Hakuin), stilled as a reflecting surface (Kūkai), or purified of what dims it (_akaki kiyoki kokoro_). In both idioms it is the **field** worked by practice. The modern therapies explicitly unify the roles: the Usui memorial treats merit, innate spiritual ability, and a corrected heart-mind as a single achievement.

### 6.2 Critique as relocation

The second claim concerns the reflexive traditions. Bodhidharma's refusal, Shinran's reversal, Dōgen's enactment, and Hakuin's diagnosis are often read as rejections of merit-making or energy-work. The evidence suggests relocation instead: merit moves from the practitioner's account to Amida (Shinran) or into the act itself (Dōgen); vital energy is not renounced but redirected through labour in activity (Hakuin). Kiyozawa's modern _shūyō_ reproduces this pattern, making self-cultivation the disciplined route to its own negation.

### 6.3 Methodological cautions

Three cautions follow. First, **romanticisation**: Yamada Shōji's study of Eugen Herrigel's _Zen in the Art of Archery_ shows how an influential image of Zen in the arts rested on misunderstanding between teacher and student, and Sekimori flags a comparable nostalgia in Gorai's reading of Shugendō.[^65] Second, **categories**: Stein, drawing on Shimazono Susumu and William Bodiford, notes that "religion" and "spiritual" lack consistent definitions, and that groups such as the Usui society occupy an ambiguous space that is neither organised religion nor simply secular.[^66] Third, **efficacy**: claims about _ki_, _kaji_, or _reiki_ are historical data about how practitioners understood cultivation, not findings this essay endorses or refutes.

### 6.4 Contemporary frameworks

Academic research continues to treat _kokoro_ as an integrative category. Kyoto University's Kokoro Research Center, founded in 2007, retained the untranslated term in its English name and combined neuroscience, Buddhist studies, clinical psychology, and aesthetics; it was reorganised in 2022 as the Institute for the Future of Human Society.[^67] In philosophy, Robert Carter reads the Japanese arts as self-cultivation, and Nishihira brings _shūyō_ into dialogue with Pierre Hadot's "spiritual exercises" and Michel Foucault's "care of the self."[^68]

## 7. Implications for Reading _Kokoro_ (Working Hypotheses)

> **Note.** This section offers hypotheses for the vault's _Kokoro_ project. It is not a claim from the literature.

_Kokoro_ appeared in 1914, amid the _shūyō_ boom described above and two decades after its author's retreat under Shaku Sōen. Four readings follow.

**K and the limits of self-power.** K's maxim — 「精神的に向上心のないものは馬鹿だ」(_seishinteki ni kōjōshin no nai mono wa baka da_, "anyone without spiritual aspiration is a fool") — is recognisably the idiom of late Meiji cultivation, and Sensei later turns it back on K in their rivalry.[^69] K was born into a Jōdo Shin temple, the tradition in which self-generated merit is precisely what cannot save. His _shōjin_ (精進, diligent striving) and self-denial therefore constitute a self-power cultivation pursued against his inheritance. Read with Kiyozawa, K's catastrophe marks the failure of the turn from self-power to other-power; read with Hakuin, it resembles a _zenbyō_ of the whole person.

**Sensei and arrested circulation.** Sensei lives on inherited capital, does no work, and largely withdraws from society. Against Ahn's account of Edo cultivation, in which vital energy must move through labour or be exhausted, Sensei figures an arrested circulation.

**Two economies.** The uncle's embezzlement of Sensei's inheritance aligns money with the destruction of trust. The _hōtoku_ ethic offered an alternative economy in which virtue is a debt repaid through sincere labour. Sensei appears stranded between a monetary economy he despises and a moral economy he can no longer inhabit.

**The testament as _ekō_.** Sensei frames his confession as a transfer: he will open his heart so that its blood reaches the narrator, satisfied if new life can lodge in the young man's breast when his own heart stops.[^69] Structurally, this resembles merit-dedication or _kaji_ — a life turned over to another — performed in secular, written form. Shizu remains outside this circuit of transmission.

---

## 8. Conclusion

Japanese traditions have not agreed on what _kokoro_ is, but they have repeatedly described its cultivation in two idioms: merit that accrues and can be turned toward others, and energy that circulates and can be received. The most reflexive voices — Bodhidharma's refusal, Shinran's reversal, Dōgen's enactment, Hakuin's diagnosis — did not dissolve these idioms so much as relocate them. The late Meiji and Taishō discourse of _shūyō_ then gathered both into an ethics of character and health that claimed to stand outside religion while drawing on it at every point, a synthesis legible in a memorial that commemorates a healer's _kudoku_ and instructs that precepts be engraved in the heart-mind. For the study of Sōseki's novel, this history suggests that the book's title names not only an interior to be understood but a site of cultivation whose modern vocabularies the novel inherits and tests.
