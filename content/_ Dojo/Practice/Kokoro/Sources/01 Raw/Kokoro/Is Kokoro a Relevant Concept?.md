# **The Concept of _Kokoro_ and Its Contemporary Relevance**

_Kokoro_ is a central Japanese term often translated as heart, mind, or soul, but it points to a broader, unified inner life that resists simple English equivalents. Across humanities, psychology, religion, and education, it remains an active, organizing concept rather than a historical curiosity.

## Meanings and Origins

**Polysemous core:** _Kokoro_ covers emotions, thoughts, will, spirit, and inner meaning, blurring the Western heart/mind split (Benedict, 2024; Hearn, 2021; Yamaguchi, 2024; Myers, 1985). In ancient Japanese poetry, it originally denoted **affection and feeling**, a “place of feeling” tied to bodily sensations and oriented toward others (Sasaki, 2010).

**Cultural key word:** It functions as a **Japanese cultural key term**, frequently used in sayings, song lyrics, and book titles, with no single foreign equivalent (Nakaya, 2019; Yamaguchi, 2018; Numata, 2021).

## Contemporary Applications and Debates

**Psychology and care:** _Kokoro_ underpins hospice “spiritual care,” disaster mental health (_kokoro no kea_), and indigenous therapies informed by Zen and interdependent selfhood (Benedict, 2024; Sherlock, 2015; Yamaguchi, 2024). Ambiguity in the term both enables wide use and complicates stigma reduction and help‑seeking (Sherlock, 2015; Yamaguchi, 2024).

**Ethics and education:** In moral education materials (_Kokoro no nōto_), it encodes valued traits like proper inner attitude toward self, others, nature, and nation, almost exclusively in positive terms (Nakaya, 2019).

**Humanities and identity:** Literature, philosophy, and cultural studies use _kokoro_ to explore identity, trauma, ecological connection, and tensions between traditional values and modernization (Hearn, 2021; Suizu, 2024; Nakamura, 2012; Gu, 2023; Yurt, 2025; Nae, 2002).

## Summary

_Kokoro_ is highly relevant today: it is a flexible, culturally dense way of talking about inner life, character, and relational selfhood. It structures discourse in education, spiritual and mental health care, science–religion dialogue, and literary and philosophical reflection, precisely because it unifies what many Western terms keep separate.
