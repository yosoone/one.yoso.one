# Intro

Unite in _kokoro_ (心), a connection at the level of truth – the truth that mind, matter, heart, and spirit are not separate territories but a single, dynamic, interwoven consciousness. It is the quiet power of meeting, in oneself, and being to being, in a set state of synchronicities, aware of an ever-evolving layered reality.

# Heart, mind, and spirit

The Japanese concept of _kokoro_ (心) defies simple translation, encapsulating a dynamic unity of body, heart, and mind that has shaped Japan’s cultural landscapes. Kokoro operates as an integrative force that combines physical, emotional, and cognitive domains by uniting the notions of heart, mind, and spirit as being indivisible from one another.

Semantically, it covers the thinking, spiritual, and emotional element of a person and is key to referring to their inner world, and an essential bridge that governs communities, and our interaction with reality.

One of the challenges in discussing _kokoro_ (心) in English is that linking words like "and", such as heart & mind & spirit, imply divisions that do not exist when considering _kokoro_ (心). Within kokoro might reveal a desire for union beyond division of self or other.

Japanese dictionaries provide further insight. The Kōjien dictionary defines _kokoro_ (心) as "the source of/prerequisite for a person’s mental processes, or mental processes themselves," encompassing the complex of knowledge, feeling, and will. The Nihon kokugo daijiten defines it as "the organ controlling rational (intellectual) and mental (emotional) processes in a human being, or these processes themselves," often used in opposition to body (karada - 体) and thing (mono - 物), and metaphorically to describe things or phenomena corresponding to the person’s soul/mind/heart. Synonyms listed include seishin (精神 - 'soul/psyche') and tamasii (魂 - soul/spirit/mind/willpower; vitality').

In modern Japanese, native speakers tend to collocate _kokoro_ (心) with the verb 'feel' (kanjiru - 感じる) and 'head' (atama - 頭) with the verb 'think' (kangaeru - 考える), suggesting that the Japanese 'feel with kokoro and think with head'. Japanese researcher Nakano Shigeru notes that "kokoro leans more toward the emotional sense of heart rather than the ‘rational’ sense of mind". However, in scholarly discourse such as psychology and philosophy, _kokoro_ (心) functions as an equivalent for 'mind', defined as a complex of feeling, thinking, reasoning, remembering, will, and interest.

When translated into English, _kokoro_ (心) has various equivalents depending on the context, including heart, feeling, emotions, mentality, psyche, soul, mind, the way people feel, spirit, psychology, way of thinking, sentiment(s), personality, thought, attention, will, and mood.

In the discourse of modern Japanese moral education (kokoro no kyōiku), _kokoro_ (心) is a key word. The Ministry of Education's study materials, Kokoro no nōto, aim to foster a "rich kokoro" (yutaka na kokoro) in students, which encompasses introspection, kindness, being touched by beauty, and altruism. The textbooks encourage the development of a "beautiful kokoro" (utsukushii kokoro). In this context, _kokoro_ (心) refers to the individual’s inner state, their attitude towards themselves, others, nature, and their community, as well as their character and personality.

It's also important to note that unlike the Chinese word xin, _kokoro_ (心) does not include the meaning of the heart as a physical organ, which is exclusively referred to by the Sino-Japanese word shinzō (心臓) Overall, kokoro represents a holistic understanding of the inner self, encompassing cognitive, emotional, and spiritual dimensions in a way that is deeply embedded in Japanese culture and thought.

To deeper understand the ideal of _kokoro_ (心) , One also might consider The principle of wa (和 - harmony, peace), which emphasize group cohesion, consensus‑building, and avoidance of open conflict which promotes mutual respect, indirect communication, and a preference for collective well‑being over individual assertion.
