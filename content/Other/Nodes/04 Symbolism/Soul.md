[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c8/Schiavonetti_Soul_leaving_body_1808.jpg/220px-Schiavonetti_Soul_leaving_body_1808.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Schiavonetti_Soul_leaving_body_1808.jpg)

Human soul leaving the body.

In many [religious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion "Religion") and [philosophical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy "Philosophy") traditions, the **soul** is the [spiritual essence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirit_\(vital_essence\) "Spirit (vital essence)") of a person, which includes one's [identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_\(social_science\) "Identity (social science)"), [personality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personality "Personality"), and [memories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory "Memory"), an immaterial aspect or essence of a [living being](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Living_being "Living being") that is believed to be able to survive [physical death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_death "Physical death"). The concept of the soul is generally applied to humans, though it can also be applied to other living or even non-living entities, as in [animism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animism "Animism").[[1]]\(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul#cite\_note-1)

## Etymology

The Modern English noun _[soul](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/soul "wikt:soul")_ is derived from [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English "Old English") _sāwol, sāwel_. The earliest attestations reported in the _[Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary "Oxford English Dictionary")_ are from the 8th century. In [King Alfred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Alfred "King Alfred")'s translation of _[De Consolatione Philosophiae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Consolatione_Philosophiae "De Consolatione Philosophiae")_, it is used to refer to the immaterial, spiritual, or thinking aspect of a person, as contrasted with the person's physical body; in the [Vespasian Psalter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vespasian_Psalter "Vespasian Psalter") 77.50, it means "life" or "animate existence".

The Old English word is cognate with other historical [Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_languages "Germanic languages") terms for the same idea, including [Old Frisian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Frisian "Old Frisian") _sēle, sēl_ (which could also mean "salvation", or "solemn oath"), [Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_language "Gothic language") _saiwala_, [Old High German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_High_German "Old High German") _sēula, sēla_, [Old Saxon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Saxon "Old Saxon") _sēola_, and [Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse "Old Norse") _sāla_. Present-day cognates include Dutch _ziel_ and German _Seele_.[[250324]]\(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul#cite\_note-oed-2)

The concept of the soul is deeply rooted in various religious, spiritual, and philosophical traditions. Different cultures and belief systems offer diverse perspectives on what the soul is and its significance. Here are a few common views:

1. **Religious Perspective:**

   - **Christianity:** In Christianity, the soul is often considered the eternal and immortal essence of an individual. It is believed to be created by God and possesses qualities such as consciousness, self-awareness, and the capacity for moral judgment. The fate of the soul after death is a significant aspect of Christian theology, with beliefs in concepts like heaven, hell, and purgatory.

   - **Islam:** Islam also teaches the existence of the soul, referred to as "ruh" or "nafs." The soul is seen as a creation of God, and its ultimate destination is determined by one's deeds in life. Islam includes beliefs in an afterlife, where the soul is judged and rewarded or punished accordingly.

   - **Hinduism:** Hinduism views the soul as eternal and unchanging, referred to as "Atman." It is considered distinct from the physical body and is believed to undergo a cycle of birth, death, and rebirth (reincarnation) until it achieves liberation (moksha).

   - **Buddhism:** Buddhism, while rejecting the idea of an eternal, unchanging soul, acknowledges the concept of consciousness and the interconnectedness of all living beings. The goal in Buddhism is to attain enlightenment and break the cycle of rebirth and suffering (samsara).

2. **Philosophical Perspective:**

   - **Plato:** In ancient Greek philosophy, Plato proposed a dualistic view of the soul. He believed in a tripartite soul consisting of reason, spirit, and desire. The soul, for Plato, is immortal and preexists before birth.

   - **Aristotle:** Aristotle, on the other hand, had a more integrated view of the soul. He saw the soul as the animating principle of the body, responsible for its functions. Aristotle did not posit the immortality of the soul in the same way as Plato.

3. **Modern and Metaphorical Use:**

   - In modern contexts, some people use the term "soul" in a more metaphorical sense to refer to the innermost essence of an individual, encompassing emotions, personality, and the core of one's identity.

In summary, the concept of the soul is multifaceted and varies across different religious, cultural, and philosophical traditions. It often involves notions of immortality, consciousness, and a deeper, eternal aspect of human existence.
