# Everything Flows

_On the fluid nature of reality, the body as living water, and practice as the art of yielding to what moves through._

---

The ancient Greek philosopher Heraclitus left behind only fragments — yet one endures above all: _panta rhei_, everything flows.¹ He meant it literally. Fire, earth, river — nothing holds its form. The same water that gives the river its name never occupies the same position twice. This was not poetic licence but ontological claim: becoming is more real than being. What we call solid objects are, at every scale, processes in slow disguise.

Modern physics has arrived at the same shore by a different vessel. At the quantum level, particles do not possess fixed locations until measured — they exist as probability distributions, smeared across possibility.² What we perceive as matter is overwhelmingly empty space, held in shape by electromagnetic fields that are themselves patterns of oscillation. The table, the hand resting on it, the breath moving through the chest — all of it is vibration within a larger vibration. Form is a standing wave. Reality is less noun than verb.

## The body as ocean

The human body reflects this directly. By mass, we are roughly seventy percent water — an ocean carried upright.³ But deeper than percentage: the cytoplasm of every cell maintains a fluid dynamics of extraordinary complexity. Ions flow across membranes, proteins fold and unfold, fascia transmits tensile force through the whole organism like water through a network of living channels. The late biologist Mae-Wan Ho described the organism as a quantum coherent whole — not a machine assembled from parts, but a field of coordinated activity in which each element is simultaneously local and global, each wave part of one sea.⁴

Traditional frameworks arrived at this understanding along different rivers. The Taoist concept of _qi_ — often translated as breath, vital energy, or life force — was always already a fluid metaphor. The Tao Te Ching, in its most literal rendering, describes the highest good as water: it benefits ten thousand things, seeks the low place, and does not contend.⁵ In Ayurvedic medicine, _prana_ moves through the subtle body as current moves through water — blocked by tension, freed by opening. The Sanskrit root of the word _nadi_ (the subtle channels of the body) means simply: flow.⁶

> The highest good is like water. Water gives life to the ten thousand things and does not strive.
> — Tao Te Ching, Chapter 8 (trans. S. Mitchell, Harper Perennial, 1988)

## Attention as current

What does a practice built on this understanding look like? It looks, perhaps, like water finding its way downhill — responsive to what is, moving through and around rather than over. The psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi spent decades studying what he named the flow state: a mode of consciousness in which the boundary between self and action dissolves, attention becomes total, and effort feels effortless.⁷ Athletes, musicians, surgeons, artists — those who enter this state describe something less like doing and more like being moved through. The practitioner becomes the riverbed; the action becomes the river.

This is not metaphor alone. Neurologically, flow states are characterised by a phenomenon called transient hypofrontality — a temporary downregulation of the prefrontal cortex, the seat of self-monitoring, comparison, and doubt.⁸ The inner commentator grows quiet. What remains is pure, unmediated responsiveness. In Zen, this condition is called _mushin_ — mind without obstruction, the original mind prior to conceptual overlays. In Patanjali's Yoga Sutras, _samadhi_ is described as the state in which the instrument of perception becomes transparent to the object perceived — the seer, seeing, and seen dissolve into a single movement.⁹

## Practice as ceremony

To practice is to return, again and again, to the conditions in which this fluidity becomes possible. This is why the ceremony matters — not because ritual is superstition, but because deliberate form creates a container. The empty bowl holds the water. Breathwork slows the nervous system, shifts dominance from sympathetic activation to parasympathetic rest, and creates the physiological conditions for permeability.¹⁰ Reiki, understood as the intentional transmission of ki (the Japanese cognate of qi), operates in the register of subtle body attunement — the practitioner as tuning fork, not pump. Movement, whether the precise geometry of Shaolin or the slow unfurl of a brush across paper, trains the body to remember what it already knows: that its most natural mode is flow.

The anthropologist Victor Turner wrote of _liminality_ — the threshold state in ritual space where the structures of ordinary identity loosen and something new becomes possible.¹¹ Ceremony, in this reading, is technology for liquefaction: it softens the fixed into the permeable, if only for the duration of the rite. Every tattooing session held as ceremony, every meditation period opened with breath and intention, every martial sequence practised until it disappears into the body — these are returns to the liquid state, the original condition beneath the crust of habit.

## Environment as field

We are not rivers in a landscape. We are rivers made of the landscape. The emerging science of embodied cognition holds that cognition is inseparable from the body's interaction with its environment — mind is not housed in the skull but distributed through the whole system of organism and world.¹² We think with our hands, with our feet on the ground, with the particular quality of light in the room. The forest changes the brain. The ocean changes the breath. The dojo, shaped with intention, becomes a condition of practice — not merely its setting.

Shintoism has always known this. The kami — the sacred presences — inhabit rivers, mountains, trees, and stones. Not symbolically, but really: the world is ensouled, and careful attention reveals this. To practice in a specific place, season after season, is to enter into relationship with that place's own intelligence. The practitioner does not import the sacred into a neutral container. They recognise what was already there, and attune.

> Entering a sacred grove, one begins to feel the presence of the kami long before conscious thought names it so.
> — Motoori Norinaga, on the nature of _mono no aware_ and sacred place (18th c.)

## The gift of impermanence

The Buddhist teaching of _anicca_ — impermanence — is often received as loss. Everything changes; nothing lasts; grief is the consequence. But there is another reading, and it is older. Impermanence is the very condition of aliveness. A river that stopped flowing would cease to be a river. A body that resisted change would calcify. The teaching of impermanence is, at its root, an invitation into trust: trust that the current knows the way, that yielding to change is the deepest form of wisdom, that the self is not a possession to be defended but a temporary eddy in a vast flow that precedes and exceeds it.

The physicist Carlo Rovelli, writing at the intersection of quantum mechanics and philosophy, arrives at a near-identical conclusion: the world is made of events, not things; of happenings, not substances; of processes rather than objects.¹³ What we name as stable is always a pattern of relationship sustained across time — not fixed, but coherent. The body, the self, the practice, the world: all of it is liquid, all of it is luminous, all of it flowing through and as the one undivided movement of reality expressing itself.

To practice is to remember this. To teach it is to make a space in which others may remember too.
