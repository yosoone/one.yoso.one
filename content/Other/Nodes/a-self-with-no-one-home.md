# A Self With No One Home: Uberization and the Shared Identity

_The collection’s underside. Everything in the other six pieces described identity becoming a record on infrastructure you do not own. This is what that looks like at the very bottom — where the architecture is subverted and exploited at the same time._

---

## What is actually happening

Across rideshare and delivery platforms, a black market in **verified accounts** has formed.

- The Tech Transparency Project identified roughly **80 Facebook groups**, combined membership over **800,000**, trading driver accounts for Uber, DoorDash, and similar apps — many openly named, many aimed at Spanish speakers and likely at undocumented immigrants.
- Accounts can be **bought or rented online without providing identification**, at rates reported around \*\*$115 every 30 days** to rent, or a few hundred dollars to buy outright (other reports cite $300–500/month).
- The simplest version is not even a market: one family member with the **right to work** sets up the account, and relatives who arrive later **share it** — often splitting a vehicle, the insurance, and the account all at once.

The platforms know. Uber points to **“Real-Time ID Check,”** which uses selfies to confirm the person working is the one who passed screening. In the UK, Uber Eats has blocked hundreds of rider accounts a month under a Home Office agreement, targeting abuse of the legal **“substitute”** system. A permanent arms race runs between verification and circumvention.

_(Framings differ sharply: some lawmakers treat this as a fraud-and-safety problem; others describe it as survival labor produced by a system that offers no legal door. Both describe the same machine from opposite sides.)_

---

## 1. The gate manufactures the workaround

This is the **“notability” gate** — made flesh.

Platform verification (SSN, background check, selfie) exists to bind one identity to one person. But a gate does not eliminate the people who cannot pass it; it creates a **market in passing**.

- The undocumented worker is **“non-notable”** to the system — legible enough to be excluded, not enough to be served.
- So the scarce thing becomes the **verified identity itself**, and a black market prices it.

> The system designed to fuse identity-to-person produces, as its own shadow, the industrial **decoupling** of the two.

---

## 2. Identity decoupled from the person

The apotheosis of **possession → permission.**

When the _permission_ (a verified account) holds the value, and the human behind it is interchangeable, the account stops describing a worker and becomes a **rentable asset** — worth more than the labor it gates.

|                 |Old form          |This exploit                     |
|-----------------|------------------|---------------------------------|
|What has value   |The worker’s labor|The verified account             |
|The human        |The point         |Interchangeable, behind the shell|
|Documented status|A fact about you  |**Capital** — rent-bearing       |

A new class line runs straight through the identity layer:

> Those the system can see become **rentiers of recognition**; those it cannot become the **tenants of someone else’s selfhood.**

---

## 3. The “we” hiding inside the “I”

The bleak inversion of the surveillance dream.

The whole stack aspires to **one-person-one-verified-identity** — total legibility, the panopticon that knows exactly who is acting. Impose that on people the system refuses to recognize, and it does not produce order. It produces:

> A single digital ghost worn by many bodies.

The platform sees one continuous, verified individual making deliveries. The reality is a rotating crew sharing one shell. The legible identity becomes a **collective vessel**. The system’s hunger to see _one_ person creates, at the margins, a hidden _many_ — the precise opposite of its design.

---

## 4. Both resistance and the deepest subjugation

It is tempting to read this as the little guy outwitting the machine — and there is truth in it: humans pooling their exclusion to improvise a shared existence the gate denied them.

But it is also the **most rightless labor** in the economy:

- no recourse, no insurance under their own name, no protection
- deactivation in an instant, with no identity to appeal from
- the platform receives the labor while being able to **disclaim the laborer**

> The body is deniable. That deniability is the structural point.

---

## The closing image

Everything across these threads has been about identity becoming a record on infrastructure you do not own. The shared-account worker is what that looks like at the very bottom: a person so far outside the permission system that the only way to exist economically is to **disappear into someone else’s record** — to become labor without an identity, behind an identity without a body.

Uberization promised to reduce the worker to an account and a rating. For the undocumented, it delivered exactly that — and then revealed the punchline:

> Once the worker _is_ just the account, the account no longer needs to be one worker at all.

The dream of perfect identity, pushed to its edge, produces its own negation:

> **A self with no one home, and a crowd living inside it.**
