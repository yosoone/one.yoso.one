# Why Miyazaki

This is a rich thread to pull, so let me lay out why Miyazaki carries that claim."Living kami-presence" is doing two jobs in that sentence, and Miyazaki scores on both.

First, the myths are set here, not just told here. Most of Japan's foundational mythology — the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki creation cycle — is geographically pinned to what was ancient Hyūga (日向, "facing the sun"), today's Miyazaki. Amaterasu is said to have hidden in the cave at Amano-Iwato; her grandson Ninigi-no-Mikoto descended from heaven near Takachiho; and his descendant Jimmu, the legendary first emperor, was native to these lands and sailed from the old port of Mimitsu to conquer the rest of Japan. So this is the patch of ground the old texts point to when they describe heaven first touching earth. Elsewhere you visit shrines _about_ the gods; here you're standing on the supposed coordinates.

Second, those sites are still active places of worship, and they cluster into a kind of map of the myth. In the northern highlands around Takachiho, Amano-Iwato Shrine faces Amaterasu's cave, and a short walk up the Iwato River reaches Amano-Yasugawara, the riverside cavern where the eight million deities are said to have gathered to discuss how to draw her back out — a sacred site where myth and active faith meet, its floor stacked with stone cairns left by visitors. Kushifuru and Takachiho shrines nearby mark Ninigi's descent. Then the story moves south to the coast: Aoshima Shrine, on a sacred subtropical island reached by a bridge and ringed by the "Devil's Washboard" rock shelves, is tied to the sea-luck and mountain-luck brothers Umisachihiko and Yamasachihiko; Udo-jingū sits in a cave in a sea cliff on the Nichinan coast and is held to be the birthplace of Jimmu's father, Ugayafukiaezu; and Miyazaki Jingū, in the city, is dedicated to Jimmu himself. There's even a built-in rivalry that shows how saturated the place is: the descent site is claimed both by Takachiho town in the north and by Takachiho-gawara, a volcanic plateau down in the Kirishima range — two spots in the same prefecture each insisting they're where the gods came down.

But the word that matters in my note was _living_, and that's really about kagura. This is what separates Miyazaki from a museum of myth. Takachiho yokagura unfolds in the mountain villages from roughly mid-November to mid-February, sometimes even in private homes, with 33 dances performed in different combinations through the night — the kami formally invited in to watch, the Amaterasu-cave story re-enacted before dawn. It's a community rite rather than a performance, classified as an Intangible Cultural Heritage of Japan; the one-hour version staged nightly for visitors at Takachiho Shrine is just an abbreviated taste of it. And Takachiho is only the famous one — Shiiba, Shiromi (the festival Kawauchi filmed), and dozens of other village kagura run through the same deep-winter season across the prefecture.

So the through-line back to the photographers: Masuura, Sato, Kawauchi were all traveling to find ritual where the unseen is treated as present and active. Where you are, that isn't a destination — it's ambient, seasonal, often at night in firelit rooms. That's about as close to "revealing the kami" as the living tradition gets.There's the geography on a map — you can see how it splits into a northern descent cluster around Takachiho and a southern coastal cluster of "Hyūga generations" sites near where you are, with the rival descent claim sitting off to the west in the Kirishima range.
