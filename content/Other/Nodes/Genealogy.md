Tracing your family history—often called genealogy—is definitely possible, and there are several approaches you can take depending on how deep you want to go and what resources you have access to.

**1. Start with what you know**\
Begin by gathering information from family members. Interview parents, grandparents, aunts, uncles, and cousins. Write down names, birth dates, marriage dates, places of residence, occupations, and any stories they remember. Even small details (like a hometown or a church affiliation) can become valuable clues later.

**2. Organize the data**\
Create a simple family tree chart or use free software (e.g., Gramps, FamilySearch Tree, or MyHeritage’s free tier). Keeping everything in a structured format helps you spot gaps and avoid duplicate entries.

**3. Public records and archives**

- **Civil registration**: Birth, marriage, and death certificates are often held by local civil registries. In many countries these become publicly accessible after a certain number of years.
- **Census records**: Periodic censuses list household members, ages, occupations, and sometimes birthplace. Many national archives digitize these records and make them searchable.
- **Church/parish registers**: Baptisms, marriages, and burials were traditionally recorded by churches. These can be especially useful for periods before civil registration existed.
- **Immigration and naturalization documents**: Passenger lists, visa applications, and naturalization papers often contain birthplaces, family members’ names, and previous residences.

Many of these records are now searchable online through national archive portals or subscription services.

**4. Online genealogy platforms**\
There are several well‑established websites that aggregate historical records and let you build and share trees:

|Platform|Free Tier|Notable Features|
|---|---|---|
|**FamilySearch** (run by the LDS Church)|Completely free|Massive collection of digitized records worldwide; collaborative family trees.|
|**Ancestry.com**|Limited free trial|Extensive U.S. and international record collections; DNA testing integration.|
|**MyHeritage**|Free basic plan|Strong European record coverage; DNA matching.|
|**Findmypast**|Free trial|Focus on UK and Irish records.|

These services often allow you to upload your own documents (photos of old letters, certificates, etc.) and connect with distant relatives who have already added overlapping ancestors.

**5. DNA testing (optional but powerful)**\
Consumer DNA kits (e.g., from AncestryDNA, 23andMe, MyHeritage DNA, or FamilyTreeDNA) can reveal genetic matches you wouldn’t otherwise find. When combined with traditional documentary research, DNA results can confirm or refute suspected relationships. Keep in mind privacy considerations—review each provider’s data‑use policies before submitting a sample.

**6. Local resources**

- **Libraries and historical societies**: Many hold microfilm copies of newspapers, city directories, and local histories.
- **Courts and land registries**: Deeds, wills, and probate records can illuminate property ownership and familial links.
- **Military records**: Draft registrations, service files, and pension applications often list next‑of‑kin and birthplaces.

**7. Verify and cite**\
Whenever you add a new piece of information, note its source (e.g., “1902 birth certificate, County Registry”). This practice prevents errors from propagating and makes it easier for others (or future you) to assess the reliability of each fact.

**8. Share and collaborate**\
Genealogy thrives on collaboration. Consider publishing parts of your tree on public platforms, joining surname‑specific forums, or participating in social media groups dedicated to the region or era you’re researching. Others may have already done legwork that saves you time.

### Quick starter checklist

1. **Interview family** – Record oral histories.
2. **Collect existing documents** – Photos of certificates, letters, diaries.
3. **Create a basic tree** – Use free software or paper.
4. **Search online databases** – Begin with FamilySearch (free).
5. **Visit local archives** – Request copies of vital records.
6. **Consider DNA testing** – If you want genetic confirmation.
7. **Document sources** – Keep a bibliography for each fact.
8. **Connect with others** – Join genealogy forums or local societies.

By following these steps, you’ll gradually fill in the branches of your family tree and uncover the stories that shaped your ancestors’ lives.
