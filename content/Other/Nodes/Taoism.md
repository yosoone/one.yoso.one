# Tao Te Ching

## 47

From the _Tao Te Ching_ by Laozi, Chapter 47:

---

**Without going outside, you may know the whole world.**
**Without looking through the window, you may see the ways of heaven.**
**The farther you go, the less you know.**

Thus the sage knows without traveling;
He sees without looking;
He works without doing.

---

This chapter captures a central Taoist idea: **inner stillness as the path to understanding**. True knowledge doesn’t come from external accumulation or restless seeking — it arises from turning inward and aligning with the Tao.

The paradox “the farther you go, the less you know” suggests that chasing outward experience fragments the mind and pulls it away from the unified, quiet awareness that perceives things as they truly are.

The closing line echoes _wu wei_ (non-action) — the sage accomplishes everything precisely by not forcing, not straining, not interfering.​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​

### Ref Claude

The _Tao Te Ching_ is attributed to **Laozi** (also spelled Lao Tzu / Lao-Tze), a semi-legendary Chinese philosopher traditionally dated to around the **6th–4th century BCE**.

A few honest caveats:

- **The text I quoted is a paraphrase/composite**, not a word-for-word rendering from a specific named translator. I should have been upfront about that.
- There are **dozens of English translations**, and they vary significantly. Well-known ones include versions by **D.C. Lau**, **Stephen Mitchell**, **Ursula K. Le Guin**, **Gia-Fu Feng**, and **James Legge**, among many others.
- The original is in **Classical Chinese**, so every English version is an interpretation as much as a translation.

If you want a specific authoritative version, I’d recommend:

- **D.C. Lau** (Penguin Classics) — scholarly and precise
- **Ursula K. Le Guin** — poetic and philosophical
- **Stephen Mitchell** — accessible and lyrical

### TAOISM

Taoism (pronounced dow-ism) is an ancient philosophy indigenous to China. It emphasises living in harmony with Nature. Taoism is based on the teachings of Lao Tzu contained in theTao Te Ching. As the story goes, Lao Tzu was a wise man who lived in the city of Chengzhou (known today as Luoyang). Lao Tzu grew tired of the moral decay in Chengzhou, and predicted the kingdom’s decline. He decided to venture west to live as a hermit in the unsettled frontier at the age of 80. At the western gate of the city, he was recognized by one of the guards, who, recognising this wise old man, asked the master to record his wisdom.
