# What Shapes Our Emotions

Emotions are shaped by several layers working together — none alone is sufficient.

## Biological / physiological layer

Your nervous system and body chemistry set the baseline. Hormones, sleep, hunger, and the vagus nerve's state (calm vs. activated) all shift what's available to feel before any "meaning" enters the picture.[^1] This is why the same event lands differently depending on whether you're rested or depleted.

## Appraisal — the meaning you assign

It's not the event itself but your interpretation of it — what it means for you, whether you can handle it — that determines the emotion.[^2][^3] Two people in the identical situation can feel fear and excitement respectively, because they've appraised the same arousal differently.

## Conditioning and memory

Emotions get wired to specific associations over time — a smell, a tone of voice, a piece of music can trigger a feeling because it's been linked, often unconsciously, to past experience.[^4] This is largely why emotional reactions can feel disproportionate to the present moment; they're often responding to a stored pattern, not just what's in front of you.

## Social and cultural shaping

What counts as an appropriate emotion, how intensely to express it, even which emotions a culture has _words_ for — all of this is learned.[^5] Some cultures elaborate grief; others elaborate shame or honor. The raw physiological arousal may be universal, but the interpretive frame around it is taught.

## Attention and practice

Contemplative traditions (Buddhist, in particular) treat emotion as something whose _arising and passing_ can be observed rather than simply undergone.[^6] Sustained meditation practice doesn't eliminate emotional response, but it changes the relationship to it — creating space between stimulus and reaction, which is itself a way of reshaping what the emotion becomes once it's noticed rather than just felt.

## In the context of Shinsei

The "soft edges rooted in inner awareness" language suggests painting as a way of registering what an emotion feels like _before_ appraisal solidifies it into a story. That places the work closer to affect theory[^7] — or more recently, constructionist models of emotion[^8] — than to any Western Romantic idea of emotion as raw, pre-formed truth waiting to be expressed.
