The short answer is that **there isn’t a single “months‑to‑replace‑everything” figure**, because the cells in our bodies turn over at very different rates. Some cells are replaced in a matter of hours, others persist for decades, and a few—like many neurons—essentially last a lifetime.

Below is a concise overview of the major cell‑type groups and how long they typically take to renew:

|Cell type (or tissue)|Approximate turnover time\*|Typical lifespan|
|---|---|---|
|**Blood cells** (red blood cells, platelets, most white‑blood cells)|Days to weeks|RBCs ≈ 120 days; platelets ≈ 7–10 days; neutrophils ≈ 1 day|
|**Skin epidermal cells**|2–4 weeks|Outer layers shed continuously|
|**Gut lining (intestinal epithelium)**|3–5 days|Rapid renewal to cope with harsh environment|
|**Liver hepatocytes**|150–300 days|Regenerate efficiently after injury|
|**Muscle fibers (skeletal)**|Months to years|Satellite cells repair damage; most fibers persist for many years|
|**Bone cells (osteoblasts/osteoclasts)**|3–6 months (remodeling cycle)|Continuous remodeling throughout life|
|**Adipocytes (fat cells)**|8–10 years (average)|Turnover is relatively slow|
|**Cardiac muscle cells**|10 years + (very low turnover)|Limited regeneration capacity|
|**Neurons (central nervous system)**|Essentially **lifetime**|Most do not divide after development|
|**Glial cells (astrocytes, oligodendrocytes)**|2–5 years (varies)|Some capacity for replacement|
|**Endothelial cells (blood vessels)**|2–3 years|Keep vasculature functional|

\*These numbers are averages drawn from scientific literature; individual variation and methodological differences can shift them somewhat.

### Putting it together

If you try to compute a single “overall replacement time” by averaging across all cell types, you end up with a rough ballpark of **about 7–10 years** for the majority of the body’s cellular mass to be turned over. Converting that to months gives **≈ 84–120 months**.

However, this aggregate figure masks the reality that:

- **A large fraction of cells (e.g., red blood cells, gut epithelium, skin) are refreshed within weeks.**
- **Some tissues (brain neurons, cardiac muscle) change very little after early adulthood.**
- **Other tissues (bone, adipose, certain glial cells) sit somewhere in the middle, renewing over several years.**

So, while you could say “the whole body’s cells are completely renewed roughly every 7–10 years,” it’s more accurate to think of the body as a mosaic of constantly renewing and long‑lasting components rather than a single, uniform turnover clock.

### TL;DR

- **No single month count applies**—cell turnover varies widely.
- **Most cells are replaced within a few weeks to a few years.**
- **On average, the entire cellular complement of an adult human turns over roughly every 7–10 years (≈ 84–120 months).**
