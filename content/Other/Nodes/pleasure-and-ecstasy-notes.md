# Pleasure and Ecstasy: Research Notes

Pure research across Buddhist, Hindu, Sufi, Christian, shamanic, and philosophical sources, alongside the affective neuroscience and psychology of pleasure and self-transcendence. Compiled as reference material — not tied to a specific project.

## Buddhism: piti and sukha, the two pleasures of jhana

The Pali canon distinguishes two positive feeling-tones that arise in deep meditative absorption (_jhana_): _piti_ and _sukha_. Piti is the more forceful of the two — commonly rendered as rapture, bliss, or even ecstasy, and described as a "tangible" feeling that can register as physical intensity: electricity, goosebumps, a humming or flooding sensation, sometimes disorienting in its force.[^1] Sukha is milder and steadier — a contented, pervasive ease that is often glossed as happiness or comfort, and stands as the direct opposite of _dukkha_ (suffering).[^2] Classic formulations distinguish them functionally: piti is the excitement of getting a desired thing, sukha is the actual savoring of it once had.[^3]

Scholastically, piti is not classified as a feeling (_vedana_) at all but as "joyful interest," meaning it can accompany wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral states of mind — it is an energetic quality of engagement rather than a hedonic tone in the strict sense.[^4] Contemporary Buddhist scholarship has also pushed back on the older view that jhanic joy and pleasure are merely incidental byproducts of concentration with no bearing on liberation. Some scholars argue piti and sukha are actively liberative: by giving the mind a taste of pleasure that doesn't depend on sensual objects, they loosen the deep-seated pull toward sense-desire (_kama_) that Buddhist psychology treats as a root cause of suffering.[^5] In other words, the pleasure of jhana isn't opposed to the path — it's part of how the path works, offering the mind a wholesome pleasure compelling enough to make craving's usual objects loosen their grip.

## Hinduism: ananda as the nature of reality itself

Vedanta's _sat-chit-ananda_ — being, consciousness, bliss — names the three inseparable qualities of Brahman, ultimate reality.[^6] Ananda is often popularly glossed as bliss or joy, but the more rigorous Advaita Vedanta reading pushes further: ==ananda is not an experience or an emotional state at all, but \*limitlessness\* — the fullness that comes from being unbound by any boundary. On this reading, confusing ananda with an ecstatic feeling is actually a category error; feelings arise and pass, whereas ananda names something that simply \*is\*, prior to and independent of experience==.[^7] Devotional (theistic) Vedanta schools read the same term differently: for the Vaishnava traditions, sat-chit-ananda names ==the very substance of a personal God's form, not an abstract, formless absolute== — the Brahma Samhita calls the supreme Lord _sac-cid-ananda-vigraha_, "==one whose very body is being, consciousness, and bliss==."[^8] The tension between these two readings — bliss as ontological fullness versus bliss as the very substance of divine personhood — runs through the whole tradition without ever fully resolving, and both readings still coexist as live options within Hindu thought today.[^9]

## Kashmir Shaivism: aesthetic wonder as a path to bliss

A useful bridge from art to ecstasy comes from the 10th–11th century Kashmiri philosopher Abhinavagupta, the great systematizer of the non-dual Trika Shaiva tradition and, separately, the definitive theorist of Sanskrit dramatic and poetic aesthetics (_rasa_ theory).[^10] For Abhinavagupta the two projects are one: he holds that the aesthetic delight a spectator feels in art — which he names _camatkara_, a flash of wonder in which the mind lights up and rests in its own delight — ==is not merely analogous to spiritual realization but structurally identical to it==.[^11] Ordinary emotion is bound to a personal history and a personal ego; ==rasa (aesthetic "flavor") arises only once a felt emotion is lifted out of that personal bondage and universalized, so that the spectator's own ego dissolves for the duration of the experience==.[^12] That dissolution mirrors, for Abhinavagupta, the recognition (_pratyabhijna_) at the heart of Kashmir Shaivism: ==the momentary loss of the bounded self in aesthetic wonder previews the same loss of the bounded self in spiritual recognition of one's identity with Shiva, universal consciousness.==[^13] ==This gives ananda a startlingly direct doctrinal link to art-making: a well-made painting or performance is, on this view, not just a symbol of bliss but an actual (if brief) technology for producing the same self-dissolving wonder that mysticism aims at by other means.==

## Sufism: wajd, fana, and the vocabulary of ecstatic states

Sufi mystical vocabulary is unusually granular about ecstatic experience, distinguishing several related but distinct states (_ahwal_, plural of _hal_): _wajd_ ("finding" or ecstasy proper) is described as a sensation that unexpectedly overtakes the heart, producing effects ranging from sorrow to joy depending on which aspect of the Divine is momentarily disclosed.[^14] It is often triggered by _dhikr_ (remembrance of God through repeated invocation) or _sama_ — the practice, most associated with the Mevlevi order of whirling dervishes, of using rhythmic music, chanting, and movement to induce a state where the self's ordinary boundaries give way.[^15] _Fana_ names something more total: ==the "annihilation" or "passing away" of the individual ego before God==, sometimes described by the phrase "==die before you die==."[^16] Traditions differ sharply on what fana actually means — some hold it is the total, literal ==union of the soul with the One== (a position associated with the martyred mystic Al-Hallaj), while ==more moderate voices insist the self is never literally identical with God, only obliterated in its sense of separateness, after which the mystic returns to ordinary life transformed== (a stage called _baqa_, "subsistence" or "remaining").[^17] Later Sufi terminology adds _sukr_ ("intoxication"), a state of being half-dazed by nearness to God without full loss of ordinary awareness, distinguishing a spectrum of ecstatic intensity rather than a single undifferentiated peak.[^18]

## Christian mysticism: rapture as wound

Teresa of Ávila's account of the _transverberation_ — an angel piercing her heart with a flaming golden dart, an experience she had repeatedly over some days in the late 1550s — became the paradigmatic Christian image of mystical ecstasy, immortalized in Bernini's 1647–52 sculpture in Rome's Cornaro Chapel.[^19] Teresa describes the pain of the piercing and the sweetness of the divine love it left behind as inseparable, and the sculpture itself has long been read as deliberately ambiguous between spiritual and erotic rapture — her head thrown back, eyes closed, mouth open, body slack.[^20] This is not incidental. Later psychological research into the phenomenology of mystical reports found that the language used to describe erotic and mystical peak experiences overlaps closely, particularly for women describing both in receptive rather than agentive terms — a finding read as evidence that culturally available imagery of surrender and being acted-upon shapes how both kinds of experience get articulated, whichever is actually occurring.[^21] Teresa's own theological framework treats the ecstasy not as an end in itself but as one stage on a longer ascent toward "spiritual marriage" — full, stable union with God described in her _Interior Castle_ as the innermost of several concentric mansions of the soul.[^22]

## Shamanism: ecstasy as technique, not accident

Historian of religion Mircea Eliade's foundational 1951 survey defines shamanism specifically by the shaman's _use_ of ecstasy as a controllable technique — the shaman is distinguished from a priest or ordinary practitioner by specializing in a trance during which the soul is believed to leave the body and travel to other cosmic realms (the sky, the underworld) on behalf of the community.[^23] Eliade's subtitle, "archaic techniques of ecstasy," is a deliberate framing choice: ecstasy in this context is not passive rapture that happens to someone, but a skill developed through initiation, often via serious illness or psychological crisis that the shaman must "cure" in themselves before being able to guide others.[^24] Drumming, in particular, is treated across many traditions surveyed by Eliade as functioning almost as a vehicle: a rhythmic technology that carries the practitioner to the symbolic center of the world (often imagined as a cosmic tree or axis) from which passage to other realms becomes possible.[^25] This framing — ecstasy as a trained, repeatable, cross-culturally attested technique rather than a rare grace — sits in productive tension with the Christian and Sufi material above, where ecstasy tends to be described as something given rather than made.

## Georges Bataille: eroticism as the wound in individual selfhood

The French philosopher Georges Bataille offers a secular, and deliberately transgressive, theory of ecstasy built around the concepts of continuity and discontinuity. Ordinary existence, for Bataille, is _discontinuous_: each person is a bounded, isolated unit, sealed off from every other person by the fact of being a separate consciousness in a separate body.[^26] ==Eroticism, death, and sacred ritual all share a single function in his account: each offers a route — violent, transgressive, and always temporary — back toward \*continuity\*, the state in which those boundaries dissolve and beings are no longer separate==.[^27] Sex approached purely as reproduction stays within the discontinuous, functional world; ==eroticism proper begins only where an act deliberately breaches a taboo== and, in doing so, offers a glimpse of the more fundamental continuity that taboos exist to hide from ordinary view.[^28] This is why Bataille treats mystical ecstasy and erotic ecstasy as structurally the same event wearing different clothes — both are ==limit-experiences in which a bounded self is momentarily undone==, and both, on his reading, function as a kind of "sacred" rupture within an otherwise rule-bound, productive, discontinuous social order.[^29]

## Psychology: ego dissolution as the common thread

Two twentieth-century psychological frameworks converge on the same core observation the traditions above make independently: whatever else is going on, ==ecstatic and blissful peak states involve some loosening or dissolution of the ordinary sense of a bounded self==.

William James's _The Varieties of Religious Experience_ (1902) proposed four defining marks of mystical states, based on a wide survey of first-person religious testimony: **ineffability** (the experience defies adequate description in words), **noetic quality** (it is felt as a state of direct knowledge or insight, not merely a feeling), **transiency** (it cannot be sustained for long), and **passivity** (the subject feels grasped or acted upon by something greater than their own will, even when the state was voluntarily induced).[^30] James treated the near-universality of this pattern across religious traditions as itself significant — evidence, in his reading, that mystical states tap into some consistent structure of human consciousness rather than being artifacts of any one doctrine.

Abraham Maslow's mid-century concept of the _peak experience_ describes moments — often triggered by art, nature, love, or creative work — in which a person feels simultaneously more fully themselves and paradoxically merged with, or dissolved into, something larger than themselves: "==the creator becomes one with his work being created.=="[^31] Maslow initially treated peak experiences as a marker of self-actualization, but later revised his hierarchy of needs to add _self-transcendence_ above self-actualization, explicitly borrowing from Eastern traditions the idea that ==the highest human development moves toward ego-transcendence and obliteration of self-consciousness, not merely toward a stronger, more integrated self.==[^32] Reported features overlap closely with James's marks: unity with one's surroundings, dissolution of ordinary time-sense, ineffability, and an intense, often humbling sense of good fortune rather than achievement.[^33]

## Neuroscience: pleasure, wanting, and the quiet self

**Pleasure itself.** The most influential contemporary model of hedonic pleasure comes from Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson's decades of work distinguishing "liking" from "wanting" as two separable brain processes. Contrary to popular belief, dopamine — long assumed to be the brain's "pleasure chemical" — does not appear to generate the actual sensory pleasure of a reward at all; it drives "wanting," the motivational pull toward a reward, including cases (such as nicotine addiction) where something is intensely wanted despite producing little or no pleasure.[^34] Actual hedonic "liking" is instead generated by small, specific "hedonic hotspots" — tiny regions in the nucleus accumbens, ventral pallidum, and a few cortical areas — where opioid and endocannabinoid (not dopamine) neurotransmission amplifies the felt pleasantness of a reward, measurable even in the facial reactions of non-verbal animals.[^35] This liking/wanting split has direct bearing on any discussion of pleasure and ecstasy: intense craving or pursuit of a state is a different brain process from the actual felt pleasure of that state, and the two can come apart.

**Ecstatic and mystical states.** Andrew Newberg's neuroimaging work (SPECT, PET, and fMRI) on Tibetan Buddhist meditators, praying Franciscan nuns, and chanting Sikhs found a consistent pattern: ==decreased blood flow to the posterior superior parietal lobe — a region Newberg calls the "orientation association area," normally responsible for maintaining a felt sense of the body's boundary in space==.[^36] The proposed mechanism is _deafferentation_: sustained meditative or prayerful focus cuts off the normal flow of orienting sensory information to this region, and its resulting quieting is hypothesized to underlie ==the felt loss of a bounded self and sense of unity with something larger== — a mechanism some researchers argue applies uniformly across the nondual, "introvertive" mystical experience described in very different doctrinal terms by different traditions.[^37] Newberg is explicit that this correlational finding cannot settle the metaphysical question of whether such states register a genuine transcendent reality or are purely generated by the brain — the scan shows that something is happening neurologically, not what that something ultimately means.[^38]

**Psychedelics.** Roland Griffiths's research group at Johns Hopkins has run the most rigorous modern studies of chemically-occasioned mystical experience, using psilocybin under controlled, supportive conditions with the Mystical Experience Questionnaire (MEQ) — a validated instrument descended from the same phenomenological tradition as James's four marks.[^39] Their landmark 2006 study found that a single high-dose psilocybin session could reliably occasion an experience meeting full criteria for a "complete" mystical experience, and that participants rated the experience, at a two-month follow-up, among the most personally and spiritually meaningful experiences of their lives — comparable to the birth of a child.[^40] Later work found the depth of this mystical-type experience (not simply the drug's other perceptual effects) statistically mediates the durable positive changes in mood, life satisfaction, and pro-social behavior observed months later — suggesting ==the ecstatic, self-dissolving quality of the experience, rather than psychedelic perception generally, is doing the psychological work.==[^41]

## Questions to carry forward

Where does a given passage sit on the liking/wanting axis — is it capturing the ache of desire, or the stillness of having arrived?

Is the ecstasy being depicted one that is _made_ (a technique, a practice, an aesthetic device — Eliade's shaman, Abhinavagupta's rasa) or one that is _given_ (a grace, an unbidden visitation — Teresa's transverberation, Sufi wajd)?

Does a passage picture ananda as a felt peak (an emotional high point) or as ground-state fullness (Vedanta's stricter reading, closer to Newberg's quieted orientation-sense than to a climax)?

Where is the boundary being dissolved — self and other (Bataille), self and artwork (Abhinavagupta), self and the sacred (James, Sufism), or self and pure awareness (Advaita, jhana)?
