# The Economy of Genocide

_How plunder, forced labor, and the competition for resources turn mass murder into a kind of business — and what the historical, economic, and moral record reveals about it._

Mass atrocity tends to arrive dressed in the language of ideology, ethnicity, and ancient hatred. Yet underneath the rhetoric of purity and survival there usually runs a ledger. Genocide moves property, redistributes land, supplies labor, and rewards participants. A growing body of scholarship across economics, history, demography, and moral theology now treats this material dimension as central to how mass killing begins, sustains itself, and leaves its mark on the generations that follow.

## Why Economists Study Mass Murder

For much of the twentieth century, the study of genocide belonged mainly to historians, lawyers, and political scientists, and economists arrived comparatively late. The landmark intervention came in 2016 with _Economic Aspects of Genocides, Other Mass Atrocities, and Their Prevention_, an Oxford University Press volume edited by Charles H. Anderton and Jurgen Brauer that gathered forty-one scholars to apply economic reasoning to the subject (Anderton and Brauer 2016). Their organizing claim is bracing — rational, strategic behavior pervades mass atrocity. Architects of violence calculate, ordinary participants weigh incentives, and bystanders respond to costs and rewards. Treating perpetrators as coldly purposeful, rather than simply mad, opens a path toward prediction and prevention.

The volume ranges across cases from California's Yana people and Aboriginal Australians to Stalin's killing of Ukrainians, the Holocaust, Rwanda, and the Democratic Republic of Congo. A recurring finding concerns the deliberate, strategic use of violence by atrocity architects, who often manipulate the ordinary instincts of ordinary people to draw them into killing (Anderton and Brauer 2016). The economic lens supplements moral and historical accounts, and it earns its place by explaining mechanisms that ideology alone leaves obscure.

## How Rational Calculation Drives Atrocity

The economic approach rests on a deceptively simple model of choice under constraint. People pursue goals — wealth, status, safety, land — within limits set by resources, law, and the actions of others. Genocide enters this picture as one option among several, chosen when its expected benefits to the decision-makers exceed its expected costs. Anderton's chapters formalize this through constrained-optimization and game-theoretic models, in which the timing and intensity of mass killing track shifting incentives (Anderton and Brauer 2016).

Microeconomic incentives prove decisive at the individual level. The chance to loot a neighbor's home, seize a shop, or claim a plot of farmland supplies a private motive that ideology alone would struggle to generate. Survival pressures push in the same direction, since participation often buys protection while abstention invites suspicion. Macroeconomic conditions shape the broader field — low growth, sudden economic shocks, and collapsing living standards all raise the measured risk of mass violence, because scarcity sharpens competition and desperation lowers the perceived cost of radical action (Anderton and Brauer 2016).

Behavioral economics adds texture. Atrocity architects exploit predictable features of human psychology — conformity, obedience, loss aversion, and the pull of the in-group — to convert hesitant citizens into active killers. This blend of cold strategy at the top and manipulated instinct at the bottom helps explain how shocking outcomes emerge from the choices of otherwise ordinary people.

## Plunder as Policy and the Seizure of Wealth

Among the most consistent features of genocide is the transfer of victims' property to perpetrators and beneficiaries. The killing and the confiscation operate as two faces of a single process.

The Armenian Genocide of 1915 offers an early, systematic example. In _Confiscation and Destruction_, historians Uğur Ümit Üngör and Mehmet Polatel document how the Young Turk regime turned the dispossession of Ottoman Armenians into state policy, building a legal and bureaucratic apparatus to sequester homes, businesses, churches, and land (Üngör and Polatel 2011). The authors situate this within an ideology of economic nationalism — a drive to construct a Turkish "national economy" by transferring commercial life from Armenian and other minority hands into Muslim Turkish ones. Property became a prize and a motive, and competition over the spoils reached from the cabinet down to the village, with institutions and individuals alike contending to keep what they had seized (Üngör and Polatel 2011).

The Holocaust shows the same logic at an industrial scale. The "Aryanization" of Jewish property — the forced transfer of businesses, real estate, art, savings, and household goods — preceded and accompanied deportation and murder. Across occupied Europe the German state organized plunder on a continental scale and channeled the proceeds homeward; Götz Aly, whose argument the later section examines, marshals financial records to show how thoroughly theft was woven into the fabric of the regime (Aly 2007).

## Forced Labor and the Machinery of Production

Beyond seizing what victims owned, genocidal regimes extracted what victims could produce. Forced and slave labor turned human beings into a depletable industrial input.

Adam Tooze's economic history of the Third Reich, _The Wages of Destruction_, traces how the Nazi war economy came to rely on coerced labor drawn from occupied territories and concentration camps, with workers held in brutal conditions and many of them worked to death (Tooze 2006). The chemical conglomerate IG Farben built a vast plant beside Auschwitz and drew on camp prisoners as labor, while it also manufactured the poison used in the gas chambers — a grim fusion of production and extermination (Tooze 2006). The Nazi euphemism for this practice, "extermination through labor" (_Vernichtung durch Arbeit_), captured a calculus in which a prisoner's life was measured in months of output before death.

Tooze's larger contribution is to place these horrors inside a coherent, if doomed, economic vision. He reads Hitler's strategy as a bid to assemble a continental super-state capable of rivaling American economic power, a project whose escalating resource demands shaped the pace of conquest and the logic of mass murder (Tooze 2006). Treating the camp system and the war economy as parts of one machine reveals the cold accounting that ran alongside the ideological frenzy.

## Scarcity, Land, and Ecological Panic

Where the Holocaust and the Armenian Genocide highlight plunder and labor, the Rwandan genocide of 1994 has drawn economists toward land, demography, and scarcity.

Rwanda was one of Africa's most densely populated countries, its rural majority dependent on shrinking family plots. In an influential 1998 study, Catherine André and Jean-Philippe Platteau described a society "caught in the Malthusian trap," in which intense population pressure on finite land bred conflict within and between families (André and Platteau 1998). Their data pointed to a disturbing pattern, since the upheaval offered some households a chance to settle scores and consolidate land, and the victims at times included Hutu landholders as well as Tutsi.

Philip Verwimp pursued this through micro-level evidence. In an economic profile of peasant perpetrators, he examined how landholding and household economics related to participation in the genocide, and his broader work links Rwanda's coffee economy, dictatorship, and mass killing (Verwimp 2005). With Damien de Walque he later mapped the demographic and socio-economic distribution of the dead, finding that educated and urban Rwandans faced elevated mortality, which left the country poorer in human capital for decades afterward (de Walque and Verwimp 2010). Such demographic work — alongside the broader study of the demography of genocide — supplies the quantitative backbone of the field.

The historian Timothy Snyder widens the frame in _Black Earth_, arguing that the Holocaust grew from what he calls "ecological panic," a conviction that survival demanded the seizure of land and food from others (Snyder 2015). On his reading, Hitler regarded the fertile black earth of Ukraine as living space, _Lebensraum_, that Germans needed to match American abundance, and the destruction of states became the precondition for mass killing (Snyder 2015). Snyder presents this as a warning for a century facing fresh anxieties over food and water. His thesis has drawn vigorous challenge, with critics arguing that he overstates the ecological reading of Hitler and stretches the contemporary parallel (Andrews 2016). The dispute itself is instructive, marking the boundary where economic and ecological explanation meets ideology.

## How Plunder Buys Popular Consent

A central puzzle of genocide is participation and acquiescence on a mass scale. Why do ordinary citizens support, tolerate, or join in? One influential answer is material — the regime shares the spoils.

Götz Aly advances this thesis forcefully in _Hitler's Beneficiaries_. Drawing on tax and financial records, he argues that the Nazi state effectively bought the loyalty of ordinary Germans through a vast campaign of theft, channeling plundered wealth into welfare, tax relief, and a rising standard of living (Aly 2007). Soldiers shipped home goods and valuables from occupied lands; the state engineered favorable terms of trade that left occupied populations hungry while Germans enjoyed coffee, salmon, and consumer comforts; and the dispossession of Jews flowed directly into German households (Aly 2007). In Aly's account, material gratification dissolved moral qualms and bound the population to the regime's crimes.

The same mechanism appears in the Armenian case. Üngör and Polatel observe that the Young Turk government secured domestic loyalty through the redistribution of confiscated Armenian wealth, widening the circle of those with a stake in the outcome (Üngör and Polatel 2011). Complicity, on this view, spreads through profit.

Aly's argument has provoked sharp and productive debate, which strengthens rather than weakens the field. Richard J. Evans praised the attention to material factors yet judged that Aly exaggerates their weight and slights the independent force of ideology, offering only half the picture (Evans 2007). Adam Tooze likewise published a pointed critique of Aly's interpretation (Tooze 2006b). The mainstream synthesis holds both truths together — ideology furnished the goals and the targets, while material incentives lubricated mass participation. Genocide, in this reckoning, is at once a crime of belief and a business.

## The Spiritual and Moral Dimension — Persons Reduced to Things

The economic study of genocide carries a moral charge that religious and philosophical traditions have long named. At its heart lies a single transformation — the reduction of persons to things, to property to be seized, labor to be consumed, and mouths to be eliminated in a competition for resources.

Many faith traditions identify the worship of wealth as a corrupting power. The Gospel warning that one master excludes the other — that a person serves God or Mammon — frames money as a force that, once enthroned, deforms the soul and the social order. The theologian Helen Alford captures the principle crisply, describing the economy as a good servant yet a harsh master, valuable at the edge of human life and ruinous once placed at its center (Yale Center for Faith and Culture 2021; Clark and Alford 2019).

Pope Francis gave this ancient concern a contemporary edge. In his 2013 apostolic exhortation _Evangelii Gaudium_, he extends the moral seriousness of the commandment that guards human life to economic arrangements, declaring that an "economy of exclusion" itself kills (Francis 2013). He pairs this with the image of a "throwaway culture" in which human beings become consumer goods to be used and then discarded, and in which people count as worthless once they cease to produce or to purchase (Francis 2013; Clark and Alford 2019). Francis writes of social systems rather than death camps, yet his diagnosis illuminates the genocidal extreme, where the logic of disposability reaches its terminal form. The peer-reviewed economics literature has engaged his critique seriously, examining how his account of waste and exclusion converses with the founding ideas of modern economics (Clark and Alford 2019).

Set beside the historical record, the spiritual claim becomes concrete. When the Young Turk state catalogued Armenian shops for transfer, when German families settled into furniture taken from murdered Jews, and when camp administrators tallied the labor value of a starving prisoner, each act enacted the same reduction the moralists warn against — a human person reclassified as an economic quantity. The moral and the material analyses converge on one truth: genocide treats people as means, and the appetite for their wealth, land, and labor makes the treatment profitable.

## The Long Economic Shadow

The economic consequences of genocide outlast the killing by generations. Mass murder destroys lives along with the human capital, institutions, and trust on which prosperity depends. Studies in the Anderton and Brauer volume measure the macroeconomic toll and trace how genocide sets back the long-run sources of economic development (Anderton and Brauer 2016). The Rwandan data make the loss tangible, since the disproportionate death of educated and urban citizens stripped the country of skills it needed to rebuild (de Walque and Verwimp 2010).

The aftermath also raises hard questions of property and justice. Seized assets often remained in the hands of perpetrator communities long after the violence ended. In the Armenian case, confiscated property was woven into the foundations of the new Turkish republic and largely stayed there, a fact that continues to fuel claims for restitution (Üngör and Polatel 2011). The same volume warns that poorly designed post-genocide settlements can entrench the vested interests created by plunder, reinforcing the exclusion of the very groups that suffered (Anderton and Brauer 2016). Recovery therefore depends on confronting the economy of the crime directly — restoring property, rebuilding human capital, and dismantling the incentives that once rewarded violence.

## What an Economic Lens Reveals

To study the economy of genocide is to resist a comforting illusion — the idea that mass murder belongs to a separate realm of pure madness, sealed off from ordinary human motives. The scholarship reviewed here, spanning Oxford's economists, historians of the Holocaust and the Armenian Genocide, demographers of Rwanda, and the moral voice of religious tradition, points to a sober conclusion. Genocide is partly a business, financed by theft, powered by coerced labor, driven by competition over land and resources, and sold to populations through a share in the spoils.

This recognition serves prevention as much as understanding. If incentives help drive atrocity, then incentives can be reshaped to discourage it — by protecting property rights, broadening economic opportunity, strengthening the states whose collapse so often precedes slaughter, and stripping the profit out of predation (Anderton and Brauer 2016). The moral traditions supply the measure of what is at stake — the dignity of the human person, which the economy of genocide systematically denies and which any decent order exists to protect. Seeing the ledger behind the violence becomes the first step toward closing it.
