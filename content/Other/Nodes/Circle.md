## Circle Symbolism

### A line that answers to a point

Euclid defines the circle through its centre:

> A _circle_ is a plane figure contained by one line such that all the straight lines falling upon it from one point among those lying within the figure equal one another.
>
> And the point is called the _center_ of the circle.[^1]

The circle is a single line, yet every part of it is measured from a point that lies off the line. Move the centre and you draw a different circle. The centre comes first; the circle follows from it.

### What the words hold

In Monier-Williams, the Sanskrit _maṇḍala_ (मण्डल) begins as an adjective: "circular, round". As a noun it is a disk, especially of the sun or moon, and then a circle, globe, orb, ring, circumference, ball or wheel. Further down the same entry it is a gathering of people: "a multitude, group, band, collection, whole body, society, company".[^2] One word holds both the figure and the people who stand in it.

The Japanese _en_ 円 (older form 圓) means round and complete. The _Nihon kokugo daijiten_ gives five senses for _ensō_ (円相, circle-form):[^3]

1. a round shape;
2. in Zen, a circle drawn as a symbol of the awakening already present in the human mind, sometimes with characters or signs inside it to show the workings of the mind or the stages of awakening;
3. in a mandala, the ring that encloses a buddha or bodhisattva;
4. in the Muromachi period, a nickname used by monks of the Kyoto Five Mountains for a string of a thousand coins, said to come from the round shape of the coins;
5. a family crest drawn as a thin circle.

So the same word covers a sacred drawing, part of a mandala, a sum of money and a crest.

### Time that returns

The circle is also a figure of time: the year, the season and the festival come round again. Mircea Eliade's _The Myth of the Eternal Return_ (English 1954), which its publisher calls a founding work of the history of religions, is the classic study of this.[^4] **Eliade's argument that archaic religions renew time by repeating the moment of creation is recalled here and has not yet been read in the book.**

## Human Sciences

### Perception: the pull of the curve

Experimental psychology has studied curves more than circles. In Bar and Neta's 2006 study, images of objects and abstract shapes were flashed for 84 milliseconds and people chose "like" or "dislike". A later review sums up the result: "Their results revealed that curved stimuli were liked more than neutral or sharp ones (Bar and Neta, 2006)." (p. 3; [pdf](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/YHPF66WY?page=3))[^5] Bar and Neta designed the study to test whether sharp contours are disliked because they signal threat. Later studies question that: some find that people are drawn towards curves rather than pushed away from angles.[^6]

The preference is older than our species. Munar and colleagues gave humans and great apes the same choice between curved and sharp-angled shapes: "Our results revealed that the human group and the great ape group indeed share a common preference for curved over sharp-angled contours, but that they differ in the manner and magnitude with which this preference is expressed behaviorally." (p. 1; [pdf](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/KX9BBQ43?page=1))[^7]

It is not universal, though. A 2022 meta-analysis of 61 studies found a reliable, medium-sized preference that changes with how long the image is shown, what kind of image it is, the viewer's expertise and the task.[^8] **(PDF not yet read; from the abstract.)**

Expertise matters for an artist. In one study the review describes, the stimuli were circles themselves: "When participants with different artistic expertise were shown a series of stimuli that consisted of a differing number of hexagons and circles, only non-expert ones found circles to be more pleasant, with experts preferring even slightly more the stimuli depicting hexagons." (p. 4; [pdf](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/YHPF66WY?page=4))[^9] The same review concludes: "All in all, these findings are a reminder that the influence of cultural factors in preference for curvature should not be overlooked." (p. 4; [pdf](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/YHPF66WY?page=4))

These studies test curved against angular contours. None of them tests the closed circle as a symbol.

### Psyche: the protective circle

C. G. Jung read the circle as protection. Writing about the terrifying vision of Brother Klaus (Nicholas of Flüe) and his long interest in a picture of a wheel, he says: "We know from experience that the protective circle, the mandala, is the traditional antidote for chaotic states of mind."[^10] In another essay: "Because of the protection it implies, the magic circle or mandala can be a form of mother archetype."[^11]

His evidence is clinical and comparative, not experimental. The same volume contains "Concerning Mandala Symbolism" (1950), with 54 figures, mainly from his own collection. It has not yet been read for this note.

### Society: the camp and the world

Émile Durkheim argued that the basic categories of thought, space among them, have social origins. His example is a circle: "There are societies in Australia and North America where space is conceived in the form of an immense circle, because the camp has a circular form; and this spatial circle is divided up exactly like the tribal circle, and is in its image."[^12] He takes the example from his and Marcel Mauss's earlier essay on primitive classification. On this reading, the circle people see in the world begins as the circle they live in.

### Understanding: the hermeneutic circle

The humanities have their own circle. To understand a text you need the whole to make sense of the parts, and the parts to make sense of the whole. Theodore George's _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_ entry puts the idea like this: "Broadly, however, the concept of the hermeneutical circle signifies that, in interpretive experience, a new understanding is achieved not on the basis of already securely founded beliefs."[^13]

It is not a loop that simply repeats: "In this, the pursuit of understanding does not build ‘higher and higher;’ it goes ‘deeper and deeper,’ gets ‘fuller and fuller,’ or, perhaps ‘richer and richer.’"[^14] The entry also notes that some within hermeneutics now doubt that interpretation is really circular. This gives [[Hermeneutic Circle]], which currently rests on Wikipedia, a citable source.

## Spirituality

### Zen: the ensō

In Zen the circle stands for awakening (see _ensō_ above). The _Shinsan Jōdoshū daijiten_ traces the _ichiensō_ (一円相, single circle) to the Chan master Huizhong 慧忠, whose students handed it on; ninety-seven kinds of circle are said to exist. It stands for true suchness (_shinnyo_ 真如) and the true nature of things (_hosshō_ 法性). In Jōdo funeral rites it is drawn in the air as the shape of the buddha-land, to bring the dead joy in the Pure Land.[^15]

An older Chan verse gives the circle its meaning. The _Xinxin ming_ 信心銘 (Inscription on Faith in Mind), attributed in the canon to Sengcan 僧璨, says of the Way:

> 圓同太虛　無欠無餘　良由取捨　所以不如[^16]

Gloss: round as great space, lacking nothing, with nothing left over; only because we grasp and reject is it not so. The character 圓 is the older form of 円.

### Buddhism: the wheel

The Buddha's first teaching is named for a wheel. At its close, the earth gods cry out:

> And when the Buddha rolled forth the Wheel of Dhamma, the earth gods raised the cry: “Near Varanasi, in the deer park at Isipatana, the Buddha has rolled forth the supreme Wheel of Dhamma. And that wheel cannot be rolled back by any ascetic or brahmin or god or Māra or divinity or by anyone in the world.”
>
> _Pavattite ca pana bhagavatā dhammacakke bhummā devā saddamanussāvesuṁ: “etaṁ bhagavatā bārāṇasiyaṁ isipatane migadāye anuttaraṁ dhammacakkaṁ pavattitaṁ appaṭivattiyaṁ samaṇena vā brāhmaṇena vā devena vā mārena vā brahmunā vā kenaci vā lokasmin”ti._[^17]

Earlier in the same discourse, the Buddha says he did not announce his awakening until his knowledge of the four truths was purified in three rounds (_tiparivaṭṭa_) and twelve aspects (SN 56.11:9.1–10.1). This wheel rolls forward and cannot be turned back. [[The Twelve Links]] sets the sutta's open chain of causes beside the commentary's closed wheel of rebirth.

In esoteric Buddhism the circle becomes the maṇḍala (see [[Mandala]]). In Japanese, the ring that encloses each buddha in a mandala is also called _ensō_.[^3]

### Shinto: the ring you walk through

At the summer purification, _nagoshi no harae_ (夏越の祓), shrines set up a _chinowa_ (茅の輪), a large ring of cogon grass (_chigaya_). It stands on the path to the shrine or in front of the worship hall, and worshippers pass through it to be cleansed of misdeeds (_tsumi_), impurity (_kegare_) and bad luck.[^18] The custom goes back to a fragment of the _Bingo fudoki_. Somin Shōrai gave Susanoo shelter for the night and was given a grass ring, which protected him from an epidemic. In ancient times the ring was worn at the waist or around the neck.[^19]

In parts of Chūgoku and Kyūshū, the last day of the sixth lunar month was also called _nagoshi_ and treated as a turning point: cattle and horses were led to rivers or the sea to graze and play.[^20]

Here the circle is a threshold. You do not look at it; you pass through it.

### Christianity: Dante's last circle

At the end of the _Paradiso_, Dante sees God as three circles, of three colours and one dimension (33.115–117). He then tries to see how a human image fits inside the circle, and compares himself to a geometer:

> Qual è ’l geomètra che tutto s’affige\
> per misurar lo cerchio, e non ritrova,\
> pensando, quel principio ond’ elli indige,
>
> tal era io a quella vista nova:\
> veder voleva come si convenne\
> l’imago al cerchio e come vi s’indova;
>
> ma non eran da ciò le proprie penne:\
> se non che la mia mente fu percossa\
> da un fulgore in che sua voglia venne.[^21]

> As the geometrician, who endeavours\
> To square the circle, and discovers not,\
> By taking thought, the principle he wants,
>
> Even such was I at that new apparition;\
> I wished to see how the image to the circle\
> Conformed itself, and how it there finds place;
>
> But my own wings were not enough for this,\
> Had it not been that then my mind there smote\
> A flash of lightning, wherein came its wish.[^22]

The Italian says only "to measure the circle"; Longfellow makes it the old problem of squaring it. Either way, the circle cannot be taken by calculation. Understanding comes as a flash, and the poem ends with Dante's will turning like an evenly moving wheel (33.142–145).

## The Arts

### Ink: Sengai

Sengai 仙厓 (1750–1837) was abbot of Shōfukuji 聖福寺 in Fukuoka. His ○△□ (_Maru sankaku shikaku_, circle, triangle, square) carries only his signature, which names him as Sengai of Shōfukuji, 「扶桑最初禅窟」 (the first Zen temple in Japan). There is no inscription to guide the reader, and the Idemitsu Museum of Arts calls it the hardest of his Zen paintings to read:

> 「○」が象徴する満月のように円満な悟道の境地に至る修行の階梯を図示したとも、この世の存在すべてを3つの図形に代表させ、「大宇宙」を小画面に凝縮させたともいわれ、その解釈には諸説があります。[^23]

In English: it is said to chart the stages of practice leading to an awakening as full as the full moon the ○ stands for, or to let three figures stand for everything that exists and condense the "great universe" into a small picture; there are many interpretations.

Sengai also made circles to be eaten. On a large ensō in the Fukuoka Art Museum he wrote, roughly, "eat this and have some tea"; the museum sees a rice cake in its rounded lower curve, and notes that the joke lands differently on those who know Zen.[^24] The Idemitsu Museum holds a similar circle and reads it as letting go even of the symbol: finishing a work only begins further practice.[^25]

In Kyoto, the main hall of Genkōan 源光庵 has two windows. The temple says the round Window of Awakening (悟りの窓) expresses Zen and _entsū_ (円通, all-pervading completeness) and stands for the great universe. The square Window of Delusion (迷いの窓) stands for human life, with its sufferings of birth, ageing, sickness and death.[^26] Sengai drew the circle beside the square; Genkōan sets them against each other.

### Paint: Kandinsky

Vasily Kandinsky painted _Several Circles_ (_Einige Kreise_) in January–February 1926: circles of many colours on a dark ground, 140.7 × 140.3 cm.[^27] The Guggenheim quotes him on why he used the circle so often:

> “If I make such frequent, vehement use of the circle in recent years, the reason (or cause) for this is not the geometric form of the circle, or its geometric properties, but my strong feeling for the inner force of the circle and its countless variations.”[^28]

**The museum does not name the source of these words; the original has not been traced.** Kandinsky puts the circle's power in what it does, not in its geometry. That is close to the ensō, and far from Euclid.

### Ground: Richard Long

Tate holds several of Richard Long's circles. _Circle of Sticks_ (1973) is a circle of wood 6.7 metres across and a centimetre high; _Slate Circle_ (1979) is made of slate.[^29] A circle on the ground is walked around, not through, and seen whole only from above.

## Open Questions

- **Centre.** Euclid defines the circle from its centre. Can a person choose their own centre, or is it found only by drawing the circle?
- **Figure or gathering.** _Maṇḍala_ means both a disk and a company of people. What does a circle of people share with a drawn circle?
- **Closed or open.** The wheel of Dhamma rolls forward; the hermeneutic circle goes deeper; Sengai's circle is eaten, and finishing it only begins practice. Is any circle ever closed?
- **Threshold or object.** The _chinowa_ is walked through, the Genkōan window looked through, Long's circles walked around. What changes when the circle is a way in rather than a thing to see?
- **The trained eye.** Non-experts preferred circles; artists slightly preferred hexagons. Where does a tattooist's or painter's eye sit, and what does that mean for the soft edges of Shinsei 心性?
- **Permanence.** A brush circle is made in one movement and cannot be corrected. What happens to an ensō when it is fixed permanently in skin?
- **Circle and square.** Sengai sets them side by side; Genkōan makes them awakening and delusion; Dante cannot square the circle. Is the square the circle's opposite, or its measure?
