## Lymph, Fascia & Flow

### What Is the Relationship?

The lymphatic system is located within the fascia. To let fluid pass through the lymph pathway, it relies on body movements — the muscles and fascia compress the vessels and push the lymph.

Lymph vessels path their way through the loose fascial layers, and lymph capillaries absorb lymph from the ground substance of the fascia — the interstitial flow of fluid between the fiber proteins in the extracellular matrix.

---

### How Lymph Is Formed

Lymph is formed when blood plasma is “squeezed” out at the beginning of the smallest blood capillaries to provide nutrition and oxygen to cells. The plasma enters the tissue and flows between cells — what is also called the ground substance in the extracellular space of the fascia, or the interstitial flow. After exchange with the cells, most fluid gets reabsorbed back into post-capillary venules, with carbon dioxide and waste products.

Interstitial fluid enters blind-ended sacs composed only of an endothelial layer tethered to the interstitial matrix. These bulbous sacs (10–60 μm in diameter) are called initial or terminal lymphatics. They possess overlapping endothelial cells that behave collectively like a valve, only permitting unidirectional entry of fluid, solute, and cells into the vessel lumen.

---

### The Role of Fascial Layers

The superficial fascia has been recognized as a specific anatomical structure between the two adipose layers — the superficial adipose tissue (SAT) and the deep adipose tissue (DAT). Knowledge about lymphatic vessels in relation to the superficial fascia has only recently begun to be described.

Lymphatic vessels are closely connected to the surrounding tissues by fine strands of reticular and elastic fibers called anchoring filaments. When interstitial pressure is low, the anchoring filaments relax, resulting in closure of the overlapping flaps. Any alteration of the superficial fascia can therefore affect these anchoring filaments, reducing their ability to drain lymph. A close relationship between lymphatic vessels and fibrous septa in the superficial adipose tissue suggests that the elasticity and organization of the fibrous matrix can improve or worsen lymphatic transport.

---

### ECM Stiffness Matters

The composition and mechanical properties of the ECM differ across the vascular tree and surrounding tissues. Tissue and vessel wall stiffening has been shown to alter endothelial behavior and contribute to vascular dysfunction in disease.

The physicochemical properties of the extracellular matrix determine the pressure and compliance of the interstitial spaces. The rate of lymphatic removal of interstitial water and protein is geared to the state of hydration of the interstitial matrix — these are important components of the “margin of safety” against edema formation.

---

### What Happens When Fascia Tightens

Healthy, supple fascia gives lymph vessels the space they need to carry away waste and filter unwanted particles. But when fascia tightens from stress, posture, or lack of hydration, it can compress lymphatic pathways, creating blockages and preventing healthy flow. In sympathetic (“fight or flight”) mode, fascia tightens, breath shortens, and lymphatic circulation struggles. In parasympathetic (“rest and digest”) mode, fascia softens and lymph moves freely.

---

### Supporting Lymph-Fascia Flow

Fascia is largely made of water. When the body becomes dehydrated, fascia can lose its elasticity and become dense or sticky. Drinking water consistently throughout the day helps keep fascia hydrated and supports fluid circulation.  Movement, breathwork, and manual therapies like myofascial release and lymphatic drainage are the primary tools for maintaining this system’s health.​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​
