# Zen Schools in Japan

Japanese Zen (禅, _zen_) is usually counted as three living schools — Rinzai, Sōtō and Ōbaku — alongside two historical lines that no longer exist as independent schools.[^1] [^4] [^11]

## Rinzai (臨済宗)

- **Origins in China.** Rinzai is one of the "five houses and seven schools" (五家七宗, _goke shichishū_) of Chinese Chan. It was founded by Linji Yixuan (臨済義玄, Rinzai Gigen; d. 866) at the Linji temple in Zhenzhou in the late Tang, and is known for brief, direct words that test the student.[^1]
- **Transmission to Japan.** Eisai (栄西) first brought Rinzai to Japan in the Kamakura period. By the end of the Muromachi period, about 24 Chinese Chan lineages had reached Japan, 21 of them Rinzai. Of these, only the Daiō line (大応派), revived in the Edo period by Hakuin Ekaku (白隠慧鶴, 1685–1768), is still transmitted today.[^1]
- **Structure today.** Rinzai has no single head temple. It is the collective name for 14 branches, each named after its head temple:[^1]
  - Nanzen-ji (南禅寺), Shōkoku-ji (相国寺), Myōshin-ji (妙心寺), Kennin-ji (建仁寺), Daitoku-ji (大徳寺), Tenryū-ji (天龍寺), Tōfuku-ji (東福寺)
  - Kenchō-ji (建長寺), Engaku-ji (円覚寺), Hōkō-ji (方広寺), Eigen-ji (永源寺), Kōgaku-ji (向嶽寺), Buttsū-ji (仏通寺), Kokutai-ji (国泰寺)
- **Coordination.** The branches work together through the joint Rinzai–Ōbaku council, the Rinnō Gōgisho (臨黄合議所).[^1]
- **Further reading.** Collcutt on the medieval Five Mountains (_gozan_) institution; Tamamura on Rinzai history.[^7] [^8]

## Sōtō (曹洞宗)

- **Two head temples.** Sōtō has two head temples of equal standing, together called the _ryōdaihonzan_ (両大本山): Eihei-ji in Fukui and Sōji-ji in Yokohama.[^2]
- **Eihei-ji (永平寺).** Founded in 1244, when Dōgen (道元) established Daibutsu-ji (大仏寺) in Echizen at the request of his patron Hatano Yoshishige; renamed Eihei-ji two years later.[^2]
- **Sōji-ji (總持寺).** Founded in 1321, when Keizan (瑩山) received the temple Morooka-dera in Noto and made it a Zen monastery. After a fire in 1898 it moved to Tsurumi, Yokohama, in 1911; the original site was rebuilt as Sōji-ji Soin.[^2]
- **Further reading.** Bodiford on Sōtō in medieval Japan.[^9]

## Ōbaku (黄檗宗)

- **Founding.** Manpuku-ji (萬福寺) in Uji was founded in 1661 by Ingen Ryūki (隠元隆琦; Yinyuan Longqi), a leading late-Ming Rinzai master from Fujian. He came to Japan in 1654 with 20 disciples and named the new temple after his own in China.[^3]
- **Independence.** First known as the Ōbaku branch of Rinzai, it became an independent school in 1876.[^3]
- **Character.** Ōbaku keeps a strongly Chinese character: sutras are chanted in a Chinese-based reading, and its ritual chant (梵唄, _bonbai_) is accompanied by drums and gongs.[^3]
- **Further reading.** Baroni on the emergence of Ōbaku in Tokugawa Japan.[^10]

## Key differences

| | Rinzai 臨済宗 | Sōtō 曹洞宗 | Ōbaku 黄檗宗 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Organisation** | 14 independent branches, no single head temple[^1] | One school, two equal head temples[^2] | One head temple, Manpuku-ji[^3] |
| **Founders** | Linji; brought by Eisai; today only Hakuin's Daiō line[^1] | Dōgen and Keizan[^2] | Ingen, 1654 / 1661[^3] |
| **Practice** | Kōan current of Dahui's Yangqi line[^1] | _Shikantaza_, "just sitting"[^13] | Zen with Pure Land devotion[^14] |
| **Sitting direction** | Facing into the hall[^12] | Facing the wall (_menpeki_ 面壁)[^12] | — |
| **Ritual** | — | — | Chinese-based chanting and _bonbai_ with instruments[^3] |

### How they sit

- **Sōtō: _shikantaza_ (只管打坐).** The Sōtō school describes zazen not as a method for reaching some further goal: the sitting posture is itself the form of the Buddha and of awakening.[^13]
- **Rinzai: the kōan current.** Rinzai inherits the kōan practice (公案禅) brought to maturity by Dahui Zonggao in the Song — one of the two great streams of Song Chan, the other being the "silent illumination" (黙照禅) of the Caodong line that Sōtō inherits.[^1]

### Which way you face

- **Sōtō faces the wall; modern Rinzai faces into the hall.**[^12]
- **It was not always so.** The Institute for Zen Studies notes that Mujaku Dōchū (無著道忠, 1653–1744), an Edo-period Rinzai scholar-monk who lived at Myōshin-ji, wrote in his monastic rule that zazen should be done facing the wall. The author reads this as a sign that facing inward had already begun by then, and concludes that the direction makes no difference to zazen itself.[^12]

### Ritual and Pure Land

- **Ōbaku keeps Chinese forms.** Its rites and sutras follow Chinese practice: sutras are chanted in a Chinese-based reading, and its melodic chant (梵唄, _bonbai_) keeps a four-beat rhythm with bells and drums. In the early to mid Edo period most of Manpuku-ji's abbots came from China.[^3]
- **Ōbaku combines Zen and Pure Land.** It brought the late-Ming practice of cultivating Zen and Pure Land together (禅浄双修), and was at times called "nembutsu Zen."[^14]

### In practice near Miyazaki

The open sittings listed in [[Zazen Near Miyazaki]] are all Rinzai, Myōshin-ji branch — expect sitting that faces into the hall.

## Historical lines

### Fuke (普化宗) — abolished

- **Origins.** Fuke traces itself to the Tang Chan monk Puhua (普化). It reached Japan in 1254 with four lay disciples who returned with the monk Kakushin (覚心). Its members, the _komusō_ (虚無僧), played the shakuhachi and travelled the country.[^4] [^5]
- **Abolition.** Abolished by government order in 1871; it survived afterwards as the Myōan, Fuke and Hōtō associations (教会, _kyōkai_).[^4]

### Nihon Darumashū (日本達磨宗) — absorbed

- An early Zen movement founded by Dainichi Nōnin (大日能忍). Its influence on Dōgen and early Sōtō is studied in Faure.[^6]

## Open questions

- The founding year of the Myōan Kyōkai after 1871 — 1888 in the _Sekai Daihyakka Jiten_ entry, 1890 on a present-day shakuhachi society's site. Not resolved.[^5]
- Temple counts for each school are left out until an official or scholarly figure is checked.
