# Zazen for Beginners: Posture, Breath, and Mind

_Speaker: Taigen Shodo Harada Roshi_
_Source: "[Zen - Introduction to zen practice / full version](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LL2XUTeoUsM)," [@OnedropzenHungary](https://www.youtube.com/@OnedropzenHungary), YouTube. Transcript cleaned and formatted._[^1] [^2]

---

I will explain the order of practice and the most important things a beginner must know to practise Zen meditation, which is called _zazen_ (座禅, seated meditation).

## The Legs

First, how to place your legs. A sitting cushion should be as comfortable as possible, and it is usually thick. Its height changes according to the condition of the practitioner.

The first way to place the legs is the **full lotus position**, in which both legs are crossed. Put your right foot onto your left inner thigh, then put your left foot onto your right inner thigh.[^3]

If you cannot do this, sit in **half lotus**. Put the right foot onto the left thigh, or the left foot onto the right thigh; either side is fine. What matters is that the lower back does not lean backwards and the back does not bend. Keep your lower back firm and centred. A straight spine is very important in this posture.

## The Hands

Once your legs are crossed, place your right hand palm up on your legs. Then place your left hand, also palm up, on top of the right. The thumbs press gently against each other and form a circle. This is the circle known from zazen.

At first, beginners often find it hard to keep the posture and align with the form, so you may also hold your hands like this _\[speaker demonstrates an alternative hand position]_. But forming the circle is the foundation. Anyone who practises zazen for a long time and grows used to it will understand the importance of this posture.

## Aligning the Spine

With your legs crossed and your hands set, tilt the lower back slightly forward, just above the tailbone, so that it does not lean backwards. This settles the spine in the centre. It feels a little like pushing your bottom backwards. In this posture, gently stretch up the lower back and the spine, and gently draw in the chin. It feels as if you were lightly raising the sky with the top of your head.

Do not lean to the right or the left with either shoulder. Keep the line of the ears and shoulders horizontal and parallel, and keep the line from the nose to the navel vertical. The abdomen and lower back need to lean slightly forward, or the posture will not be correct. With the chin drawn in and the crown gently holding up the sky, the spine becomes almost straight.

## The Eyes and the Place of Awareness

Let your gaze fall naturally along the line of the nose, about one metre ahead of you. Do not strain to stare, and do not close your eyes. We practise zazen to settle the mind, or to put the mind in order, but we do not simply sit. In everyday life our eyes are open, so zazen must be of use there too. Keep the eyes open as naturally as possible, with the view in front of you inside your field of sight.

You may rest your awareness in your left palm, where the hands meet. You can also place it about five centimetres below the navel, in the lower abdomen. Focus your awareness on one of these places. The eyes and the awareness rest in different places because the eyes stay natural while the centre of awareness is where the mind is. The place of awareness becomes the centre of zazen.

We receive the sky's _chi_, the energy of heaven, through the top of the head. We draw up the earth's chi, the energy of the earth, from below. The two unite in the lower abdomen. This teaching is the basis of zazen.

## Ordering the Mind Through the Breath

We have now put the form in order. But even with the form in order, if your mind does not calm down, you will not find rest, however long you sit. Putting the mind in order is the difficult part. It means ordering the formless mind. Those who are used to it understand this, but it is hard to grasp for those who are not.

Ordering the breath is an important tool here, because it gradually leads the mind toward order too. To order the breath, sit in zazen and let go of the forcing and tension in the whole upper body: the shoulders, the neck muscles, and the chest. Keep the upper body relaxed. The area below the ribs is especially important. This is the diaphragm, and near it is the place where the oesophagus enters the stomach. This area tenses more easily than any other, and if it does not open up, we cannot breathe deeply.

When we cannot breathe deeply, chi gets stuck in the upper body. In everyday life we think a great deal and are preoccupied with the outside world, so our chi rises easily whatever we do. After a whole life lived this way, the diaphragm and the entrance to the stomach become tense and hard, and the diaphragm no longer wants to move when we breathe. This is very common. That is why it is so important in zazen to let the breath sink to the lower abdomen and breathe fully with the diaphragm and the belly.

- **Exhalation** relaxes the body.
- **Inhalation** restores vitality: a sense of fullness, energy, and activity.

With these principles understood, breathe out as long and as slowly as possible. The body will gradually relax, and the mind will find rest too.

We often try to force the inhalation, but this is not good. As much as possible, let the body breathe in naturally.

## How to Breathe

During zazen we usually do not breathe through the mouth. We inhale and exhale through the nose. Because this is hard to see, I will demonstrate more simply using my mouth and my hand.

Exhale. When the upper body is relaxed, the belly always draws in as we breathe out. This is how the body is made. The belly draws in, and we can exhale until it cannot contract any further. On average this takes eight to 10 seconds, so aiming for an eight-second exhalation is fine. With practice, some people can exhale for 20 or 30 seconds. That is difficult until you are properly used to it, and you do not need it in everyday life anyway. If you aim for eight seconds, the mind's rest is born step by step.

When you have breathed out until the abdomen can contract no further, the body wants to return to its original state. The contracted abdomen yields to that natural pull and draws breath in. On no account should you force the breath in. The body breathes in by itself.

These are the most important elements of right breathing and right zazen for beginners. Practise by naturally extending the exhalation as long as you can, for eight or 10 seconds. Do not stop after one breath. Sit for a period of five or 10 minutes, and once you are used to that, work up to 30 minutes, then an hour. To do this, fasten your attention to the exhalation. Attention and exhalation become one, and the breath becomes orderly. As it is also said, the mind becomes orderly. This is how we practise.

## Counting the Breath

Here I should mention a method called **counting the breath**, which strengthens concentration. We count the exhalations: "One." Once the belly is fully contracted, the body breathes in fully. Then: "Two." A long inhalation. And so on: one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten. When we reach ten, we start again from one.

The main point is to count each breath with fresh, renewed energy, as if you had just sat down. Be careful not to let the counting become a routine, and keep your awareness sharp. This is very important in zazen.

Gradually the breath deepens and the count becomes regular. Better said, this peaceful state becomes a natural part of us. The mind calms down and no longer wanders into unnecessary thoughts. We could call this the **unity of body and mind**. When the body finds rest, the mind finds rest too. Just as the body sits here firmly, the mind puts down roots here, in the present.

## The Wandering Mind

There are many names for the mind, but here I will use the word "mind." It produces many kinds of thoughts: about the past, about the future, about other people, about unfamiliar things. The mind follows them and keeps jumping around. So even when we begin a piece of work that matters to us, the work has little value, because the mind keeps wandering away.

When the body is at rest, however, the mind also finds complete peace, and it calms down in a moment. When the body stands up, the mind follows it like a shadow and stands up with it. When the body walks, the mind walks with it. When we work, the mind works, and when we go about everyday life, it lives with us. Putting the mind in order means that it follows us closely in every situation of life, here and now.

When body and mind are closely linked, as when we sit like this, a great calm seems to fill us. When we work, though, we see, hear, and experience many things, and the mind becomes confused and our calm is disturbed. But once we are truly used to zazen, our state of mind is no longer pushed around so easily, whether we are working or sitting. We describe this state by saying, **"The place of practice is limitless."**[^4] There is hardly any difference between moving, working, and sitting in silence. Such is the calm mind.

## Zazen as a Way of Life

With such a mind we gain a broader perspective on our surroundings. This is one of the most important things in zazen. We do not seek only our own happiness. Instead, we look calmly at the circumstances around us, regard our environment as a companion, and remain calm and at peace. Zazen means developing this kind of humanity. Seeking only one's own happiness is not zazen. Putting the mind in order so that it becomes the foundation of one's whole life: that is zazen.

So zazen is, first, a natural, deep, and rich way of posture and breathing. It is also a form of mind in which the mind is fully linked to the state of the body in a particular place and time. I think anyone who reaches this naturally will better understand what zazen is.

With this in mind, I wish from the heart that zazen puts down deep roots in your lives.

## About the Teacher

Shodo Harada Roshi is the abbot of Sogenji (曹源寺; full name Gokokuzan Sōgen Zenji, 護国山曹源禅寺), a Zen monastery in Okayama, Japan.[^5] [^7] He is a Dharma heir of Yamada Mumon Roshi, from whom he received _inka_ (formal transmission) before entering Sogenji.[^5] [^6] He leads One Drop Zen, an international Rinzai Zen sangha with practice centres in North America, Europe, and India, including an affiliated group in Budapest, Hungary.[^5]
