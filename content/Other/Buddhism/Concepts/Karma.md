## The core idea

_Karma_ (Pāli _kamma_, Japanese _gō_ 業) means "action." In the Buddha's teaching it means one thing above all: **intentional action**. The Buddha defines it as intention (_cetanā_), which then shows itself in body, speech and mind.[^1] What follows from an action is its _vipāka_, "ripening" or "result."

Five points run through the whole tradition:

1. **Karma is the choice, not the fate.** The weight falls on the mind that acts, not on what happens to you.
2. **Results ripen, they are not handed out.** Seeds, salt in water, rocks and oil in a lake: the similes are about natural processes, not a judge.
3. **Not everything is karma.** The Buddha lists karma as one cause of experience among eight.[^2]
4. **The same act ripens differently in different minds.** A lump of salt spoils a cup of water but not a river.[^3]
5. **The aim is the end of karma, not good karma.** The fourth kind of deed leads to the ending of deeds.[^4]

## Before the Buddha

The idea that a person's deeds shape their next life appears early in the Upaniṣads. In the _Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad_, Yājñavalkya takes Ārtabhāga aside to discuss what survives death, and what they discuss is action (3.2.13); later the same text teaches that as a person acts, so he becomes (4.4.5).[^5]

Johannes Bronkhorst argues that karma and rebirth came from religious movements in India, chiefly the Jains and Buddhists, and were only later absorbed into Brahmanism, which resisted the idea for a long time.[^6] The Buddha's Jain contemporaries, as MN 101 reports them, held that all experience comes from past deeds, and that old deeds are worn away by austerity:

> ‘Everything this individual person experiences—pleasurable, painful, or neutral—is because of past deeds. So, due to eliminating past deeds by fervent mortification, and not doing any new deeds, there’s nothing to come up in the future. With no future consequence, deeds end. With the ending of deeds, suffering ends. With the ending of suffering, feeling ends. And with the ending of feeling, all suffering will have been worn away.’ Such is the doctrine of the Jain ascetics.
>
> _‘yaṁ kiñcāyaṁ purisapuggalo paṭisaṁvedeti sukhaṁ vā dukkhaṁ vā adukkhamasukhaṁ vā, sabbaṁ taṁ pubbekatahetu. Iti purāṇānaṁ kammānaṁ tapasā byantībhāvā, navānaṁ kammānaṁ akaraṇā, āyatiṁ anavassavo; āyatiṁ anavassavā kammakkhayo; kammakkhayā dukkhakkhayo; dukkhakkhayā vedanākkhayo; vedanākkhayā sabbaṁ dukkhaṁ nijjiṇṇaṁ bhavissatī’ti. Evaṁvādino, bhikkhave, nigaṇṭhā._[^7]

The Buddha's move was to relocate karma in the mind: not the physical deed, but the intention behind it (see MN 56 below).

Gananath Obeyesekere compares Indic karma with rebirth beliefs in West Africa, Melanesia, Siberia, the North American north-west coast and ancient Greece (Pythagoras, Empedocles, Pindar, Plato), and questions the assumption that India was the single source of rebirth ideas.[^8] What distinguishes the Indic systems, on his reading, is the ethical transformation named in his subtitle: rebirth made to depend on moral action.

## Early Buddhism: intention is the deed

### "It is intention that I call deeds" (AN 6.63)

The _Nibbedhika Sutta_ gives the Buddha's most compact analysis: what karma is, its source, its result, its cessation and the way to that cessation.

> It is intention that I call deeds. For after making a choice one acts by way of body, speech, and mind. And what is the source of deeds? Contact is their source. \[...] And what is the result of deeds? The result of deeds is threefold, I say: in this very life, on rebirth in the next life, or at some later time. This is called the result of deeds. And what is the cessation of deeds? When contact ceases, deeds cease. The practice that leads to the cessation of deeds is simply this noble eightfold path, that is: right view, right purpose, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right immersion.
>
> _Cetanāhaṁ, bhikkhave, kammaṁ vadāmi. Cetayitvā kammaṁ karoti— kāyena vācāya manasā. Katamo ca, bhikkhave, kammānaṁ nidānasambhavo? Phasso, bhikkhave, kammānaṁ nidānasambhavo. \[...] Katamo ca, bhikkhave, kammānaṁ vipāko? Tividhāhaṁ, bhikkhave, kammānaṁ vipākaṁ vadāmi— diṭṭheva dhamme, upapajje vā, apare vā pariyāye. Ayaṁ vuccati, bhikkhave, kammānaṁ vipāko. Katamo ca, bhikkhave, kammanirodho? Phassanirodho, bhikkhave, kammanirodho. Ayameva ariyo aṭṭhaṅgiko maggo kammanirodhagāminī paṭipadā, seyyathidaṁ— sammādiṭṭhi …pe… sammāsamādhi._[^1]

Three things stand out:

- **Contact (_phassa_) is the source.** The same link opens the proliferation chain in MN 18 (see [[Mental and Conceptual Proliferation - prapañca - papanca - Chaotic Thoughts]]). Karma begins where sense contact meets an unguarded mind.
- **Ripening has three times:** this life, the next, or later. There is no fixed schedule.
- **Karma can cease.** The path is not a way of improving one's karma but of ending it.

### The mind is the heaviest act (MN 56)

The Jain ascetic Dīgha Tapassī reports that his teacher holds the physical rod to be the most blameworthy (3.12). The Buddha reverses the order:

> “Of the three deeds thus analyzed and differentiated, which deed do you describe as being the most blameworthy for performing bad deeds: physical deeds, verbal deeds, or mental deeds?” “I describe mental deeds as being the most blameworthy for performing bad deeds, not so much physical deeds or verbal deeds.”
>
> _“Imesaṁ panāvuso gotama, tiṇṇaṁ kammānaṁ evaṁ paṭivibhattānaṁ evaṁ paṭivisiṭṭhānaṁ katamaṁ kammaṁ mahāsāvajjataraṁ paññapesi pāpassa kammassa kiriyāya pāpassa kammassa pavattiyā, yadi vā kāyakammaṁ, yadi vā vacīkammaṁ, yadi vā manokamman”ti? “Imesaṁ kho ahaṁ, tapassi, tiṇṇaṁ kammānaṁ evaṁ paṭivibhattānaṁ evaṁ paṭivisiṭṭhānaṁ manokammaṁ mahāsāvajjataraṁ paññapemi pāpassa kammassa kiriyāya pāpassa kammassa pavattiyā, no tathā kāyakammaṁ, no tathā vacīkamman”ti._[^9]

The first verse of the _Dhammapada_ says the same in verse:

> Intention is the leader of things;
> intention is first, they’re made by intention.
> If with corrupt intent
> you speak or act,
> suffering follows you,
> like a wheel, the ox’s foot.
>
> _Manopubbaṅgamā dhammā,_
> _manoseṭṭhā manomayā;_
> _Manasā ce paduṭṭhena,_
> _bhāsati vā karoti vā;_
> _Tato naṁ dukkhamanveti,_
> _cakkaṁva vahato padaṁ._[^10]

### Owner and heir (AN 5.57, MN 135)

One of the five reflections every person, lay or ordained, is asked to make often:

> ‘I am the owner of my deeds and heir to my deeds. Deeds are my womb, my relative, and my refuge. I shall be the heir of whatever deeds I do, whether good or bad.’ A woman or a man, a layperson or a renunciate should often review this.
>
> _‘Kammassakomhi, kammadāyādo kammayoni kammabandhu kammapaṭisaraṇo. Yaṁ kammaṁ karissāmi—kalyāṇaṁ vā pāpakaṁ vā— tassa dāyādo bhavissāmī’ti abhiṇhaṁ paccavekkhitabbaṁ itthiyā vā purisena vā gahaṭṭhena vā pabbajitena vā._[^11]

In MN 135 the young brahmin Subha asks why people differ in lifespan, health, beauty, wealth and wisdom. The Buddha answers with the same formula:

> “What is the cause, worthy Gotama, what is the reason why even among those who are human beings some are seen to be inferior and superior? For people are seen who are short-lived and long-lived, sickly and healthy, ugly and beautiful, insignificant and illustrious, poor and rich, from low and eminent families, witless and wise. \[...] “Student, sentient beings are the owners of their deeds and heir to their deeds. Deeds are their womb, their relative, and their refuge.
>
> _“Ko nu kho, bho gotama, hetu ko paccayo yena manussānaṁyeva sataṁ manussabhūtānaṁ dissanti hīnappaṇītatā? Dissanti hi, bho gotama, manussā appāyukā, dissanti dīghāyukā; dissanti bavhābādhā, dissanti appābādhā; dissanti dubbaṇṇā, dissanti vaṇṇavanto; dissanti appesakkhā, dissanti mahesakkhā; dissanti appabhogā, dissanti mahābhogā; dissanti nīcakulīnā, dissanti uccākulīnā; dissanti duppaññā, dissanti paññavanto. \[...] “Kammassakā, māṇava, sattā kammadāyādā kammayonī kammabandhū kammappaṭisaraṇā._[^12]

The rest of the sutta pairs each outcome with a kind of conduct: killing with a short life (5.1–5.3), hurting creatures with sickness (7.1–7.3), irritability with ugliness (9.1–9.4), and so on. Read with the suttas below, this is a teaching about tendencies, not an audit of every misfortune.

### Not everything is past karma (SN 36.21, AN 3.61)

The wanderer Moḷiyasīvaka reports a view that everything one feels is caused by past deeds. The Buddha calls it wrong:

> ‘Everything this individual person experiences—pleasurable, painful, or neutral—is because of past deeds.’ \[...] “Sīvaka, some feelings stem from bile disorders. You can know this from your own personal experience, and it is generally deemed to be true. Since this is so, the ascetics and brahmins whose view is that everything an individual person experiences is because of past deeds go beyond personal experience and beyond what is generally deemed to be true. So those ascetics and brahmins are wrong, I say.
>
> _‘yaṁ kiñcāyaṁ purisapuggalo paṭisaṁvedeti sukhaṁ vā dukkhaṁ vā adukkhamasukhaṁ vā sabbaṁ taṁ pubbekatahetū’ti. \[...] “Pittasamuṭṭhānānipi kho, sīvaka, idhekaccāni vedayitāni uppajjanti. Sāmampi kho etaṁ, sīvaka, veditabbaṁ yathā pittasamuṭṭhānānipi idhekaccāni vedayitāni uppajjanti. Lokassapi kho etaṁ, sīvaka, saccasammataṁ yathā pittasamuṭṭhānānipi idhekaccāni vedayitāni uppajjanti. Tatra, sīvaka, ye te samaṇabrāhmaṇā evaṁvādino evaṁdiṭṭhino: ‘yaṁ kiñcāyaṁ purisapuggalo paṭisaṁvedeti sukhaṁ vā dukkhaṁ vā adukkhamasukhaṁ vā sabbaṁ taṁ pubbekatahetū’ti. Yañca sāmaṁ ñātaṁ tañca atidhāvanti, yañca loke saccasammataṁ tañca atidhāvanti. Tasmā tesaṁ samaṇabrāhmaṇānaṁ micchāti vadāmi._[^2]

He then lists eight causes of feeling, closing with a verse:

> “Bile, phlegm, and wind,
> their conjunction, and the weather,
> not taking care of yourself, exertion,
> and the result of deeds is the eighth.”
>
> _“Pittaṁ semhañca vāto ca,_
> _Sannipātā utūni ca;_
> _Visamaṁ opakkamikaṁ,_
> _Kammavipākena aṭṭhamī”ti._[^2]

In AN 3.61 the Buddha rejects the same view as one of three "sectarian tenets," alongside creation by God and pure chance. His objection is practical: if everything is past karma, present effort has no point, and nothing is left that should or should not be done.

> Those who believe that past deeds are the most important thing have no enthusiasm or effort, no idea that there are things that should and should not be done. Not finding as a genuine fact that there are things that should and should not be done, they’re unmindful and careless, and can’t rightly be called ascetics.
>
> _Pubbekataṁ kho pana, bhikkhave, sārato paccāgacchataṁ na hoti chando vā vāyāmo vā idaṁ vā karaṇīyaṁ idaṁ vā akaraṇīyanti. Iti karaṇīyākaraṇīye kho pana saccato thetato anupalabbhiyamāne muṭṭhassatīnaṁ anārakkhānaṁ viharataṁ na hoti paccattaṁ sahadhammiko samaṇavādo._[^13]

So early Buddhism is not fatalist. Past karma conditions the present; it does not decide what you do next.

### Salt and water (AN 3.100)

Your note "Salt and Water" matches this sutta. The same small bad deed lands one person in hell and is spent in this life for another. What differs is the person:

> Take the case of the individual who does a trivial bad deed, but it lands them in hell. Meanwhile, another individual does the same trivial bad deed, but experiences it in this life, so that not even a little remains, let alone a lot. \[...] An individual who hasn’t developed their physical endurance, ethics, mind, or wisdom. They’re small-minded and mean-spirited, living in suffering. \[...] An individual who has developed their physical endurance, ethics, mind, and wisdom. They’re not small-minded, but are big-hearted, meditating on the immeasurables. \[...] Suppose a person was to drop a lump of salt into a mug of water. What do you think, mendicants? Would that mug of water become salty and undrinkable?” “Yes, sir. Why is that? Because there is only a little water in the bowl.” “Suppose a person was to drop a lump of salt into the Ganges River. What do you think, mendicants? Would the Ganges River become salty and undrinkable?” “No, sir. Why is that? Because the Ganges River is a vast mass of water.”
>
> _Idha, bhikkhave, ekaccassa puggalassa appamattakampi pāpakammaṁ kataṁ tamenaṁ nirayaṁ upaneti. Idha pana, bhikkhave, ekaccassa puggalassa tādisaṁyeva appamattakaṁ pāpakammaṁ kataṁ diṭṭhadhammavedanīyaṁ hoti, nāṇupi khāyati, kiṁ bahudeva. \[...] Idha pana, bhikkhave, ekacco puggalo abhāvitakāyo hoti abhāvitasīlo abhāvitacitto abhāvitapañño paritto appātumo appadukkhavihārī. \[...] Idha, bhikkhave, ekacco puggalo bhāvitakāyo hoti bhāvitasīlo bhāvitacitto bhāvitapañño aparitto mahatto appamāṇavihārī. \[...] Seyyathāpi, bhikkhave, puriso loṇakapallaṁ paritte udakamallake pakkhipeyya. Taṁ kiṁ maññatha, bhikkhave, api nu taṁ parittaṁ udakaṁ amunā loṇakapallena loṇaṁ assa apeyyan”ti? “Evaṁ, bhante”. “Taṁ kissa hetu”? “Aduñhi, bhante, parittaṁ udakakapallake udakaṁ, taṁ amunā loṇakapallena loṇaṁ assa apeyyan”ti. “Seyyathāpi, bhikkhave, puriso loṇakapallakaṁ gaṅgāya nadiyā pakkhipeyya. Taṁ kiṁ maññatha, bhikkhave, api nu sā gaṅgā nadī amunā loṇakapallena loṇaṁ assa apeyyā”ti? “No hetaṁ, bhante”. “Taṁ kissa hetu”? “Asu hi, bhante, gaṅgāya nadiyā mahā udakakkhandho so amunā loṇakapallena loṇo na assa apeyyo”ti._[^3]

The sutta opens by rejecting the idea that a deed is experienced exactly as it was done: if that were so, it says, the spiritual life could not be lived (1.2). Karma is not a fixed debt. It meets a mind, and the size of that mind decides how much the salt matters. AN 10.219 makes the same point about the boundless states of love, compassion, rejoicing and equanimity:

> They understand: ‘Formerly my mind was limited and undeveloped. Now it’s limitless and well developed. Whatever limited deeds I’ve done don’t remain or persist there.’
>
> _So evaṁ pajānāti: ‘pubbe kho me idaṁ cittaṁ parittaṁ ahosi abhāvitaṁ, etarahi pana me idaṁ cittaṁ appamāṇaṁ subhāvitaṁ. Yaṁ kho pana kiñci pamāṇakataṁ kammaṁ, na taṁ tatrāvasissati na taṁ tatrāvatiṭṭhatī’ti._[^14]

AN 10.217 balances this: deliberate deeds are not wiped out without being experienced.[^15] The salt is still there; the water changes.

> “Mendicants, I don’t say that intentional deeds that have been performed and accumulated are eliminated without being experienced. And that may be in this very life, or in the next life, or in some subsequent period. But in any case, there is no making an end of suffering without experiencing intentional deeds that have been performed and accumulated, I say.
>
> _“Nāhaṁ, bhikkhave, sañcetanikānaṁ kammānaṁ katānaṁ upacitānaṁ appaṭisaṁveditvā byantībhāvaṁ vadāmi. Tañca kho diṭṭheva dhamme upapajje vā apare vā pariyāye. Na tvevāhaṁ, bhikkhave, sañcetanikānaṁ kammānaṁ katānaṁ upacitānaṁ appaṭisaṁveditvā dukkhassantakiriyaṁ vadāmi._[^15]

### Prayer does not float a rock (SN 42.6)

Asked whether he can send the dead to heaven as the western brahmins claim to, the Buddha answers with a simile:

> “Chief, suppose a person were to throw a broad rock into a deep lake. And a large crowd were to come together to offer up supplication and entreaty, circumambulating it with cupped palms, and saying: ‘Rise, good rock! Float, good rock! Float to shore, good rock!’ What do you think, chief? Would that broad rock rise up or float because of their supplications?” “No, sir.”
>
> _“Seyyathāpi, gāmaṇi, puriso mahatiṁ puthusilaṁ gambhīre udakarahade pakkhipeyya. Tamenaṁ mahā janakāyo saṅgamma samāgamma āyāceyya thomeyya pañjaliko anuparisakkeyya: ‘ummujja, bho puthusile, uplava, bho puthusile, thalamuplava, bho puthusile’ti. Taṁ kiṁ maññasi, gāmaṇi, api nu sā puthusilā mahato janakāyassa āyācanahetu vā thomanahetu vā pañjalikā anuparisakkanahetu vā ummujjeyya vā uplaveyya vā thalaṁ vā uplaveyyā”ti? “No hetaṁ, bhante”._[^16]

A pot of ghee broken in the lake rises whatever the crowd chants (7.1). The point concerns ritual (see [[Ritual vs Practice]]): no ceremony changes the direction of a life's deeds. The _Dhammapada_ puts it plainly:

> For it is by oneself that evil’s done,
> one is corrupted by oneself.
> It’s by oneself that evil’s not done,
> one is purified by oneself.
> Purity and impurity are personal matters,
> no one can purify another.
>
> _Attanā hi kataṁ pāpaṁ,_
> _attanā saṅkilissati;_
> _Attanā akataṁ pāpaṁ,_
> _attanāva visujjhati;_
> _Suddhī asuddhi paccattaṁ,_
> _nāñño aññaṁ visodhaye._[^17]

### Seeds (AN 3.34)

Deeds born of greed, hate and delusion ripen wherever the new life is born. The simile is agricultural:

> Suppose some seeds were intact, unspoiled, not weather-damaged, fertile, and well-kept. They’re sown in a well-prepared, productive field, and the heavens provide plenty of rain. Then those seeds would grow, increase, and mature. \[...] Any deed that emerges from contentment—born, sourced, and originated from contentment—is given up when greed is done away with. It’s cut off at the root, made like a palm stump, obliterated, and unable to arise in the future.
>
> _Seyyathāpi, bhikkhave, bījāni akhaṇḍāni apūtīni avātātapahatāni sārādāni sukhasayitāni sukhette suparikammakatāya bhūmiyā nikkhittāni. Devo ca sammādhāraṁ anuppaveccheyya. Evassu tāni, bhikkhave, bījāni vuddhiṁ viruḷhiṁ vepullaṁ āpajjeyyuṁ. \[...] Yaṁ, bhikkhave, alobhapakataṁ kammaṁ alobhajaṁ alobhanidānaṁ alobhasamudayaṁ, lobhe vigate evaṁ taṁ kammaṁ pahīnaṁ hoti ucchinnamūlaṁ tālāvatthukataṁ anabhāvaṅkataṁ āyatiṁ anuppādadhammaṁ._[^18]

Deeds from non-greed, non-hate and non-delusion do not become new seeds of this kind. The image of seeds (_bīja_) becomes central in Yogācāra (below).

### Old karma and new karma (SN 35.146, SN 12.37)

Your note asks how much of our thinking is the result of past actions and decisions. The suttas answer that the very instruments of experience are old karma:

> And what is old deeds? The eye is old deeds. It should be seen as produced by choices and intentions, as something to be felt. The ear … nose … tongue … body … mind is old deeds. It should be seen as produced by choices and intentions, as something to be felt. This is called old deeds. And what is new deeds? The deeds you currently perform by way of body, speech, and mind. These are called new deeds.
>
> _Katamañca, bhikkhave, purāṇakammaṁ? Cakkhu, bhikkhave, purāṇakammaṁ abhisaṅkhataṁ abhisañcetayitaṁ vedaniyaṁ daṭṭhabbaṁ …pe… jivhā purāṇakammā abhisaṅkhatā abhisañcetayitā vedaniyā daṭṭhabbā …pe… mano purāṇakammo abhisaṅkhato abhisañcetayito vedaniyo daṭṭhabbo. Idaṁ vuccati, bhikkhave, purāṇakammaṁ. Katamañca, bhikkhave, navakammaṁ? Yaṁ kho, bhikkhave, etarahi kammaṁ karoti kāyena vācāya manasā, idaṁ vuccati, bhikkhave, navakammaṁ._[^19]

> “Mendicants, this body doesn’t belong to you or to anyone else. It’s old deeds, and should be seen as produced by choices and intentions, as something to be felt.
>
> _“Nāyaṁ, bhikkhave, kāyo tumhākaṁ napi aññesaṁ. Purāṇamidaṁ, bhikkhave, kammaṁ abhisaṅkhataṁ abhisañcetayitaṁ vedaniyaṁ daṭṭhabbaṁ._[^20]

So the eye, the mind and this body are the ripening of past intentions. What you do with them now is new karma. SN 12.37 turns straight from this to dependent origination: the body is not "mine," but a process with conditions.

### The loop, and the linchpin (Snp 3.9)

Your "infinite eternal loop" is _saṃsāra_: intention produces results, results condition new intention. The _Vāseṭṭha Sutta_ closes with the image of a linchpin holding a moving chariot to its wheels:

> In this way the astute regard deeds
> in accord with truth.
> Seeing dependent origination,
> they’re expert in deeds and their results.
> Deeds make the world go on,
> deeds make people go on;
> sentient beings are bound by deeds,
> like a moving chariot’s linchpin.
>
> _Evametaṁ yathābhūtaṁ,_
> _Kammaṁ passanti paṇḍitā;_
> _Paṭiccasamuppādadassā,_
> _Kammavipākakovidā._
> _Kammunā vattati loko,_
> _Kammunā vattati pajā;_
> _Kammanibandhanā sattā,_
> _Rathassāṇīva yāyato._[^21]

Earlier in the same poem, one is a brahmin by deeds, not by birth (62.3–62.4), a direct challenge to caste.

### The ending of deeds (AN 4.232)

The Buddha names four kinds of deed:

> There are dark deeds with dark results. There are bright deeds with bright results. There are dark and bright deeds with dark and bright results. There are neither dark nor bright deeds with neither dark nor bright results, which lead to the ending of deeds.
>
> _Atthi, bhikkhave, kammaṁ kaṇhaṁ kaṇhavipākaṁ; atthi, bhikkhave, kammaṁ sukkaṁ sukkavipākaṁ; atthi, bhikkhave, kammaṁ kaṇhasukkaṁ kaṇhasukkavipākaṁ; atthi, bhikkhave, kammaṁ akaṇhaasukkaṁ akaṇhaasukkavipākaṁ kammakkhayāya saṁvattati._[^4]

The next sutta defines the fourth: it is the intention (_cetanā_) to give up the other three (AN 4.233, 5.2).[^22] It leads out of becoming. Bright karma is good, but it is still karma.

### Unthinkable (AN 4.77)

The precise working of karma is one of four things the Buddha calls unthinkable:

> “Mendicants, these four things are unthinkable. They should not be thought about, and anyone who tries to think about them will go mad or get frustrated. \[...] The results of deeds …
>
> _“Cattārimāni, bhikkhave, acinteyyāni, na cintetabbāni; yāni cintento ummādassa vighātassa bhāgī assa. \[...] Kammavipāko, bhikkhave, acinteyyo, na cintetabbo; yaṁ cintento ummādassa vighātassa bhāgī assa._[^23]

The tradition gives karma as a principle to live by, not a ledger to calculate. Trying to trace each misfortune to a cause in a past life is the frustration the sutta warns against.

### No place to hide (Dhp 127)

> Not in midair, nor mid-ocean,
> nor hiding in a mountain cleft;
> you’ll find no place on the planet
> to escape your wicked deeds.
>
> _Na antalikkhe na samuddamajjhe,_
> _Na pabbatānaṁ vivaraṁ pavissa;_
> _Na vijjatī so jagatippadeso,_
> _Yatthaṭṭhito mucceyya pāpakammā._[^24]

### Dreams

Your note opens "As in Dreams." The Vinaya tells of monks worried that an emission in a dream broke a rule against intentional emission. The Buddha replies:

> “The Buddha has laid down a training rule that intentional emission of semen is an offense entailing suspension. We had an emission while dreaming, which is not without intention. Could it be that we’ve committed an offense entailing suspension?” They told the Buddha. “It’s true, monks, that a dream is not without intention, but it’s negligible.
>
> _“bhagavatā sikkhāpadaṁ paññattaṁ— ‘sañcetanikā sukkavissaṭṭhi saṅghādiseso’ti. Amhākañca supinantena asuci muccati. Atthi cettha cetanā labbhati. Kacci nu kho mayaṁ saṅghādisesaṁ āpattiṁ āpannā”ti? Bhagavato etamatthaṁ ārocesuṁ. “Atthesā, bhikkhave, cetanā; sā ca kho abbohārikāti._[^25]

"Negligible" translates _abbohārika_, "not to be counted." Dream intention exists, but it is too weak to count as a deed. The busy thoughts of the waking mind are a different matter: these are _manokamma_, mental deeds, and MN 56 calls them the heaviest.

## Abhidharma: the variety of the world

Vasubandhu's _Abhidharmakośabhāṣya_ opens its chapter on karma with the claim that karma, not a creator, accounts for the variety of the world (translations from the Chinese in this note are Claude's):

> 世別由業生，思及思所作，思即是意業，所作謂身語。
>
> 論曰：非由一主先覺而生，但由有情業差別起。

"The variety of the world is born of karma. \[Karma] is intention and what intention makes. Intention is mental karma; what it makes is bodily and verbal karma." The commentary adds: "\[The world] does not arise from a single lord with prior knowledge; it arises only from the differences in the karma of sentient beings."[^26]

The verse repeats AN 6.63's definition (_cetanā_ and _cetayitvā_) and makes it cosmological: the shared karma of beings shapes the shared world.

## Madhyamaka: karma empty, but not lost

Nāgārjuna's chapter on karma (_Mūlamadhyamakakārikā_ 17) opens with the same twofold definition:

> 「大聖說二業，思與從思生，是業別相中，種種分別說。」
> 「佛所說思者，所謂意業是；所從思生者，即是身口業。」

"The Great Sage taught two kinds of karma: intention, and what arises from intention; within their particular marks he explained them in many ways. What the Buddha called intention is mental karma; what arises from intention is bodily and verbal karma." (17.2–3)[^27]

He then refuses both ways of making karma a thing. If karma lasted unchanged until it ripened, it would be permanent; if it perished, it could not bear fruit:

> 「雖空亦不斷，雖有亦不常，業果報不失，是名佛所說。」

"Though empty, it is not annihilated; though it exists, it is not permanent. Karma and its fruit are not lost: this is what the Buddha taught." (17.20)[^27]

The chapter ends with lines that answer your "As in Dreams":

> 諸煩惱及業，作者及果報，皆如幻與夢，如炎亦如嚮。

"The afflictions and karma, the agent and the fruit, are all like an illusion and a dream, like a mirage and an echo." (17.33)[^27] For Siderits and Katsura's translation from the Sanskrit, see their chapter 17.[^28]

This is not a denial that karma works. Dreams have effects: the dreamer wakes sweating. Karma works the way a dream works, dependently, without anything solid underneath.

## Yogācāra: seeds and habit-energy

Yogācāra explains how karma persists without a self to carry it. Deeds leave _bīja_ (seeds) and _vāsanā_ (habit-energy, 習氣) in the ==storehouse consciousness== (_ālaya-vijñāna_). The _Triṃśikā_ gives the mechanism of rebirth in one verse:

> 由諸業習氣、二取習氣俱，前異熟既盡，復生餘異熟。

"Through the habit-energies of karma, together with the habit-energies of the twofold grasping, when the previous ripening is exhausted, another ripening arises." (v. 19)[^29]

"The twofold grasping" is the split into grasper and grasped, self and world. Karma supplies the direction; the habit of splitting experience into subject and object supplies the frame. William Waldron's study of the _ālaya-vijñāna_ traces this model from its Indian sources, and his account of the self-making self is summarised in [[The Self-Wrought Self]].[^30] The seed and perfuming model, and its link to habitual thought, is in [[Mental and Conceptual Proliferation - prapañca - papanca - Chaotic Thoughts]].

## Chan and Zen: not blind to cause and effect

### Baizhang's fox (Wumen guan, case 2)

The best-known Zen teaching on karma. An old man attends Baizhang's talks and one day stays behind:

> 老人云。諾某甲非人也。於過去迦葉佛時。曾住此山。因學人問。大修行底人還落因果。也無。某甲對云。不落因果。五百生墮野狐身。今請和尚。代一轉語貴。脫野狐遂問。大修行底人還落因果。也無。師云。不昧因果。老人於言下大悟。
>
> 無門曰。不落因果。為甚墮野狐。不昧因果。為甚脫野狐。若向者裏著得一隻眼。便知得。前百丈贏得。風流五百生。
> 不落不昧　兩采一賽　不昧不落　千錯萬錯

"The old man said: 'Yes. I am not a human being. In the past, in the time of Kāśyapa Buddha, I lived on this mountain. A student asked, "Does a person of great practice still fall into cause and effect?" I answered, "He does not fall into cause and effect." For five hundred lives I fell into the body of a fox. Now I ask you to say a turning word for me, so I may be freed from the fox body.' He then asked: 'Does a person of great practice still fall into cause and effect?' The master said: 'He is not blind to cause and effect.' At these words the old man was greatly awakened."

"Wumen says: Not falling into cause and effect, why did he fall into a fox? Not blind to cause and effect, why was he freed from the fox? If you get one eye on this, you will know that the former Baizhang won five hundred lives of elegance. The verse: Not falling, not blind: two faces of one die. Not blind, not falling: a thousand errors, ten thousand errors."[^31]

_Furaku inga_ 不落因果 ("not falling into cause and effect") claims that awakening puts one above karma. _Fumai inga_ 不昧因果 ("not blind to," or "not obscuring," cause and effect) says awakening means seeing karma clearly, not escaping it. Wumen's verse then refuses to let either phrase become a formula.

### Dōgen: deep faith in cause and effect

In the late fascicle _Jinshin inga_ 深信因果 ("Deep Faith in Cause and Effect"), Dōgen retells the fox kōan and refuses to treat the two answers as equal: the first denies cause and effect, the second is deep faith in it. The Sōtō school's own commentary stresses that practice must affirm causality while seeing it as empty, and that karma must not be used to justify fatalism or discrimination.[^32] Steven Heine's book on the fox kōan follows the story through Chan, Dōgen and Japanese folklore.[^33]

Jacqueline Stone describes how the Critical Buddhism (_hihan bukkyō_ 批判仏教) scholars Hakamaya Noriaki and Matsumoto Shirō took the twelve-fascicle _Shōbōgenzō_, with its basic teaching on karmic causality, as a shift in Dōgen's thought back to causality as the core of Buddhism, and presented original-enlightenment (_hongaku_) thought as the source of social discrimination. Stone notes that this reading also served an apologetic purpose for the Sōtō school, which faced charges from buraku liberation groups.[^34]

### Shinran: karmic conditions

In the _Tannishō_ 歎異抄 (ch. 13), Shinran asks his disciple Yuien whether he would kill a thousand people if told this would ensure birth in the Pure Land. Yuien says he could not kill even one. Shinran answers that this shows we do not act simply as we wish: without the karmic conditions (_gōen_ 業縁), one does not kill; with them, one might harm many without wishing to.[^35] Here karma is the reason for humility, not for judging others. The point is that no one can claim moral credit for goodness that conditions made possible.

### Japan's first karma tales

The _Nihon ryōiki_ 日本霊異記 (early ninth century), compiled by the monk Kyōkai, is the earliest Japanese collection of Buddhist tales. Its full title, _Nihonkoku genpō zen'aku ryōiki_ 日本国現報善悪霊異記, promises "miraculous stories of karmic retribution of good and evil in this life, in Japan." Most of its tales show deeds repaid in the same lifetime.[^36]

### Shinto: _tsumi_ and _harae_

Shinto has no doctrine of karma. Its nearest terms are _tsumi_ 罪 (offence, including sickness, disaster and error in old usage) and _kegare_ 穢れ (pollution). Kokugakuin's _Basic Terms of Shinto_ says these can be removed by _harae_ 祓 (purification) and that Shinto has no concept of original sin.[^37] The contrast with SN 42.6 and Dhp 165 is sharp: in Buddhism, no rite undoes an intentional deed; in Shinto, purification restores the normal condition. Centuries of _shinbutsu shūgō_ (神仏習合, kami–buddha combination) let the two logics coexist in Japanese practice.

## The shadow side

Karma has been used to blame victims.

- **Japan.** William Bodiford records that Sōtō Zen temples used discriminatory posthumous names and memorial registers for outcaste (_hisabetsu buraku_) families, and that the school was forced to reform after the "Machida affair" (1979–84). He notes that doctrines of karmic retribution suggest the disadvantaged deserve their fate, and that karma was often read in Japan in fatalistic terms, bound up with taboo and ritual pollution.[^38]
- **Across Asia.** Brian Victoria documents Japanese Buddhist leaders who used karma to explain away poverty, disease and wartime death, and argues that the Buddha's ethical reading of karma has often been turned into victim-blaming. He cites Richard Gombrich, who sees the Buddha's equation of karma with intention as a bold step that made people masters of their own destinies.[^39] [^40]
- **India.** In a study of 385 Indian university students, belief in karma partly mediated the link between social-dominance and authoritarian attitudes and support for anti-egalitarian policies. The authors read karma here as a legitimising ideology for caste.[^41]

The suttas themselves supply the corrective: not all suffering is karma (SN 36.21), the precise working of karma is unthinkable (AN 4.77), and fatalism is a wrong view (AN 3.61). The Sōtō school's own teaching now warns against using karma to justify discrimination or fatalism.[^32]

## Karma, illusion or free will?

Your last line asks the central philosophical question.

**Not fatalism.** The Buddha rejects the view that everything is due to past deeds (AN 3.61, SN 36.21). If it were, nothing would be left that should or should not be done.

**Not a free self either.** Early Buddhism teaches no-self (_anattā_), so there is no independent chooser standing outside causes. Intention is itself a conditioned mental factor. This is why some philosophers describe the Buddhist position as "agentless agency": choice is real, but no separate self does the choosing. Rick Repetti's anthology collects the main positions.[^42]

**Illusion, but effective.** Nāgārjuna calls karma, agent and fruit like a dream (MMK 17.33), and in the same chapter says that karma and its fruit are not lost (17.20). The Madhyamaka answer is that karma is empty of its own nature and for exactly that reason can work.

**What ethics follows.** Goodman and Schultz note that, in Buddhist sources, the karmic weight of an action depends strongly on its motivation, and that scholars disagree whether Buddhist ethics is best read as a virtue ethics (Damien Keown) or a form of consequentialism (Goodman).[^43]

For the neuroscience of intention (Libet and after), see [[Intention]].

## Psychology and science

The sciences cannot test rebirth. They can study the parts of karma that lie in this life: intention, habit, and what belief in karma does to people.

### Intention in moral judgment

The Buddha's equation of karma with intention can look like common sense. Cross-cultural evidence suggests it is not universal. Barrett and colleagues compared eight small-scale societies with two Western ones and found substantial variation in how much intent and mitigating circumstances affect moral judgment. All groups took intent into account to some degree, but to very different extents. The authors conclude that the weight large industrial societies give to intention may be a recent cultural development rather than a fixed feature of human moral judgment.[^44] On this evidence the Buddha's definition was a real moral innovation, as Gombrich argues.

### Habit: how deeds become character

The early texts say repeated intention shapes the mind (see MN 19 in [[Mental and Conceptual Proliferation - prapañca - papanca - Chaotic Thoughts]]). Psychology agrees in its own terms. Wood and Rünger's review shows that behaviours repeated in stable contexts become automatic, cued by context rather than goals, and that people often read their habitual behaviour as intentionally chosen.[^45] This last finding speaks to your line about "a mind that is always justifying itself": habit runs first, and the mind supplies reasons afterwards.

### What belief in karma does

- **Why people believe it.** White and Norenzayan treat karma as a belief in supernatural justice. They trace it to general cognitive tendencies, to motivations for meaning and control, and to cultural evolution, and they show it shapes both causal judgments and prosocial behaviour.[^46]
- **It can make people fairer.** In four experiments (N = 3,591), including high-powered pre-registered ones, thinking about karma reduced selfishness in anonymous economic games among people who believe in karma: Hindus, Buddhists, Christians and non-religious Americans.[^47]
- **People "invest" in karma when they lack control.** When people want an outcome they cannot control, such as a job offer, they give more time and money to others, and afterwards feel more optimistic.[^48]
- **Believers are trusted more, not more trustworthy.** American Christians expected more trustworthiness from karma believers and trusted them more; the karma believers were no more trustworthy in fact.[^49]
- **It can justify inequality.** See the Indian caste study above.[^41]

## Where the traditions meet

- **Intention is the heart of it.** AN 6.63, the _Abhidharmakośa_ and Nāgārjuna all define karma as intention and what intention makes.
- **Karma is a process, not a verdict.** Seeds, salt, rocks and oil: every image is natural, not judicial.
- **Context shapes ripening.** The salt-and-river simile, the boundless mind in AN 10.219, and the psychology of habit all say the same act lands differently in different minds.
- **Seeing karma clearly is the practice.** _Fumai inga_, "not blind to cause and effect," matches the early Buddhist aim of knowing deeds, their source and their end (AN 6.63).

## Where they part

- **Rebirth.** Early Buddhism, Abhidharma and Yogācāra treat rebirth as literal. Some modern readers (and Victoria) favour a this-life or naturalistic reading. Science has nothing to say either way.
- **Escaping karma.** Early texts speak of "ending" deeds; the fox kōan warns that claiming to stand above karma is itself karma.
- **Grace and conditions.** Shinran's stress on karmic conditions leads to reliance on Amida, not self-effort. Early texts stress effort (AN 3.61).
- **Purity.** Buddhism holds that no rite cancels a deed; Shinto holds that purification restores the normal condition.
