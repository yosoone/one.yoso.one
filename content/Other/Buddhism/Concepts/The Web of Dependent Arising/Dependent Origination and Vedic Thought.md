# Dependent Origination and Vedic Thought

## About this note

This note sets the twelve links beside the Vedic accounts of creation. It follows Joanna Jurewicz's "Playing with Fire" (2000), the fullest study of the question. Jurewicz is a Vedicist; she says she is not a Buddhologist and works only with the classical twelve-link chain and the most general meaning of each link (p. 77).[^1][^2] Her claim is not that the Buddha borrowed from the Veda, but that he answered it.

## The thesis

> Inspired by Prof. Richard Gombrich’s investigation, I am inclined to believe that this similarity is neither accidental, nor caused by the Buddha’s inability to free himself from the mental paradigms of his culture. I would rather argue that he formulated the _pratītyasamutpāda_ as a polemic against Vedic thought. Through the identification of the creative process with the process that leads only to suffering, he rejected the Brāhmaṇic way of thinking in a truly spectacular way. (p. 78; [pdf](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/NEB99HER?page=2))

In the Vedic texts she uses, creation is the self-knowing of the Absolute. The _ātman_ (Prajāpati, the Creator) is both the one who creates and the forms he takes: the world, the human being, the inner self, and the fire altar that enacts them in ritual. Deny the _ātman_, as the doctrine of not-self does, and the same sequence of stages becomes a process with no one behind it, one that leads only to suffering (pp. 78–79). She allows that the polemic may belong to the compilers of the Canon rather than to the Buddha himself (p. 78, n. 5).

Jones, in his survey of dependent arising, treats this as a live possibility rather than a settled result:[^3]

> An intriguing suggestion is that the twelve _nidān&#x61;_&#x73; of dependent arising were originally meant as a parody of Vedic cosmogony, which would explain how they were formulated in their historical context, which was later forgotten. According to this interpretation, the twelve _nidān&#x61;_&#x73; explain the arising of conditioned experience in the absence of an _ātman_ or essential self, in contrast to the cosmogonic thinking of the _Upaniṣads_ and other Brahmanical literature of ancient India, which depict the religious quest as a rediscovery of the _ātman_ (Jurewicz 2000; Gombrich 2009: 129–143). (p. 11; [pdf](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/J7JCPXH5?page=12))

## Link by link

| 3 | _viññāṇa_ / _vijñāna_ | The self "made of consciousness" (_vijñānamaya_). In the Veda this manifestation repeats itself again and again. | TU 2; BU 4.4.5 | 85–89 |
| 4 | _nāmarūpa_ | The Creator gives the world name and form and enters it "up to the nail tips". Naming both shapes the world and hides the Creator within it, so it also makes knowing harder. | BU 1.4.7; ŚB 6.1.3; CU 6.3 | 89–91 |

| 8–9 | _taṇhā_, _upādāna_ | The Ṛgvedic poets, burning with inner heat, carry on creation by knowing and naming the world. In the Ṛgveda the root of _tṛṣṇā_ describes fire's activity. _Upādāna_ means both "fuel" and "grasping". | ṚV 10.129; ṚV hymns on Agni | 94–98 |

| title | _nidāna_ | In ṚV 10.130.3 _nidāna_ is the link between the Creator and his creation, their identity. The Buddha's "great" _nidāna_ (DN 15) denies that link. | ṚV 10.130.3; ŚB 11.1.6.3 | 100 |

The _āyatana_ passage shows how close the vocabulary runs. The guardians, in Olivelle's translation as Jurewicz quotes it, ask the self: “Find us a dwelling in which we can establish ourselves and eat food” (AU 1.2.1), and are told: “Enter, each into your respective abode” (AU 1.2.3) (p. 92; [pdf](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/NEB99HER?page=10)).

On _upādāna_, the fire image carries the argument:

> And this constant, voracious devouring of the fuel and its digesting are expressed by _upādāna_. The meaning of this word is both “fuel” and “grasping”. (p. 98; [pdf](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/NEB99HER?page=12))

On the title of DN 15:

> It is really “a great explanation”: there is no _ātman_, the _nidāna_ of the cosmogony. (p. 100; [pdf](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/NEB99HER?page=13))

## Where the two part ways

**Desirable and undesirable.** For the Veda, creation is good; for the Buddha, the same sequence ends in suffering. Jurewicz names this "the fundamental difference" at the outset (p. 77).

**A cycle and a line.** Vedic creation turns: consciousness manifests again and again (p. 88), and the poets repeat the Creator's work (p. 95, n. 48). The Buddha's chain does not:

> At the same time, it seems that the Buddha (perhaps for polemical purposes) aimed greatly to simplify the Vedic ideas; the most important result of this is that he let go the cyclical character of the process: the _pratītyasamutpāda_ is a simple, linear process. (p. 81; [pdf](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/NEB99HER?page=3))

She allows one exception, a possible repetition of ignorance in craving (p. 81, n. 12). This fits the open ring of SN 12.2 described in [[The Twelve Links#Open ring or closed wheel]]: on Jurewicz's reading the circle was the Veda's, and the early chain gave it up. That connection is Claude's, not hers.

**Two chains in one.** She reads the Vedic material as supporting a division of the twelve links into two shorter chains, one starting from ignorance and one from craving, as proposed in the commentary on the _Udānavarga_. The poets' creative burning repeats, in small, the Creator's. She rejects Frauwallner's view that the two shorter chains came first and were joined later (pp. 95–97 and n. 51). Bucknell's argument that the twelve-link list grew from shorter lists bears on this and has not yet been read against her ([[The Twelve Links#History of the formula]]).

**Blowing out.** Since the _ātman_ is fire, she describes the Buddha's move as cutting off the _ātman_, or rather blowing him out, and reads _nirvāṇa_ as also refuting the Vedic claim that fire underlies the cosmos (p. 101).

> The similarities between the Vedic cosmogony and the _pratītyasamutpāda_ which I have been trying to show are too evident to be pure coincidence. (p. 101; [pdf](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/NEB99HER?page=13))

## How far to trust the reading

- **Jurewicz's own limits.** She treats the polemic as "only one aspect of this huge problem" and leaves the rest to Buddhologists (pp. 77–78). The parallel for _vedanā_ is weak by her own account (p. 93).
- **Order.** Where a Vedic text puts the stages in a different order, as BU 1.4 does with name and form after the division into husband and wife, she explains it as the Buddha gathering several Vedic accounts into one simple scheme (p. 90, n. 37). A sceptic would press here: the more freely the sources can be rearranged, the easier the match.
- **Other scholars.** Jones calls the reading "an intriguing suggestion" and places it beside Gombrich (2009, 129–43), who argues along the same lines.[^3][^4] Gombrich's own work is not yet read.
- **The Vedic texts themselves.** The Upaniṣad and Brāhmaṇa passages are known here only through Jurewicz. They still need reading in an academic translation (Olivelle for the Upaniṣads, which she uses; Eggeling for the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa).
