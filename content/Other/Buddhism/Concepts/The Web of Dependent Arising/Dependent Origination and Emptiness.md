# Dependent Origination and Emptiness

## The core idea

Dependent origination (_paṭiccasamuppāda_, Skt. _pratītyasamutpāda_, 縁起 _engi_) says that what arises does so on conditions and ceases when they cease. Emptiness (_suññatā_, Skt. _śūnyatā_, 空 _kū_) says that what arises this way has no self of its own.

The two ideas were joined in stages:

- **The early discourses.** Dependent origination explains how suffering arises and ends. "Empty" usually means empty of self and of what belongs to a self.
- **Nāgārjuna.** Anything that arises dependently lacks intrinsic nature (_svabhāva_), and that lack is what "emptiness" means. He calls this the middle way.
- **The _Heart Sūtra_.** Form is emptiness, and emptiness is form.

This note follows that line through the primary texts. It then tests the claims of Leigh Brasington's _Dependent Origination and Emptiness_, the book this research began from, against scholarship and science.

## Starting point: the two items already in Zotero

**Leigh Brasington, _Dependent Origination and Emptiness_ (2nd ed., 2024).** Brasington is a lay meditation teacher, and he published the book himself under a CC BY-NC licence.[^1] He argues five things:

- Dependent origination concerns necessary conditions, not causes: "Dependent origination is a teaching about necessary conditions." (p. 15)
- The twelve links should be read moment to moment, not across three lives (pp. 38–41).
- The deeper teaching is a general principle, "this-that conditionality" (_idappaccayatā_) (p. 48).
- Nāgārjuna expands this into emptiness (pp. 57–66).
- Everything is "Streams Of Dependently Arising Processes Interacting", which he calls SODAPI: "When we look carefully, all we find are Streams Of Dependently Arising Processes Interacting – SODAPI." (p. 51)

He stresses that "It is very important to understand that “emptiness” does not mean “non-existence.”" (p. 57). He also warns that "It's important to remember that “Dependent Origination,” “Emptiness,” and “SODAPI” are all empty and are just concepts." (p. 82)

The book is useful practice-oriented synthesis. It is not peer-reviewed, and some of its historical claims are its author's own conjectures. Section 6 below sorts them.

**Gabriel Ellis, "Dependent Origination as Emergence of the Subject" (draft 2.0, 2020).** This is an unpublished draft by a PhD candidate at Warsaw University. It reads the twelve links as the development of the mind from the unconscious of the fetus: "In short, I argue that Dependent Origination describes the fabric of the human mind and how it develops from the ‘unconscious field’ of the fetus to the human mind as we know it." (p. 2)[^2] It is cited here only as one modern psychological reading. It is not scholarship to rely on.

## 1. Dependent origination in the early discourses

### The general principle (SN 12.21)

The shortest statement of conditionality, segments 1.8–1.9 in full:

> So, when this exists, this comes to be; due to the arising of this, this arises. When this doesn’t exist, this doesn’t come to be; due to the cessation of this, this ceases. That is:
>
> _Iti imasmiṁ sati idaṁ hoti, imassuppādā idaṁ uppajjati. Imasmiṁ asati idaṁ na hoti, imassa nirodhā idaṁ nirujjhati._[^3]

The "That is:" at the end of 1.9 leads straight into the twelve links. In the discourses the general formula and the twelve-link sequence usually come together.

### The twelve links (SN 12.1)

The standard definition, segments 2.1–2.14 in full:

> “And what is dependent origination? Ignorance is a requirement for choices. Choices are a requirement for consciousness. Consciousness is a requirement for name and form. Name and form are requirements for the six sense fields. The six sense fields are requirements for contact. Contact is a requirement for feeling. Feeling is a requirement for craving. Craving is a requirement for grasping. Grasping is a requirement for continued existence. Continued existence is a requirement for rebirth. Rebirth is a requirement for old age and death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, sadness, and distress to come to be. That is how this entire mass of suffering originates. This is called dependent origination.
>
> _“Katamo ca, bhikkhave, paṭiccasamuppādo? Avijjāpaccayā, bhikkhave, saṅkhārā; saṅkhārapaccayā viññāṇaṁ; viññāṇapaccayā nāmarūpaṁ; nāmarūpapaccayā saḷāyatanaṁ; saḷāyatanapaccayā phasso; phassapaccayā vedanā; vedanāpaccayā taṇhā; taṇhāpaccayā upādānaṁ; upādānapaccayā bhavo; bhavapaccayā jāti; jātipaccayā jarāmaraṇaṁ sokaparidevadukkhadomanassupāyāsā sambhavanti. Evametassa kevalassa dukkhakkhandhassa samudayo hoti. Ayaṁ vuccati, bhikkhave, paṭiccasamuppādo._[^4]

The same sutta then gives the sequence of cessation: when ignorance fades away and ceases, each link ceases in turn, and the whole mass of suffering ceases with them (3.1–3.12).

### A law of nature, not a creation (SN 12.20)

SN 12.20 presents conditionality as something the Buddha discovered, not something he made up. Segment 2.3 in full:

> Whether Realized Ones arise or not, this law of nature persists, this regularity of natural principles, this surety of natural principles, specific conditionality.
>
> _Uppādā vā tathāgatānaṁ anuppādā vā tathāgatānaṁ, ṭhitāva sā dhātu dhammaṭṭhitatā dhammaniyāmatā idappaccayatā._[^5]

The same sutta distinguishes dependent origination (_paṭiccasamuppāda_), the principle, from dependently originated phenomena (_paṭiccasamuppannā dhammā_), the things that arise under it (1.2, 4.1–4.14). Each link is described in the same terms, ending with segment 4.13: "Ignorance is impermanent, conditioned, dependently originated, liable to end, vanish, fade away, and cease."

The sutta ends where Brasington's chapter on Sāti (MN 38) ends. A noble disciple who has clearly seen dependent origination can no longer run back to the past, forward to the future, or into doubt about the present, asking whether they existed, will exist, or exist now (5.1–5.9).[^5]

### Seeing it is seeing the Dhamma (MN 28)

Sāriputta quotes the Buddha, segments 28.4–28.7 in full:

> But the Buddha has said: “One who sees dependent origination sees the teaching. One who sees the teaching sees dependent origination.” And these five grasping aggregates are indeed dependently originated.
>
> _Vuttaṁ kho panetaṁ bhagavatā: “yo paṭiccasamuppādaṁ passati so dhammaṁ passati; yo dhammaṁ passati so paṭiccasamuppādaṁ passatī”ti. Paṭiccasamuppannā kho panime yadidaṁ pañcupādānakkhandhā._[^6]

The sentence right after the famous saying applies it to the five grasping aggregates. Seeing dependent origination is seeing the person as dependently arisen.

### Two sheaves of reeds (SN 12.67)

Consciousness and name-and-form condition each other, like two sheaves of reeds leaning together. Segments 7.4–7.6 and 7.10–7.11:

> Suppose there were two sheaves of reeds leaning up against each other. In the same way, name and form are requirements for consciousness. Consciousness is a requirement for name and form. \[...] If the first of those sheaves of reeds were to be pulled away, the other would collapse. And if the other were to be pulled away, the first would collapse.
>
> _Seyyathāpi, āvuso, dve naḷakalāpiyo aññamaññaṁ nissāya tiṭṭheyyuṁ. Evameva kho, āvuso, nāmarūpapaccayā viññāṇaṁ; viññāṇapaccayā nāmarūpaṁ; \[...] Tāsañce, āvuso, naḷakalāpīnaṁ ekaṁ ākaḍḍheyya, ekā papateyya; aparañce ākaḍḍheyya, aparā papateyya._[^7]

This is the clearest place where the discourses describe mutual conditioning rather than a one-way chain. The Yogācāra _Cheng weishi lun_ later borrows the same image of reeds for the simultaneity of seed and manifestation (see [[Mental and Conceptual Proliferation - prapañca - papanca - Chaotic Thoughts]]).

### How the doctrine developed

Several scholars trace a history behind the twelve-link formula:

- **Variant lists.** Roderick Bucknell compares the many canonical versions of the chain, which differ in length and starting point. He argues that the standard twelve links developed from shorter and differently ordered lists.[^8] Brasington cites Bucknell for his reading of name-and-form (p. 19, n. 4) and sets out his own chart of variant lists in Appendix 1.
- **The Vedic background.** Joanna Jurewicz reads the twelve links against Vedic accounts of creation and argues that the formula answers those cosmogonies in the Buddha's own terms.[^9] Brasington accepts her account of the vocabulary but not her view of the Buddha's aims (p. 18).
- **Psychology before ontology.** Eviatar Shulman argues that in the early texts dependent origination describes the workings of the mind in experience. On his reading it is not a general theory of how all things exist.[^10]

_These three papers are paraphrased here, not quoted, until their PDFs have been read (see To review)._

### How the twelve links are read

**Across three lives.** The Theravāda commentarial tradition, systematised in Buddhaghosa's _Visuddhimagga_ (ch. 17), spreads the links over past, present and future lives. Dhivan Thomas Jones's encyclopedia entry is direct about its date: "It is anachronistic to read this three-life interpretation back into the early discourses."[^11] Jones also notes that Theravāda and Sarvāstivāda each had their own version of the three-life reading, and that it has always been disputed.

**Moment to moment.** This reading applies all twelve links to each moment of experience. Brasington takes it from Buddhadāsa Bhikkhu (pp. 40–41). Jones: "An alternative interpretation of the twelve _nidān&#x61;_&#x73;, made in various ways by exegetes old and new, applies the teaching specifically to the working of the mind in the present moment."[^11] SN 12.20's closing passage, which turns the disciple away from questions about past, future and present selves, supports a present-tense reading. The three-life reading, however, is still orthodox in Theravāda. Brasington himself notes that Ven. Payutto finds about a third of the sutta teachings on dependent origination refer to it (p. 39).

**Any scale.** Varela, Thompson and Rosch's [[The Embodied Mind|*The Embodied Mind*]] declines to choose: "The circle is an analytic structure that can be used to describe events of any duration from a single moment to a lifetime or, in the Buddhist view, to many lifetimes." (p. 111)[^12] Its authors place the point of freedom one link later than Brasington does. Brasington puts it between feeling and craving (p. 19); they put it between craving and grasping: "Craving is an extremely important juncture in this chain of causality. Up to this point, the links have rolled off automatically on the basis of past conditioning. At this point, however, the aware person can do something about the situation: he can interrupt the chain or he can let it go on to the next link (grasping)." (p. 114)[^12]

## 2. Emptiness in the early discourses

### The world is empty of self (SN 35.85)

Segments 1.2–1.4 in full:

> “Sir, they say that ‘the world is empty’. What does the saying ‘the world is empty’ refer to?” “Ānanda, they say that ‘the world is empty’ because it’s empty of self or what belongs to self.
>
> _“‘suñño loko, suñño loko’ti, bhante, vuccati. Kittāvatā nu kho, bhante, suñño lokoti vuccatī”ti? “Yasmā ca kho, ānanda, suññaṁ attena vā attaniyena vā tasmā suñño lokoti vuccati._[^13]

In this early sense, "empty" means empty _of_ something: of a self (_atta_) and of what belongs to a self (_attaniya_). The sutta applies this to each sense, its objects, its consciousness, its contact and the feeling that arises from contact.

### Emptiness as a meditative descent (MN 121)

The _Cūḷasuññata Sutta_ describes emptiness as a series of meditative stages. At each stage the mind notices what is absent and what remains. The formula repeated at each stage, segments 4.10–4.11:

> And so they regard it as empty of what is not there, but as to what remains they understand that it is present. That’s how emptiness manifests in them—genuine, undistorted, and pure.
>
> _Iti yañhi kho tattha na hoti tena taṁ suññaṁ samanupassati, yaṁ pana tattha avasiṭṭhaṁ hoti taṁ ‘santamidaṁ atthī’ti pajānāti. Evampissa esā, ānanda, yathābhuccā avipallatthā parisuddhā suññatāvakkanti bhavati._[^14]

This is emptiness as a practice of attention rather than a thesis about things. SN 35.85 and MN 121 also qualify a claim in _The Embodied Mind_: "Theravada Buddhism does not teach sunyata." (p. 220)[^12] That is true of emptiness in the Madhyamaka sense of lacking intrinsic nature. It is not true of emptiness as such, which the Pāli discourses teach in the senses shown here. It is close to Brasington's "levels" of emptiness experience (pp. 78–80), which he explicitly offers as a map and not a doctrine.

### The middle way between "exists" and "does not exist" (SN 12.15)

This is the one early discourse that Nāgārjuna names. Segments 2.1–2.3 and 3.1–3.4:

> “Kaccāna, this world mostly relies on the duality of existence and non-existence. But for one who truly sees the origin of the world with right understanding, the concept of non-existence regarding the world does not occur. And for one who truly sees the cessation of the world with right understanding, the concept of existence regarding the world does not occur. \[...] ‘All exists’: this is one extreme. ‘All does not exist’: this is the second extreme. Avoiding these two extremes, the Realized One teaches by the middle way: ‘Ignorance is a requirement for choices.
>
> _“Dvayanissito khvāyaṁ, kaccāna, loko yebhuyyena—atthitañceva natthitañca. Lokasamudayaṁ kho, kaccāna, yathābhūtaṁ sammappaññāya passato yā loke natthitā sā na hoti. Lokanirodhaṁ kho, kaccāna, yathābhūtaṁ sammappaññāya passato yā loke atthitā sā na hoti. \[...] ‘Sabbamatthī’ti kho, kaccāna, ayameko anto. ‘Sabbaṁ natthī’ti ayaṁ dutiyo anto. Ete te, kaccāna, ubho ante anupagamma majjhena tathāgato dhammaṁ deseti: ‘avijjāpaccayā saṅkhārā;_[^15]

The middle way is taught _by_ dependent origination. Things arise, so "does not exist" does not hold of the world. Things cease, so "exists" does not hold either.

The Chinese parallel, _Saṃyuktāgama_ 301, contains the same teaching:

> 所以者何？世間集如實正知見，若世間無者不有，世間滅如實正知見，若世間有者無有，是名離於二邊說於中道，所謂此有故彼有，此起故彼起，謂緣無明行，乃至純大苦聚集，無明滅故行滅，乃至純大苦聚滅。
>
> Why is this? When one rightly knows and sees the arising of the world as it really is, \[the view that] the world does not exist does not occur; when one rightly knows and sees the ceasing of the world as it really is, \[the view that] the world exists does not occur. This is called teaching by the middle, free of the two extremes: namely, because this exists, that exists; because this arises, that arises; that is, conditioned by ignorance are formations, up to the arising of this whole great mass of suffering; with the ceasing of ignorance, formations cease, up to the ceasing of this whole great mass of suffering.[^16]

This parallel matters for Brasington's claim about SN 12.15 (Section 6). The Chinese version joins the general formula ("because this exists, that exists") to the twelve links, just as the Pāli does.

## 3. Nāgārjuna: dependent origination _is_ emptiness

### The central verse (MMK 24.18–19)

In the _Mūlamadhyamakakārikā_ (_Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way_), chapter 24, Nāgārjuna answers an opponent who says emptiness destroys the Four Noble Truths. His reply identifies dependent origination with emptiness.

The Sanskrit of 24.18, as the Taishō editors give it in their note to Kumārajīva's translation:

> _Yaḥ pratītyasamutpādaḥ śūnyatāṃ tāṃ pracakṣmahe, sā prajñaptir upādāya pratipat saiva madhyamā._[^17]

Kumārajīva's Chinese, verses 24.18–19 (T 30, 33b11–14):

> 眾因緣生法，我說即是無，
> 亦為是假名，亦是中道義。
> 未曾有一法，不從因緣生，
> 是故一切法，無不是空者。[^18]

In 24.18 the Taishō text reads 無 ("non-existent"). The commentary that follows repeats the verse with 空 ("empty"), and the editors' note glosses the word as _śūnyatā_.

A gloss of the two verses (Claude's): _Whatever is dependently arisen, that we call emptiness. It is a dependent designation, and just that is the middle way. There is no phenomenon that is not dependently arisen, so there is no phenomenon that is not empty._

Jay Garfield's translation, as quoted by Brasington (p. 65), translates from the Tibetan:

> “Whatever is dependently co-arisen
> That is explained to be emptiness.
> That, being a dependent designation,
> Is itself the middle way.”[^19]

Mark Siderits and Shōryū Katsura translate the _Kārikā_ from the Sanskrit together with the Indian commentaries, which makes their version the better one for close work on the wording.[^20]

### The emptiness of emptiness

The commentary in Kumārajīva's _Zhong lun_ explains the verse:

> 眾緣具足和合而物生，是物屬眾因緣故無自性，無自性故空，空亦復空。
>
> When the many conditions are complete and come together, a thing arises. Because this thing belongs to its many conditions, it has no self-nature; because it has no self-nature, it is empty; and emptiness too is empty.[^21]

Garfield argues that this is why Nāgārjuna opens the _Kārikā_ with causation (ch. 1). Emptiness is not a hidden substance behind things. It is itself empty, a conventional designation that is true of dependently arisen things and has no separate existence. On this reading dependent origination and emptiness are the same fact seen from two sides.[^22] Jan Westerhoff's philosophical introduction treats _svabhāva_ ("intrinsic nature") as the key term. Emptiness is the absence of _svabhāva_, not the absence of things.[^23] Brasington makes the same point in plain language (p. 57). So do Varela, Thompson and Rosch: "Nagarjuna’s point is not to say that things are nonexistent in an absolute way any more than to say that they are existent. Things are codependently originated; they are completely groundless." (p. 223) Their summary of the argument ends: "We now have a context for understanding emptiness with respect to codependent origination: all things are empty of any independent intrinsic nature." (p. 224)[^12]

Jones summarises Nāgārjuna's position: "Nāgārjuna’s philosophy interprets dependent arising not as an ultimately real feature of the world, but as a conventional designation for the causal processes that characterize the world of phenomena, which is empty of intrinsic existence and depends for its intelligibility on the mind (Westerhoff 2013)."[^11]

### Form is emptiness (_Heart Sūtra_)

Xuanzang's Chinese, T 8, 848c07–08:

> 「舍利子！色不異空，空不異色，色即是空，空即是色；受、想、行、識，亦復如是。
>
> "Śāriputra! Form is not other than emptiness; emptiness is not other than form. Form is emptiness; emptiness is form. So too are feeling, perception, formations and consciousness."[^24]

Brasington notes that SN 35.85 already says forms are empty, centuries before the _Heart Sūtra_ (p. 77). The words match, but the sense has widened. In SN 35.85, forms are empty _of self_. In the _Heart Sūtra_, read through Madhyamaka, form is empty of _intrinsic nature_, and emptiness in turn is not found apart from form.

## 4. Interdependence: a later layer

Brasington's SODAPI rests on the idea that everything is interrelated (pp. 51–52, 62–63). Bhikkhu Anālayo argues that this idea should be kept separate from early dependent arising. His definition: "The Buddhist teaching on dependent arising (or dependent origination) concerns specific conditions whose presence is indispensable for something to come into existence."[^25]

On the later idea: "In later times, Buddhist exegesis developed various perspectives on causality, where in Huayan philosophy in particular the notion of interconnectedness or interdependence arose, according to which all phenomena relate to each other in one way or another. Despite its traction in the contemporary setting, this notion needs to be recognized as a later development that is by no means identical with the basic Buddhist teaching on dependent arising."[^25]

Jones makes a related point about scope: "I consider how the principle of conditionality is not a quasi-scientific theory that applies to everything, but a philosophical principle that applies primarily to experience."[^11] He treats the ecological reading of dependent arising as interdependence, associated with Joanna Macy among others, as a dimension of Buddhist modernism, and notes historical and conceptual difficulties with it (§ 9).

Brasington's own caveats partly meet this criticism. "One of the most important things to keep in mind when exploring any of the Buddha's teachings is that he was a phenomenologist, not a metaphysicist." (p. 8) Of SODAPI he writes: "SODAPI is a concept that hopefully can be useful as you deeply examine reality so that you can understand what you are experiencing. But it's not metaphysics either." (p. 52) Still, his step from dependent arising as a universal principle (p. 48) to an indivisibly interrelated universe (pp. 62, 82) is closer to Huayan than to the Nikāyas.

_The Embodied Mind_ shows both pulls. Its gloss on the twelve links already reads them as mutual: "The Buddha was said to have discovered on the eve of his enlightenment not only the momentariness of the arising of the aggregates but also the entire edifice of causality—the circular structure of habitual patterns, the binding chain, each link of which conditions and is conditioned by each of the others—that constitutes the pattern of human life as a never-ending circular quest to anchor experience in a fixed and permanent self." (p. 110) Yet of Nāgārjuna it insists: "It is a very precise argument, not just a general handwaving that everything is dependent on everything." (p. 225)[^12]

## 5. Science and philosophy of mind

These parallels are structural. None of the studies tests a Buddhist doctrine, and none shows that emptiness is true.

### Relational physics

In relational quantum mechanics, Carlo Rovelli argues that physical states are defined only relative to other systems: "The notion rejected here is the notion of absolute, or observer-independent, state of a system; equivalently, the notion of observer-independent values of physical quantities."[^26] Brasington quotes Rovelli's popular book _Helgoland_ on reality as a web of interactions (p. 52). Michael Peters discusses the three-way comparison of Wittgenstein, Nāgārjuna and relational quantum mechanics.[^27]

The comparison is suggestive. A physical theory about relative states is still not a proof of the Madhyamaka claim that nothing has _svabhāva_.

### The constructed self

Varela, Thompson and Rosch's _The Embodied Mind_ built its enactive account of cognition on Madhyamaka. In the authors' words: "In fact, it is one such important tradition, the Madhyamaka, which has served as the basis for our thought in this book." (p. 219) The authors take care not to identify science with emptiness: "We offer the vision of enactive cognitive science and of evolution as natural drift neither as a claim that this is the only way science can be done nor as a claim that this is the very same thing as Madhyamaka." (p. 228)[^12]

Dahl, Lutz and Davidson group meditation practices into three families: attentional, constructive and deconstructive. The deconstructive family uses self-inquiry to expose how models of self and world are built. That is close to the early practice of seeing the aggregates as "not self".[^28]

### Proliferation and prediction

Laukkonen and Slagter propose, within predictive processing, that meditation progressively loosens the layers of abstract prediction through which experience is built. On their account the mind moves "from many to (n)one".[^29] This fits MN 18's chain from contact to proliferation, which [[Mental and Conceptual Proliferation - prapañca - papanca - Chaotic Thoughts]] covers. It also fits MN 121's stepwise emptying of perception.

### The default mode network

For his point about the default mode network, Brasington cites Wikipedia and _Quanta Magazine_ (pp. 80–81). A primary source for the meditation claim is Brewer and colleagues' small cross-sectional study, which found lower activity in the main default-network hubs of experienced meditators.[^30] As [[Mental and Conceptual Proliferation - prapañca - papanca - Chaotic Thoughts]] notes, such findings are provisional.
