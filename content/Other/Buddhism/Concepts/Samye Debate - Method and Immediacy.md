# The Samye Debate: Method and Immediacy

## Summary

At the end of the eighth century the Tibetan court heard two answers to one question: does awakening have to be **made**, or only **uncovered**? The Indian scholar Kamalaśīla argued that liberating knowledge grows from cultivated causes — study, reflection, analytical meditation, merit and compassion. The Chinese Chan master Heshang Moheyan argued that the mind is already awake, that conceptual activity — even virtuous activity — keeps beings circling, and that for people of sharp faculties, resting without conceptual fabrication is itself the path.[^1]

Three corrections shape this note.

1. **The argument was about sūtra-level Mahāyāna practice.** The tantric subtle body — channels, winds, drops, knots — belongs to a later and separate layer. Projecting it back onto Samye misreads both sides.[^2]
2. **The event is known through sources that contradict each other.** Whether a single staged debate took place at all is disputed.[^3]
3. **"Sudden" and "gradual" did not stay on their original sides.** The traditions that claimed the sudden side — Chan, Zen, Dzogchen, Mahāmudrā — each built a gradual scaffold around it. The gradualists, for their part, placed a non-conceptual knowing at the end of their path.

The science of insight fits this pattern without settling it. Moments of insight are experienced as sudden, yet they are preceded by measurable preparation. Meditation research separates practices that _construct_ qualities from practices that _deconstruct_ habitual cognition, and psychology treats genuine blankness as a different state from open awareness.[^4]

---

## 1. Setting

Trisong Detsen (Khri srong lde btsan, r. 755–797) presided; Samye (bSam yas) was Tibet's first Buddhist monastery.[^5] Modern dates run from about 792–794, the window in which Kamalaśīla probably wrote his _Bhāvanākramas_ in Tibet, to about 797.[^6] The label "Council of Lhasa" was popularised by the title of Paul Demiéville's 1952 study, the first published translation of the Chinese account; Tibetan tradition sets the events at Samye.[^7]

What was at stake, in the terms the sources give, was which Buddhism the court would adopt: the Chan of Chinese meditation masters, or the scholastic Buddhism of India's great monastic universities.[^8] In the Tibetan telling, the Indian side stood for the six perfections, traversed through ten stages to a gradual awakening; the Chinese side for a single method, the realisation of the mind's nature.[^9] Tibetan authors call the two positions the simultaneous approach (_cig car gyi 'jug pa_) and the gradual approach (_rim gyis 'jug pa_). "Simultaneous" means first that all methods are contained in one and all stages traversed at once; the temporal sense, "sudden", is secondary.[^10]

## 2. Three sources, three stories

### The _Testament of Ba_ (_dBa' bzhed_)

The earliest Tibetan narrative, and the source for every later Tibetan history of the debate; a fragment survives among the Dunhuang manuscripts.[^11] It is not a neutral record. The Chinese position gets a brief paragraph, the Indian rebuttal runs for pages, and much of that rebuttal is spoken by a nobleman of the Ba clan — whose chief rivals, the Dro, appear to have patronised Chan teachers.[^12] Read closely, its earliest versions reject **Moheyan's particular meditation practice**, not Chinese Buddhism as such; the same text reports a member of the Ba clan receiving teachings from the Korean Chan master known as Reverend Kim.[^13]

### Wangxi's _Dunwu dasheng zhengli jue_ (頓悟大乘正理決)

A Chinese manuscript from Dunhuang (Pelliot chinois 4646), presented as a record by Moheyan's disciple Wangxi, first translated by Demiéville.[^14] Here the opponents are unnamed "Brahmin monks", the exchange runs **by letter over several months**, and it ends with an imperial edict declaring Moheyan's Chan faithful to the sūtras and permitting monks and laity to practise it. It also says Moheyan came at the invitation of a queen from the Dro clan.[^15]

### Bu ston's _History of Buddhism_ (_Chos 'byung_)

The normative later account, by Bu ston Rin chen grub (1290–1364). Moheyan opens by comparing virtuous and non-virtuous acts to **white and black clouds that equally obscure the sky**, and concludes that ending all mental activity liberates at once. Kamalaśīla answers with the stages of analytical meditation, arguing that even non-conceptual wisdom comes out of a specific process of analysis. The frame is openly partisan: a deathbed prophecy by Śāntarakṣita summons Kamalaśīla, some of Moheyan's humiliated disciples take their own lives, and the story closes with Moheyan sending men to murder Kamalaśīla.[^16]

### What the scholarship concludes

Gómez and Ruegg questioned whether a formal debate happened; van Schaik suggests a series of consultations and written exchanges; Dalton notes that every source nevertheless agrees that a debate of some kind took place.[^17] A tenth-century Tibetan treatise devoted entirely to ranking the simultaneous and gradual methods — ==Nubchen Sangyé Yeshé's \*Lamp for the Eyes of Contemplation\*== (_bSam gtan mig sgron_) — does not mention a debate at all.[^18]

The political reading is at least as strong as the doctrinal one. Dalton suggests that the Indian framework, with its law-like account of karma, suited a court building a new political order, while the antinomian edge of popular Chan teaching threatened it.[^19]

## 3. Moheyan in his own texts

The Dunhuang caves preserved Tibetan and Chinese texts attributed to Moheyan, pieced together by Luis Gómez and studied by Japanese scholars, notably Ueyama Daishun.[^20] In one meditation instruction (IOL Tib J 468), ==Moheyan tells the student to look at his own mind: the absence of all mental activity is non-thought. But thought does move, and he does not ask for it to be suppressed:==

> "If there is movement of the conceptual mind, be aware of it."[^21]
>
> _rtog pa’I sems g.yos na tshor bar bya/_

The word for awareness, _tshor_, renders the Chinese _jue_ 覺 (awakening, awareness). What is to be dropped is **analysis** of the movement — as moving or still, existent or non-existent, virtuous or non-virtuous, defiled or pure.[^22] ==He also reserves this method for people of sharp faculties; others, he says, need graduated methods==.[^23]

Scholars read these fragments differently. Dalton finds they support the traditional picture of an uncompromising advocate of immediacy; van Schaik finds a teacher who asks for awareness rather than suppression, and who is far more moderate than his legend.[^24]

==Doctrinally, the Chinese account leans on buddha-nature== (_tathāgatagarbha_), which Kamalaśīla never mentions, ==and is silent on compassion and the stages of meditation==, both central for Kamalaśīla. Dalton reads each side as addressing debates at home more than each other.[^25] Moheyan's own milieu was the polemical eighth-century Chinese quarrel between self-styled "Southern" suddenists and the "Northern" lineage to which Dunhuang documents connect him (see §6).[^26]

## 4. Kamalaśīla: meditation as cultivation

Kamalaśīla (c. 740–795) wrote three short treatises, all titled _Bhāvanākrama_ (_The Process of Meditation_). The third opens by addressing **beginners in the way of the Mahāyāna sūtras**, and subsumes every kind of concentration under calm (_śamatha_) and insight (_vipaśyanā_).[^27] ==Calm is one-pointedness of mind; insight is \*bhūtapratyavekṣā\*, "the discernment of reality==".[^28]

His case, as Martin Adam reconstructs it:[^29]

- **Three wisdoms in sequence.** ==Wisdom from study opens the meaning of scripture; wisdom from reflection sorts definitive from provisional meaning; wisdom from meditation realises what reflection has ascertained — like a horse running a course already marked out==.[^30]
- **Insight is meditative, not merely intellectual.** The discernment of reality is conceptual analysis performed _within_ concentration, compared to examining blemishes on one's face in a mirror. Translating it as ordinary reasoning, Adam warns, gives an overly intellectual picture of the whole Tibetan meditative tradition built on these texts.[^31]
- **Analysis consumes itself.** Quoting a sūtra, Kamalaśīla says that the knowledge born from discernment burns the wood of concepts, as friction-fire burns the fire-sticks that made it. Whoever merely abandons mental activity without analysing, he insists, will never get free of concepts.[^32]
- **Action matters.** To the claim that no wholesome action need be done, he retorts that this is the Ājīvaka doctrine of liberation through the exhaustion of karma.[^33]

Adam isolates the crux. Kamalaśīla's objection concerns **knowledge**: non-conceptual gnosis cannot arise from a practice with no cognitive content. Moheyan's concerns **action**: deliberate, volitional practice is karma, and karma binds. Both agreed that concentration comes first; they disagreed about what kind.[^34] Later Tibetan polemic sharpened this into the jibe that non-mentation leaves the mind like an egg — a line that seems to go back to the rebuttal in the _Testament of Ba_.[^35]

## 5. The loser's long afterlife in Tibet

In the Tibetan histories Moheyan is banished and Indian Buddhism becomes the religion of state.[^36] Yet many, perhaps all, of the Tibetan Chan manuscripts from Dunhuang date from **after** the empire, which suggests Chan declined in Tibet slowly and for other reasons, rather than ending by decree.[^37]

**Ranked, not rejected.** Nubchen's tenth-century _Lamp for the Eyes of Contemplation_ places the simultaneous approach of Chan above the gradual path, but below the Vajrayāna and the Great Perfection. Nubchen had access to the same kind of Chan texts that survived at Dunhuang, and quotes the lines on awareness cited above.[^38]

**An emblem of error.** For later scholastics, "the Hwashang" became shorthand for the idea that awakening follows from simply stopping concepts, and a charge to throw at Mahāmudrā and Dzogchen.[^39] Sakya Paṇḍita (1182–1251) attacked the Kagyu "Self-Sufficient White Remedy" (_dkar po chig thub_) — the claim that recognising the mind's nature alone accomplishes the whole path — for replacing the union of method and emptiness with bare non-elaboration, and likened it to the Great Perfection of the Chinese tradition (_rgya nag lugs kyi rdzogs chen_).[^40] Khedrupjé (1385–1438) accused Tibetan meditators who speak of meditation free of all mentation of reviving the Hwashang's thesis.[^41] In ritual dance, Hashang survives as a figure of fun.[^42]

**Nyingma defences.** Nyangral Nyima Özer (1124–1192) has the emperor say the two paths do not ultimately differ: simultaneous entry for the best faculties, the graded path for the rest. Longchenpa (1308–1363) adopts Hashang's cloud simile and says that what the great master taught was true, even if lesser minds could not take it in. Jigmé Lingpa (1730–1798) distinguishes the conceptual mind (_sems_) from awareness (_rig pa_): stopping thought without that distinction yields only a blank indeterminacy (_lung ma bstan_), while awareness has the clarity of a crystal ball — and yet he adds that only a perfect buddha can know what Hashang's view really was.[^43]

Van Schaik's conclusion is pointed: these authors honour simultaneous realisation while placing it, in practice, out of reach of almost everyone living; Katog Tsewang Norbu (1698–1755) went further, treating the Great Perfection as inherently gradual and simultaneism as a Chinese phenomenon.[^44] A modern Dzogchen teacher, Namkhai Norbu, reading Nubchen, explains the fault attributed to Chan as, at root, a grasping at ultimate truth.[^45]

## 6. The same argument inside Chan and Zen

The sudden–gradual quarrel did not travel from China to Tibet so much as break out in both.

- **Shenhui (684–758)** attacked the imperially patronised lineage of Shenxiu as "gradualist", promoted Huineng as Sixth Patriarch, and won: by the ninth century "Southern" sudden awakening was the accepted line. McRae was among the first to take apart the traditional North–South story.[^46]
- **The _Platform Sūtra_** hedges its own slogan. The Dharma, it says, has no sudden or gradual; people are sharp or dull, and the names follow from that. Thompson suggests _dun_ 頓 might be better rendered "poised" or "ready" than "sudden", and stresses that Huineng's no-thought (_wunian_ 無念) is not insentience but an unentangled attentiveness.[^47]
- **Zongmi (780–841)** formalised a settlement that later Tibetan authors echo: sudden awakening followed by gradual cultivation.[^48]
- **Dōgen (1200–1253)** dissolved the sequence altogether: practice and realisation are one (_shushō ittō_ 修証一等). Zazen is not a means to a later awakening but awakening practised — which removes the finish line while making practice endless.[^49]
- **Hakuin Ekaku (1686–1769)**, champion of the kōan breakthrough, fell into "Zen sickness" (_zenbyō_ 禅病) and recovered through inner contemplation (_naikan_ 内観) and the "soft-butter" visualisation (_nanso_ 軟酥), gathering vital energy (_ki_ 気) in the _tanden_ (丹田, cinnabar field) below the navel. He recorded the methods in _Yasenkanna_ (夜船閑話, _Idle Talk on a Night Boat_).[^50] Zen kept its own somatic technology.

Bernard Faure's _The Rhetoric of Immediacy_ generalises the point: Chan's language of unmediated awakening coexisted with, and depended on, ritual, lineage and institution.[^51] Thompson likewise notes that the _Platform Sūtra_'s sudden awakening takes place inside a ritual setting.[^52]

## 7. Where the subtle body actually belongs

The earlier draft framed Samye as "Vajrayāna completion stage versus Zen". That is anachronistic.

- **Chronology.** A developed conceptual structure of the subtle body is first attested in Indian materials from the seventh and eighth centuries onward, within the emerging tantras. Kamalaśīla's manuals are sūtra-based and written for beginners.[^53] The completion-stage systems that Tibetans later practised — above all the Six Dharmas of Nāropa, descending from Indian adepts such as Tilopa (988–1069) — belong to the eleventh century and after.[^54]
- **The numbers.** The figure of **72,000 channels** is older than Buddhist tantra: the _Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad_ already speaks of 72,000 channels running from the heart.[^55] The number **twenty-four** belongs most prominently to the twenty-four sacred sites of the Cakrasaṃvara maṇḍala, which practitioners map onto their own bodies.[^56] No source consulted gives "24 karmic knots" (see _To review_).
- **Where method-versus-immediacy lives in Tibet.** Kagyu teaching pairs a **path of means** (_thabs lam_: the Six Dharmas of Nāropa, such as inner heat and illusory body) with a **path of liberation** (_grol lam_: Mahāmudrā, recognising the mind's nature).[^57] Sakya Paṇḍita's complaint was precisely that some Kagyu teachers presented the second as sufficient without the first.[^58] And Dunne shows that even the most "effortless" Mahāmudrā instruction is framed by long, highly conceptual and effortful preliminaries.[^59]

Science does bear on the method side. Benson's team reported striking rises in peripheral temperature in _tummo_ (_gtum mo_, inner heat) practitioners; Kozhevnikov and colleagues later recorded rises into the slight-to-moderate fever range (up to 38.3 °C at the armpit), produced by forceful "vase" breathing and sustained by visualisation. They suggest the heat is neither merely a by-product of meditation nor its goal, but a means to deep meditative states.[^60] Zen _tanden_ breathing likewise slows respiration and reshapes heart-rate variability.[^61] Subtle-body practices are physiologically real techniques. What they measure, though, is heat and autonomic change — not awakening.

## 8. What the science can and cannot say

### Sudden experience, gradual machinery

- In verbal insight problems, the solution arrives as an "aha!", but EEG shows a burst of gamma activity over the right anterior temporal lobe roughly 300 ms before insight solutions, preceded by a burst of alpha over posterior cortex, read as a brief damping of visual input; fMRI places the effect in the right anterior superior temporal gyrus.[^62] Brain states _before_ a problem is presented already bias whether it will be solved by insight or by analysis.[^63]
- Stephen and colleagues found that participants' spontaneous discovery of a mathematical rule was predicted by prior changes in entropy and power-law behaviour — hallmarks of a phase transition. The reorganisation is sudden; the conditions for it accumulate.[^64]

Kamalaśīla's fire-sticks are a good pre-modern image of exactly this: the friction is gradual, the ignition is sudden, and the fire consumes what produced it.

### Constructing, deconstructing, releasing

- Dahl, Lutz and Davidson sort meditation into **attentional**, **constructive** and **deconstructive** families. Constructive practices cultivate qualities such as compassion; deconstructive practices use self-inquiry to generate insight into one's models of self and world.[^65] On this map, Kamalaśīla needs all three families, while Moheyan's instruction sits closest to attentional practice with the analytic step deliberately withheld (Claude's framing).
- Dunne proposes **Innateism** and **Constructivism** as the ends of a spectrum — the Tibetan _cig car ba_ and _rim bzhin pa_ — with non-dual traditions leaning Innateist.[^66] A Mahāmudrā instruction he translates shows the Innateist style in miniature: "Do not pursue the past. Do not usher in the future. Rest evenly within present awareness, clear and nonconceptual."[^67] His historical note is striking: in eleventh-century India, the Mahāmudrā master Maitrīpa made _amanasikāra_ — "non-attention", the very term Kamalaśīla had attacked — a mark of correct meditation.[^68]
- In predictive-processing terms, Laukkonen and Slagter describe deconstructive meditation as progressively reducing abstract, temporally deep modelling of the world. Insight arises through model reduction; non-dual awareness is the limit, the "here and now".[^69] Again: gradual pruning, sudden simplification.

### Blankness is the goal of neither side

- Ward and Wegner showed mind-blanking to be a distinct state — attention failing to bring anything into awareness — separate from mind-wandering. Sometimes the mind only seems absent because we failed to monitor it; sometimes it is actually gone.[^70]
- That gives empirical weight to a worry both camps shared. Tibetan polemic compares non-mentation to an egg; Jigmé Lingpa calls unexamined thought-stopping blank indeterminacy; the Mahāmudrā manual Dunne analyses warns against absorption in thoughtless nothingness; and Moheyan himself tells the meditator to be aware of moving thought.[^71]
- Lindahl and colleagues documented a wide range of challenging meditation experiences among Western Theravāda, Zen and Tibetan practitioners, and found that their interpretation depended heavily on tradition and teachers. Traditions have their own words for such experiences — the Zen _makyō_ (魔境, perceptual disturbances in meditation) and the Tibetan _nyams_.[^72] Hakuin's Zen sickness is an early first-person record of the same territory.

### Limits

Science measures attention, temperature, oscillations and self-report. It cannot test omniscience, buddhahood or the metaphysics of buddha-nature. What the data support is narrower: **sudden transformations in complex systems usually ride on gradual preparation.** That finding is compatible both with "awakening is produced" and with "awakening is revealed once interference stops".

## 9. Synthesis: five questions inside one debate

| Question | Kamalaśīla (gradual) | Moheyan (simultaneous) | Later answers |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Is awakening innate or constructed? | Its qualities must be cultivated | The mind is already awake, only obscured | A spectrum; most traditions sit between the poles (Dunne) |
| What do concepts do? | Analysis burns concepts from within | Concepts, even virtuous ones, obscure like clouds | Mahāmudrā and Dzogchen release thought but separate awareness from blankness |
| Does deliberate action help or bind? | Merit and compassion are indispensable | Volitional practice is karma | Zongmi: cultivate after awakening; Dōgen: practice _is_ realisation |
| Who is the teaching for? | Beginners in the sūtra way | The sharp-witted | _Platform Sūtra_, Nyangral, Jigmé Lingpa: simultaneous entry for the few |
| What did the court need? | A law-like karmic order | Patronage from a rival clan | Clan politics shaped the record (van Schaik, Dalton) |

Across twelve centuries the positions converge on a narrow remainder. The gradualist concedes that the path ends in a knowing that is not built from concepts; the suddenist concedes that almost everyone needs a long preparation, and that "not thinking" misunderstood is only blankness. What remains contested is whether the culmination is **caused** by the path, or **uncovered** when the path stops interfering — and whether the path itself should be felt as a climb or as the summit already underfoot.
