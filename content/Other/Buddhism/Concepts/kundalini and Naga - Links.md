# Kuṇḍalinī and Nāga — Academic Links

A focused examination of the scholarly, etymological, textual, cosmological, and embodied connections between kuṇḍalinī and nāga across Indian and Buddhist traditions.

---

## The Etymology Holds the Thread

The academic connection between kuṇḍalinī and nāga begins at the level of language itself — before cosmology, before iconography, before practice.

The Sanskrit adjective _kuṇḍalin_ means "circular, annular." _Kuṇḍalinī_ as a noun means "coiled" — ring, bracelet, the coil of a rope. Critically, the root noun _kuṇḍa_ — meaning "bowl" or "water-pot" — is attested in the Cologne Digital Sanskrit Dictionaries (Śabda-Sāgara) as the name of a Nāga in the Mahābhārata (1.4828): "_Kuṇḍa_ — the name of a Nāga or serpent." The word naming the inner serpentine energy of the subtle body shares its etymological root with a named Nāga deity. These two figures — the cosmic serpent-being of Buddhist and Hindu cosmology, and the coiled energy at the base of the human spine — emerge from the same Sanskrit ground.

David Gordon White (University of California Santa Barbara), in his scholarship on the tantric traditions, glosses the 8th-century _Tantrasadbhava Tantra_'s use of _kuṇḍalī_ as "she who is ring-shaped" — the first named appearance of the term in a tantric context. The _Śāradatilaka_ (11th c.) then uses _kuṇḍalī_ as a name for Goddess Durga, a form of Śakti, establishing the serpentine energy as divine feminine in the early Śākta tantric literature.

In the Kaula Śākta tradition, the scholar Christopher Wallis identifies Kuṇḍalinī as "the innate intelligence of embodied Consciousness" — the supreme Goddess residing in the six chakras along the central channel. The correspondence embedded in Sanskrit itself points to a shared cultural substrate: the serpent as the form that concentrated vital force takes when it gathers and waits.

## Kuṇḍalinī as Nāgī — The Inner Serpent Goddess

Joseph Campbell, in _The Mythic Image_ (Princeton University Press, 1974) and _Inner Reaches of Outer Space_ (Alfred van der Marck, 1986), describes kuṇḍalinī as "the figure of a coiled female serpent — a serpent goddess not of 'gross' but 'subtle' substance — which is to be thought of as residing in a torpid, slumbering state in a subtle center, the first of the seven, near the base of the spine: the aim of the yoga then being to rouse this serpent, lift her head, and bring her up a subtle nerve or channel of the spine to the so-called 'thousand-petaled lotus' (Sahasrāra) at the crown of the head."

Campbell's framing makes explicit what the tantric texts imply: kuṇḍalinī is structurally a nāgī — a female nāga — inhabiting the subtle body. She shares the nāga's defining characteristics: coiled in the underground of the body (the nāga's domain is subterranean and aquatic), holding latent force that can destroy or protect (the nāga's venom and its shelter over the meditating Buddha), rising only when conditions have been prepared. The internal and external nāga are the same figure, one cosmological and one somatic.

Arthur Avalon (Sir John Woodroffe), in _The Serpent Power: The Secrets of Tantric and Shaktic Yoga_ (Ganesh & Co., 1918) — the foundational Western scholarly study of kuṇḍalinī — defines the energy philosophically as "consciousness: the power of matter to know itself." This formulation dissolves the distinction between the serpentine force and awareness itself. Kuṇḍalinī is not a thing that moves through consciousness; it is consciousness in its most concentrated, embodied, materially instantiated form. Avalon's work remains the primary philological reference for Western scholarship on kuṇḍalinī, drawing directly on the _Ṣaṭcakranirūpaṇa_ (c. 1577) and the _Pādukāpañcaka_, two Sanskrit treatises on laya yoga.

## Ananta-Śeṣa, Patañjali, and the Yoga Sūtras

One of the most academically significant convergences of nāga and kuṇḍalinī occurs in the figure of Patañjali, compiler of the _Yoga Sūtras_ — the foundational text of classical yoga philosophy.

Patañjali is traditionally depicted as half-human, half-serpent: a nāga. He is held by the tradition to be an incarnation of Ananta-Śeṣa (also called Adishesha), the cosmic thousand-headed serpent upon whom Vishnu reclines across the primal ocean. The New World Encyclopedia (Patañjali entry) states: "Patañjali is considered to be an incarnation of the serpent Ananta, whose name means 'the endless one'... Patañjali himself is usually depicted as half human and half serpent, with the human torso emerging from the coils of the all-powerful serpent who is awakening in the moment of creation." The Siddhar Thirumoolar, a contemporary of Patañjali in the South Indian Siddha tradition, describes him as one of the eighteen masters of Kundalini Yoga (New World Encyclopedia, Patañjali).

The _Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā_ (Svātmārāma, 15th c.) traces its lineage to Matsyendranātha of the Nātha tradition — the same lineage that passes through the Mahasiddhas and their caṇḍalī practices (Wikipedia, Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā). The Bogar Mahā Rishi's _Bhogar 7000_ lists Patañjali explicitly as a Siddhar and master of kuṇḍalinī yoga (New World Encyclopedia, Patañjali). The serpent-sage who codified yoga is himself a nāga — the wisdom of the inner serpentine force embodied in the one who mapped the path to its perfection.

The cosmological mapping is precise. Shesha's thousand heads correspond to the Sahasrāra, the thousand-petaled lotus at the crown — the terminus of kuṇḍalinī's ascent. His single coiling body is the Suṣumnā, the central channel. His position beneath all of manifest creation maps to the mūlādhāra. In the Bhagavad Gita (10.29), Krishna states: _Anantaś ca asmi nāgānām_ — "Of the nāgas, I am Ananta." Shesha means "the Remainder" — that which persists through all pralaya (cosmic dissolution). This is the awareness that cannot be extinguished, the _Nāga_ in the epithet sense: _āgum na karotī ti Nāgo_ (Mahāniddesa 201, Pali Canon).

## Caṇḍālī / Tummo — The Buddhist Technical Form

The most rigorously established academic link between nāga-serpent energy and kuṇḍalinī in Buddhist tradition is the caṇḍālī-kuṇḍalinī correspondence.

The New World Encyclopedia (Tummo entry) states directly: "The Sanskrit terms caṇḍālī and kuṇḍalinī are clearly etymologically related." Miranda Shaw, scholar of Vajrayāna and author of _Passionate Enlightenment: Women in Tantric Buddhism_ (Princeton University Press, 1994), clarifies the historical lineage: "Kuṇḍalinī yoga in the Natha Sampradaya and Vajrayāna in Tibetan Buddhism both take their origin from the Mahasiddhas who were active in India from the 8th century to the 12th century. Kuṇḍalinī yoga was spoken of as Caṇḍalī yoga by these Mahasiddhas and became known as gTummo rnal 'byor in Tibet" (cited in New World Encyclopedia, Tummo).

Caṇḍalī yoga was a key practice of Milarepa (1040–1113), the most celebrated yogi in Tibetan history, whose name _repa_ — "cotton-robed one" — designates his mastery of tummo (Judith Simmer-Brown, "The Practice of Fierce Inner Heat," _Lion's Roar_, 2024). Dr. Pasang Yonten Arya (2006) describes the raising of _drod_ or tummo through the _tsa lung_ vortices (chakras) "in a manner comparable to the 'serpent fire' (Sanskrit: kuṇḍalinī; caṇḍalī)" (cited in New World Encyclopedia, Tummo). This establishes an unambiguous historical transmission: the inner heat practice of Tibetan Buddhist completion-stage yoga descends from the same Mahasiddha lineage as Hindu kuṇḍalinī yoga.

Tummo (_gtum mo_) means "fierce woman" in Tibetan. The Sanskrit _caṇḍālī_ carries the same meaning — fierce, savage, wild feminine. The kuṇḍalinī, caṇḍālī, and tummo are the same energy approached from three directions: Hindu yogic, Indian tantric, and Tibetan Buddhist. All three involve the movement of concentrated vital force through the central channel and terminate in the experience of non-dual awareness.

The _Hevajra Tantra_ — one of the foundational sources for tummo (Wikipedia, Tummo) — is also a text in which nāga beings attend the Dharma assembly. The nāgas and the inner fire practice share the same tantric textual home.

## The Nāga Universe and the Fivefold System

In Tibetan Buddhist iconography, the nāga universe is organized into five directional categories corresponding to the five elements and five colors: white warrior nāgas in the east, yellow merchant nāgas in the south, red Brahmin nāgas in the west, green laborer nāgas in the north, and black _caṇḍāla_ (outcaste) nāgas at the center (Beer, R., _The Encyclopedia of Tibetan Symbols and Motifs_, Shambhala, 1999).

The black _caṇḍāla_ nāgas at the center correspond — by name — to _caṇḍālī_, the inner fire. The most reviled social position holds the most powerful energy at the center of the cosmos. This is a consistent structural principle of tantra: the expelled, the marginal, holds the concentrated force that the social order has suppressed. The _caṇḍāla_ nāga at the center of the nāga mandala and the _caṇḍālī_ fire at the center of the subtle body are structurally identical — the energy that, when met and integrated, produces liberation. The naming is not coincidental. It is the same word.

## Shesha and the Subtle Body — Macrocosm and Microcosm

The cosmological nāga Ananta-Śeṣa maps onto kuṇḍalinī anatomy with precision that the tradition makes explicit. The Ananta-Śeṣa entry of the Nathas tradition dictionary states: "Kundalini Shakti in its latent state is depicted as a coiled snake resting in the area of the muladhara-chakra... The 1000 hoods of Shesha are an allegory of the sahasrara. Sahasrara, unlike other chakras, is located outside the human body. When Kundalini reaches this level, it no longer belongs to the realm of animal or human consciousness but becomes purely divine" (Nathas.org dictionary).

When Vishnu wakes, Shesha rises. When kuṇḍalinī is awakened, she rises through the Suṣumnā from mūlādhāra to the thousand-petaled lotus at the crown. The cosmological nāga and the subtle body energy are the same figure described at different scales. The foundational yogic axiom articulates this directly: _yathā piṇḍe, tathā brahmāṇḍe_ — "as in the body, so in the cosmos."

## The Current Scholarly Conversation

The most current academic engagement with this question is _The Serpent's Tale: Kuṇḍalinī, Yoga, and the History of an Experience_ (2025) by Sravana Borkataky-Varma (University of Houston / Center for the Study of World Religions, Harvard Divinity School) and Anya Foxen (California Polytechnic State University / Harvard Divinity School), published by Columbia University Press.

The book's structure encodes the link: Chapter 1, "South Asian Roots: Serpents, Fire, and the Ascent of Kuṇḍalinī," places the serpent at the foundation of the historical account. Borkataky-Varma and Foxen argue that there has never been one single authentic model of Kuṇḍalinī but a multiplicity of visions, ranging from esoteric texts to global gurus (Columbia University Press, 2025). This plurality opens the comparative space between nāga cosmology and inner fire practice rather than foreclosing it.

The methodological horizon is addressed by Borkataky-Varma in a 2024 research reflection at the Center for the Study of World Religions, Harvard Divinity School, invoking Jonathan Z. Smith's 1982 provocation — "in comparison, a magic dwells" — and framing comparative scholarship as the search for "hidden correspondences between the primordial chaos of what is and what people do with it" (CSWR, Harvard Divinity School, 2024). The nāga-kuṇḍalinī connection is not a comparison invented by a comparativist. It is embedded in the texts — in etymology, iconography, lineage, and practice.

## The Nāga as Kuṇḍalinī's External Form

A synthesis across these threads: the nāga of Buddhist and Hindu cosmology is the externalized, mythologized form of the same energy that yoga and tantra describe as kuṇḍalinī in the subtle body. The correspondences hold at every register.

The nāga coils in the underground realm, beneath water, at the root of the world tree — kuṇḍalinī coils at the base of the spine, in the mūlādhāra, at the root of the subtle body (_Gheranda Samhita_ 6:16: "In the muladhara is kundalini, having the form of serpent").

The nāga holds latent force that can destroy or protect — kuṇḍalinī "bestows liberation on yogins and bondage on the ignorant" (Svātmārāma, _Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā_).

The nāga rises when it recognizes the awakened mind — kuṇḍalinī rises when the practitioner creates the interior conditions for its ascent through prāṇāyāma, bandha, visualization, and samādhi (_Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā_; Six Yogas of Nāropa, as transmitted in Milarepa's lineage).

The nāga spreads its hood at the crown of a still form — kuṇḍalinī reaches the Sahasrāra at the crown of the subtle body, Shesha's thousand hoods flowering (Bhagavad Gita 10.29; Ananta-Śeṣa / Nathas tradition).

The nāga transforms into human form — kuṇḍalinī, awakened, transforms the practitioner into a being no longer bound by ordinary identity structures. Avalon: she is "the power of matter to know itself" (_The Serpent Power_, 1918).

The Buddha as _Nāga_ (_āgum na karotī ti Nāgo_, Mahāniddesa 201) is the one in whom the serpentine force has been fully perfected — not suppressed but wholly integrated into awakened awareness. The Mucalinda image — the great nāga coiling seven times around the still, awakened Buddha, sheltering him from the storm with seven hoods (Udāna 2.1, Pali Canon) — is the outer form of what the tantric traditions call the completion stage: the inner serpent fire, risen through the seven chakras, gathered at the crown, flowering into the canopy of non-dual awareness.
