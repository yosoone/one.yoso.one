# Taking The Bodhisatva Vow

## Thich Nhat Hanh

However innumerable beings are, I vow to meet them with kindness and interest.  However inexhaustible the states of suffering are, I vow to touch them with patience and love. However immeasurable the Dharmas are, I vow to explore them deeply.  However incomparable the mystery of interbeing, I vow to surrender to it freely. — Thich Nhat Hanh, Bodhisattva vow

# Background Research

The Four Great Vows (四弘誓願, _shiguseigan_) are the foundational aspiration of the Bodhisattva — the being who seeks awakening not for themselves alone, but for the liberation of all sentient beings without exception.

What makes these vows remarkable is their deliberately impossible scale. Sentient beings are _numberless_, afflictions _inexhaustible_, dharma gates _boundless_, the Buddha Way _unsurpassable_ — and yet the Bodhisattva vows to engage each one fully. The vows are not goals to be checked off; they are orientations of the heart, renewed moment to moment.

In Zen, they are typically chanted together at the close of services. In the Theravada tradition, the same aspiration lives in the _praṇidhāna_ of future Buddhas. The bodhisattva ideal reaches its fullest expression in figures like Avalokiteśvara (Kannon/Guanyin) — compassion personified, who hears the cries of the world and turns back from the threshold of nirvana.

## On origin and history

The Great Vows probably originated in China around the sixth century and may have been derived from an earlier Sanskrit verse. By the turn of the eighth century, Chinese Zen master Huineng was teaching their implications. Today they are recited at the end of services in most Mahayana centers.

The standard four-line formulation likely crystallized within Chinese Buddhism between the 5th and 7th centuries CE, with roots in earlier Mahayana scriptures, particularly the _Avatamsaka Sutra_.

## On Zhiyi and the Four Noble Truths connection

In the Tendai school, each of the four vows corresponds to one of the Four Noble Truths — saving beings corresponds to the truth of suffering, ending afflictions to the truth of arising, studying the teachings to the truth of the path, and attaining enlightenment to the truth of cessation.

## On the Zen paradox

The Mahayana versions emphasize that a Bodhisattva vows to do these impossible things while also knowing that, in emptiness, there is never any sentient being to save from the start. Realizing "no sentient beings and nothing to realize" — and helping others realize this — is precisely how one rescues them.

## On living by vow

As one Zen teacher puts it: "A bodhisattva is a person who lives by vow instead of by karma... Vow is like a magnet or compass that shows us the direction toward the Buddha."

# On English translations

Contemporary English translations of the vows rely heavily on D.T. Suzuki's version, first published in 1935.
