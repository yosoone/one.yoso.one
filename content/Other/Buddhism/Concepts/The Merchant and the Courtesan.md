# The Merchant and the Courtesan Summary by Claude

## The teaching

Kalu Rinpoche tells the story as part of a guided meditation on desire.[^1] In his framework for transforming emotions, the practitioner stops at the sensation of happiness that desire produces, without treating it as unwholesome and without trying to reject it — staying clearly aware of the joy, tasting it without being carried off by the craving to grasp the object.[^1] He then has students meditate on someone or something they find attractive, resting in the pleasant sensation the thought produces rather than acting on it.

The story itself: a merchant chief travels with traders to a city famous for a beautiful courtesan. Rather than pay for her, he tells his companions he will keep the desire and its joy without spending money or health on the object itself. The courtesan, insulted at being desired but not paid, sues him before the king for visiting her without payment. The king rules in her favour — and orders the merchant's wealth reflected on the surface of a lake, so she can be paid by the reflection of his wealth, exactly as he was satisfied by the reflection of her.[^1] Kalu Rinpoche files the practice under _śīla_, ethical conduct, one of the six pāramitās.[^1]

I have not located this story in any Buddhist canon Kalu Rinpoche might be pointing to; treat "a sutra" as his own framing rather than a verified citation. See To review in [[Trusted Sources]].

## The classical parallel: the judgment of Bocchoris

The closest documented parallel is a story Plutarch records twice-removed, as an anecdote his subject's mistress Lamia used to tell. An Egyptian loved the courtesan Thonis, who demanded a large sum for her favours. He dreamed he had enjoyed them, and his desire ceased. Thonis sued for payment, and the judge Bocchoris ordered the man to bring the sum into court and move it back and forth, so Thonis could grasp its shadow — since what is imagined is only a shadow of the real.[^2]

Lamia thought the judgment unjust: the dream ended the young man's passion, but the shadow of the money did not end the courtesan's desire for it.[^2] The objection is sharp, and it applies just as well to Kalu Rinpoche's merchant. The two reflections are not symmetrical. The merchant's inward joy genuinely satisfies him; the courtesan's reflected wealth satisfies nothing. Whatever ethical point the story is making about desire, it is made at her expense, and the story does not ask what she is owed for that.

## A wider family: payment in kind, payment in illusion

The same shape — a debt discharged with something as insubstantial as the thing purchased — recurs widely enough that folklorists give it its own motif numbers.[^3] The best-documented East Asian version is a Japanese legend, "The Case of the Stolen Smell": an innkeeper sues a poor student for enjoying the smell of his cooking without paying, and the judge rules that the price of a smell is the sound of money, made by passing coins from hand to hand.[^4] The story is often attributed to the Edo-period magistrate Ōoka Tadasuke, but that attribution should be held loosely — nearly all of the _Ōoka seidan_ legends are not historical, and many are adaptations of the Song-dynasty Chinese casebook _Tangyin bishi_ (棠陰比事) and its Japanese imitations rather than cases Ōoka actually heard.[^5] I have not found this specific tale among the small number of cases scholarship treats as genuinely his.

## Doctrinal placement

Within dependent origination, feeling (_vedanā_) conditions craving (_taṇhā_), which conditions clinging (_upādāna_). Kalu Rinpoche's method stays at the link of vedanā: the pleasant feeling-tone is fully received, but the next link — reaching for the object — is not fed.

The Pali canon has its own story about not fearing pleasure, though it points a different way. In the Mahāsaccaka Sutta, the Buddha-to-be recalls entering the first jhāna as a child under a rose-apple tree, and asks himself why he should fear a pleasure that has nothing to do with sensual pleasures or unskillful states — concluding that he is not afraid of it.[^6] The pleasure there is explicitly secluded _from_ sensuality, not drawn _from_ an object of sensual desire, which is the harder thing Kalu Rinpoche's practice attempts. That reframing — working with an afflictive emotion's energy directly rather than only renouncing it — belongs more to the later Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna transformation model than to the early canon.

## A scientific bridge

Two findings from affective neuroscience touch the same territory, with different degrees of support.

Berridge and Robinson's incentive-salience theory separates "wanting" (a motivational pull, tied to dopamine) from "liking" (the pleasure of consumption itself, mediated by smaller, more fragile systems) — a dissociation that maps loosely onto craving versus the felt joy Kalu Rinpoche asks students to rest in.[^7]

Papies, Barsalou and Custers found that a brief mindfulness procedure — observing a reaction to an attractive food image as a transient mental event rather than a real one — fully eliminated the automatic approach response to that food, an effect that held over a five-minute delay.[^8] This is close to the instruction itself: notice the pleasant sensation as a mental event, without treating it as something to chase.

Morewedge, Huh and Vosgerau's widely cited finding that repeatedly imagining eating a food reduces later consumption of it is the closest experimental echo of the Bocchoris story — imagined satisfaction measurably changing appetite for the real thing.[^9] It should be held more cautiously: a Czech replication with a larger sample found no significant effect, though also without enough power to rule the effect out.[^10]

## Open questions

Where does the story's ethics fail the courtesan, and does that failure sit inside the teaching or only around it?

Is resting in desire-born joy a genuine third option between indulgence and suppression, or does it depend on the desired object never becoming a person whose own stake in the encounter has to be reckoned with?
