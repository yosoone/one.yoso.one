# The Three Bodies of the Buddha (_Trikāya_)

The _trikāya_ (三身, _sanshin_, three bodies) is the Mahāyāna answer to a single question: how can a Buddha be beyond the world and active within it at the same time? John Makransky identifies this as the central tension of Mahāyāna thought, that buddhahood both transcends the world and engages with it.[^1]

---

## The Three Bodies

| Body | Sanskrit | Japanese | What it is | Who perceives it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dharma-body | _dharmakāya_ | 法身 _hosshin_ | Buddhahood itself — formless, unconditioned, identical with reality and emptiness | No one; it is not an object of perception |
| Enjoyment body | _saṃbhogakāya_ | 報身 _hōjin_ | A radiant, glorified form, the fruit of countless lives of practice | Advanced bodhisattvas, in pure lands |
| Emanation body | _nirmāṇakāya_ | 応身 _ōjin_ / 化身 _keshin_ | A form that appears in the ordinary world to teach | Ordinary beings |

### 1. The Dharma-Body (_Dharmakāya_)

- Buddhahood as it is in itself: formless, unconditioned, and one with reality.
- The term is older than the three-body scheme. In Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma it meant the Buddha's "body of dharmas," the collection of his enlightened qualities.[^2]
- The _Prajñāpāramitā_ sutras deepened it into buddhahood as the embodiment of reality itself.[^3]

### 2. The Enjoyment Body (_Saṃbhogakāya_)

- The form in which a Buddha teaches advanced bodhisattvas in a pure land.
- It is the "reward" (報, _hō_) of practice across countless lives.
- This is the form of the cosmic Buddhas seen in visualisation and [[Mandala|mandalas]].

### 3. The Emanation Body (_Nirmāṇakāya_)

- The form that appears in the world to teach beings who could not perceive the other two.
- Śākyamuni, born in Lumbinī and dying at Kuśinagara, is the classic example.
- Together with the enjoyment body, it is the conditioned expression of the unconditioned Dharma-body. This is how buddhahood stays engaged with beings.[^4]

---

## How the Doctrine Developed

1. **Early Buddhism — two bodies.** The physical body (_rūpakāya_) and the body of the Buddha's qualities and teaching (_dharmakāya_).[^2]
2. **Yogācāra — three bodies.** Fourth- and fifth-century thinkers, especially Asaṅga, systematised the three-body scheme.[^5]
3. **Later India and Tibet — a fourth body?** The eighth-century commentator Haribhadra argued for a fourth body. Indian scholars disputed this, and the controversy continued in Tibet through Tsongkhapa and Gorampa.[^6]

## The Shingon Turn: Kūkai's Four Bodies

In exoteric Buddhism the Dharma-body is silent, and only the enjoyment and emanation bodies teach. [[Kukai|Kūkai]]'s distinctive claim, _hosshin seppō_ (法身説法), is that the Dharma-body _itself_ preaches.[^7] See [[Kukai, the Letter A, and the Dragon Girl]].

He counts four bodies rather than three, set out in the _Benkenmitsu nikkyōron_ (弁顕密二教論):[^7]

| Body | Japanese | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Self-nature body | 自性身 _jishōshin_ | Buddhahood as reality itself |
| Enjoyment body | 受用身 _juyūshin_ | Enjoyed by the Buddha himself, and shown to bodhisattvas |
| Transformation body | 変化身 _henkeshin_ | Appears in the world to teach |
| Equal-flow body | 等流身 _tōrushin_ | Takes any form a being needs, human or not |

The fourth body connects to the question of whether animals can be buddhas. In Kūkai's scheme the Buddha can appear in _any_ form of being.

## Formless as All Forms

_Question: if the Buddha is limitless, can he manifest in all forms, in every aspect of our lives and of reality — formless because he assumes all forms?_

Mahāyāna answers yes, and Shingon answers most fully. But the answer needs two refinements, and the tradition itself raised a serious objection.

### Emptiness Is Form

The _Heart Sutra_ (般若心経, _Hannya shingyō_) states the principle: 色即是空、空即是色, "form is emptiness, emptiness is form."[^8] Emptiness is not something apart from forms; it is what every form is. A formless Buddha is therefore not absent from forms. Because he is confined to none, he is present in all.

### Kūkai: The Six Elements in Eternal Union

In the _Sokushin jōbutsu gi_ (即身成仏義, _Attaining Buddhahood in This Very Body_), Kūkai writes 六大無碍常瑜伽: the six great elements — earth, water, fire, wind, space and consciousness — are "unobstructed and in eternal union."[^9] Everything that exists is made of the same Buddha-body, and each thing interpenetrates every other.

Read with the _Shōji jissō gi_ verse, where every sense-object is a letter,[^10] and with the equal-flow body (等流身) above, this gives the question's own answer: the Dharma-body is formless _because_ it is every form.

### Two Refinements

1. **"He" can mislead.** The Dharma-body is not a person behind the world choosing forms. It is emptiness itself, not a substance or a self, which is why scholars distinguish it from pantheism or a creator God.[^11] The better image is water taking the shape of every wave, not an actor taking roles.
2. **"Manifest" has two senses.**

   - **Ontological:** everything _is_ already the Dharma-body — the tea bowl, the typhoon, the ink on the paper.
   - **Compassionate:** a Buddha _appears_ in a chosen form, as a skilful means (方便, _upāya_), to reach a particular being.

   Kūkai holds both.

### The Objection: Original Enlightenment (_Hongaku_)

If the Buddha is every aspect of life, then greed, cruelty and suffering are the Buddha too. Why practise at all?

Japan faced this question historically. Original enlightenment thought (本覚思想, _hongaku shisō_) dominated medieval Buddhist intellectual circles. It held that enlightenment is neither a goal to achieve nor a potential to realise, but the true status of all things. Every object, animate and inanimate, was said to manifest the primordially enlightened Buddha just as it is, and even eating, sleeping and deluded thinking were called the Buddha's conduct.[^12]

Its critics read it in two ways. Some saw a dangerous antinomianism that undermined the precepts and the need for discipline. Others saw an ideology that sacralised the existing order and so legitimised hierarchy and discrimination.[^12] Jacqueline Stone's study moves the debate from abstract philosophy to history, placing _hongaku_ in its ritual, institutional and social settings.[^12]

In the 1980s and 1990s the "Critical Buddhism" (批判仏教, _hihan bukkyō_) scholars Hakamaya Noriaki (袴谷憲昭) and Matsumoto Shirō (松本史朗) went further, arguing that _hongaku_ is not properly Buddhist at all.[^13]

### Kūkai's Answer

Kūkai's answer is already in the _Shōji jissō gi_. All letters are the Buddha's speech, but the letters of the nine unenlightened realms are "false" until their source is known.[^10] Everything is the Buddha, but not everything _knows_ it.

Practice — mantra, mudrā, meditation, and perhaps the brush — is the work of reading the letters truly. The view of the Buddha as formless in all forms does not remove the path. It changes the path from reaching somewhere else into recognising what is already here.
