# The Six Perfections

## Thich Nhat Hanh

> The practice of the paramitas can be the practice of our daily lives. We are on the shore of suffering, anger, and depression, and we want to cross over to the shore of well-being. To cross over, we have to do something, and that is called paramita.[^1]

> The Buddha said, "Don't just hope for the other shore to come to you. If you want to cross over to the other shore, the shore of safety, well-being, non-fear, and non-anger, you have to swim or row across. You have to make an effort." This effort is the practice of the Six Paramitas.[^2]

# Background Research

## Aspiration

The vows in [[00 The Bodhisattva Vows]] are the aspiration. The six perfections (_pāramitā_, 波羅蜜) are the practice that carries it out: the training a bodhisattva undertakes on the way to awakening, for the sake of all beings.[^3]

### The six

| | Sanskrit | Japanese | Thich Nhat Hanh's gloss[^4] |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | _dāna_ | 布施 _fuse_ | giving, offering, generosity |
| 2 | _śīla_ | 持戒 _jikai_ | precepts or mindfulness trainings |
| 3 | _kṣānti_ | 忍辱 _ninniku_ | inclusiveness, the capacity to receive, bear, and transform the pain inflicted on you by your enemies and also by those who love you |
| 4 | _vīrya_ | 精進 _shōjin_ | diligence, energy, perseverance |
| 5 | _dhyāna_ | 禅定 _zenjō_ | meditation |
| 6 | _prajñā_ | 般若 _hannya_ | wisdom, insight, understanding |

The Japanese names follow Katsusaki Hirohiko's entry in the Jōdo Shū dictionary, which gives a plain Japanese gloss for each: giving freely, keeping the precepts, bearing and enduring, striving, settling the mind, and waking to wisdom.[^3]

### The word: perfection, or the other shore

Two readings of _pāramitā_ have lived side by side.

- **Supremacy, perfection.** Edgerton derives _pāramitā_ from _pāramī_, itself from _parama_, "highest", and glosses the root sense as mastery or supremacy. He thought the literal meaning of the technical term was surely "supremacy".[^5]
- **Gone to the other shore.** The Tibetan translators rendered the word _pha rol tu phyin pa_, "gone to the other shore", as if it were _pāram_, "the far shore", plus a form of the verb _i_, "to go". Edgerton dismissed this as a fanciful etymology.[^5] Monier-Williams nonetheless lists "coming or leading to the opposite shore" beside "perfection" and "transcendental virtue".[^6]

The shore reading is the one practice kept. Chinese translators rendered the six as 六度 (_rokudo_, "the six crossings") and 六到彼岸 ("the six reachings of the other shore").[^3] Thich Nhat Hanh glosses _paramita_ through the character 度, "to cross over".[^1] 84000 gives _prajñāpāramitā_ as "the transcendent state of wisdom".[^7]

Philology says "perfection". Practice says "crossing". The note keeps both.

### Six, seven, ten

The number was never fixed. Edgerton's survey of the Buddhist Sanskrit sources finds:[^5]

- **Six** — the usual list, in the _Lotus Sūtra_, _Mahāvastu_, _Divyāvadāna_, _Bodhisattvabhūmi_, _Laṅkāvatāra_ and _Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra_. The _Lotus Sūtra_ sometimes counts five and names _prajñā_ separately.
- **Seven** — the six plus _upāyakauśalya_, skill in means.
- **Ten** — the six plus _upāya_, _praṇidhāna_ (vow), _bala_ (strength) and _jñāna_ (knowledge), as in the _Mahāvyutpatti_ (913–923).
- **Pāli** — always ten, called _pāramī_: _dāna_, _sīla_, _nekkhamma_ (renunciation), _paññā_, _viriya_, _khanti_, _sacca_ (truthfulness), _adhiṭṭhāna_ (resolve), _mettā_ and _upekkhā_.

The six also appear in one early discourse collection. In an _Ekottarika-āgama_ discourse (EĀ 27.5), the bodhisattva Maitreya asks the Buddha how to develop the six perfections. Anālayo notes that this collection contains rather late elements.[^8]

### How they hold together

**Wisdom leads the other five.** The _Perfection of Wisdom_ sūtras teach the six again and again as the bodhisattva's main practice. The _Aṣṭasāhasrikā_ (the "Eight Thousand Lines") says _prajñā_ guides the other five. The sūtras' own position is that all six support one another inside the single ideal of the perfection of wisdom.[^3] The _Heart Sutra_ is the short form of this literature; see [[The Heart Sutra, Also known as the PrajnaParamita]] and [[Diamond Sutra]].

**A perfection is a state of mind.** Śāntideva asks how giving can be a perfection when the world is still poor. His answer: the perfection of giving is the mind that gives away everything, fruits included, to all beings. The perfection of ethics, likewise, is the mind that has turned away from harm.[^9] The same act can be ordinary kindness or a _pāramitā_; the difference is the mind behind it. [[Bodhicitta]] gathers how the tradition names that mind.

**Merit and wisdom.** For the paired accumulations of merit and wisdom that the path requires, see [[Merit]].

**The Pure Land.** The _Sutra of Immeasurable Life_ says the bodhisattva Dharmākara practised the six perfections himself and taught others to practise them — the heart of his bodhisattva path, in Katsusaki's reading.[^3]

### Śāntideva's map

Śāntideva's _Bodhicaryāvatāra_ (eighth century) is organised around the later perfections. Giving and ethics are treated within chapter 5, on guarding awareness. Chapters 6 to 9 then take patience, vigour, meditation and wisdom in turn.[^10]

Two turns in the text stand out:

- **Vigour is delight, not strain.** He defines _vīrya_ as enthusiasm for the good.[^11]
- **All of it is for wisdom.** Chapter 9 opens by saying that the Sage taught all the preceding practices for the sake of wisdom.[^12]

### In Japan

**Rokuharamitsu-ji** 六波羅蜜寺, Kyoto, carries the six in its name. The temple dates its founding to 951, by Kūya 空也. On its site it explains _haramitsu_ as reaching the other shore, the world of awakening, and describes the six as practices for reaching the Buddha's state while still living in this world.[^13]

**Higan** 彼岸, "the other shore", names both awakening (or the Pure Land) as opposed to _shigan_ 此岸, this shore of birth and death, and the equinox weeks when families visit graves.[^14] The Jōdo Shū dictionary says the _higan-e_ 彼岸会 observance began in Japan, not India or China. It is first recorded in 806 in the 『日本後紀』(_Nihon kōki_, Later Chronicles of Japan), when provincial temple monks read the _Diamond Sūtra_ for seven days in spring and autumn for Prince Sawara. It spread from the Heian period with faith in Amida's western Pure Land, since the sun then sets due west.[^15]

A common explanation holds that the seven days of _higan_ are for practising the six perfections. The Jōdo Shū entries on 彼岸 and 彼岸会 do not make this link, and no scholarly source for it has been found yet. The words themselves connect _pāramitā_ and _higan_, since 到彼岸 is one of the translations; whether the observance was built on the six is a separate claim.

### Science and scholarship

No study tests the six perfections as such. Psychology has studied practices that sit close to several of them. These modern constructs are not the Buddhist categories, so read the findings as neighbours, not confirmations.

**Giving (_dāna_).** In 2008, Dunn, Aknin and Norton gave people money and randomly assigned them to spend it on themselves or on others. Those who spent it on others reported more happiness.[^16] The original experiment was small (46 people). A registered replication by the same team ran three studies. Kim and colleagues describe the results as mixed but largely supportive: the experiment closest to the original gave strong evidence, and a large recall study (5,199 people) found a small effect.[^17] Kim and colleagues' own close replication (133 people) found no difference using the original analysis, but did find one in the original direction on a more direct measure of happiness.[^18] The fair reading: giving probably makes the giver happier, but by less than first claimed.

**Ethics (_śīla_).** No research was found that measures ethical discipline in this sense. It stays an open thread; see [[Karma]] for the karma-belief studies.

**Patience (_kṣānti_).** Sarah Schnitker's three studies examine how patience relates to well-being and to pursuing goals, and whether patience can be trained.[^19]

**Vigour (_vīrya_).** The popular idea that willpower is a limited fuel that runs down with use ("ego depletion") did not survive a test across 23 laboratories and 2,141 participants: the pooled effect was close to zero (_d_ = 0.04).[^20] A meta-analysis of "grit" found it predicts performance only modestly and overlaps heavily with conscientiousness.[^21] Śāntideva's definition of vigour as delight in the good (above) fits this better than the image of grinding effort.

**Meditation (_dhyāna_).** A review of 47 trials with 3,515 participants found moderate evidence that meditation programmes reduce anxiety and depression, and low evidence for stress. They did no better than specific active treatments such as exercise or cognitive-behavioural therapy.[^22] These trials mostly test mindfulness programmes, not _dhyāna_ as meditative absorption. Van Dam and colleagues warn that the field's definitions are loose and its claims often run ahead of its evidence.[^23]

**Wisdom (_prajñā_).** Psychologists now describe wisdom as sound judgement in complex situations. At its centre is what they call ==perspectival metacognition==: noticing and steering one's own thinking. It also aims at the good of others, not only one's own.[^24] Grossmann and colleagues set out this emerging consensus as a "common wisdom model".[^25] Psychology's wisdom is judgement; _prajñā_ in ==the \*Perfection of Wisdom\* sūtras is insight into emptiness==. ==They share the humility of seeing past one's own view==.
