# Nagas in Buddhism

---

## Overview

The nāga (नाग) occupies a singular position in Buddhist cosmology — neither fully divine nor fully animal, neither entirely mythic nor simply symbolic. Across every major current of Buddhist tradition, from the Pali Canon of Theravāda to the tantric termas of Vajrayāna, the nāga appears as guardian, adversary, devotee, ecological force, and keeper of hidden wisdom. The word itself is Sanskrit for _cobra_ or _serpent_, yet its referent in Buddhist literature exceeds any single category.

The _Princeton Dictionary of Buddhism_ (Buswell & Lopez, Princeton University Press, 2014) describes nāgas as among the most frequently appearing non-human beings in all of Buddhist scripture — present at the Buddha's enlightenment, at his death, in the transmission of the Prajñāpāramitā literature, and in the ongoing ritual life of Buddhist communities across Asia. To understand the nāga is to move through many registers at once: cosmological, ecological, philosophical, psycho-spiritual, and artistic.

---

## Origins and Pre-Buddhist Substrate

The nāga enters Buddhism from a rich pre-existing substrate. Ancient Indian and Southeast Asian cultures venerated serpent-beings as protectors of springs, wells, rivers, and underground treasure long before the formalization of Buddhist doctrine. References appear in the Mahābhārata, the Purāṇas, and in archaeologically attested serpent-worship sites across the subcontinent.

Encyclopædia Britannica describes nāgas as "a strong, handsome species who can assume either wholly human or wholly serpentine form and are potentially dangerous but often beneficial to humans," living in the underground realm of Nāga-loka or Pātāla-loka, "filled with resplendent palaces beautifully ornamented with precious gems." When Buddhism emerged, it assimilated this pre-existing cosmological being rather than displacing it — a characteristic move of early Buddhist transmission.

Scholarly analysis from the 2023 _Puṣpikā Proceedings_ (12th International Indology Graduate Research Symposium, Vienna) notes that at early Buddhist sites on the Indian subcontinent, nāgas as cobra beings are depicted with a remarkable conception of bodily fluidity between human and cobra forms — a feature that had been underexplored in earlier scholarship examining pre-Common Era sculpture.

---

## Cosmological Status and Ontology

Within Buddhist cosmology, the nāga holds an ambiguous threshold position. The _Encyclopedia of Buddhism_ (encyclopediaofbuddhism.org) notes that while some powerful nāgas are grouped among the gods, they are generally classified as animals — placing them technically in an unfavorable rebirth realm despite their evident powers, wisdom, and reverence for the Dharma.

The Vinaya texts treat nāgas as "shape-shifters" unsuitable for monastic ordination. In the Vinayavastu _Pravrajyāvastu_ ("Chapter on Going Forth"), a nāga who takes the form of a Brahmin youth to receive ordination is discovered when his true nature emerges during sleep. The Buddha's ruling — that candidates must be human — established a cosmological threshold that defines the nāga's condition: capable of faith, devoted to practice, yet barred from the path of formal renunciation. In Theravāda Southeast Asia, this paradox became the source of one of Buddhism's most enduring ritual forms: _nāga ordination_, in which monks-to-be in Thailand, Laos, and Cambodia are honored with the title _nak_ (nāga) immediately before their ordination, in remembrance of the serpent-being who longed to join the Saṅgha.

The Vinaya (ii.109) records four royal families of nāgas — Virūpakkhā, Erāpathā, Chabyāputtā, and Kanhagotamakā — and distinguishes between land-dwelling nāgas (_thalaja_) and water-dwelling nāgas (_jalaja_), the latter inhabiting rivers, lakes, and seas, the former residing beneath the surface of the earth.

---

## The Mucalinda Encounter — Primordial Protection

The most celebrated nāga episode in all of Buddhist scripture is the sheltering of the newly enlightened Buddha by Mucalinda, the nāga king. In the _Mucalindasutta_ of the Pali Canon (Udāna 2.1), a great storm arises during the Buddha's post-enlightenment meditation. Mucalinda emerges from the roots of a tree, coils his body seven times around the seated Buddha, and spreads his seven-headed hood as a canopy against the rain. When the storm passes, he transforms into a young brahmin and pays homage.

The seven coils echo the seven chakras of yogic subtle body anatomy, and the seven-headed hood appears in Khmer Buddhist architecture at Angkor as one of the most reproduced sacred images in all of Southeast Asian art. The Mahā Parinibbāna Sutta (DN 16) records that the nāgas were guardians of one of the original eight portions of the Buddha's bodily relics — held by the kings of the nāgas at Rāmagāma — and also of one of his sacred teeth. The nāgas thus serve as custodians of the Buddha's physical legacy into future ages.

---

## The Eight Classes and Attendance at Teachings

Nāgas are counted among the _aṣṭasenā_ — the eight classes of non-human beings said to attend the Buddha's discourses. These eight are: devas (heavenly beings), nāgas, yakṣas, gandharvas, asuras, garuḍas, kiṁnaras, and mahoragas. The presence of nāgas in this assembly positions them not as peripheral figures but as core witnesses to and protectors of the Dharma's transmission (Buswell & Lopez, _Princeton Dictionary of Buddhism_, 2014, s.v. _aṣṭasenā_).

In the Mahāvastu (compiled between 2nd century BCE and 4th century CE), the nāga king Kāla invites Shakyamuni to dwell in his abode, as previous Buddhas — Krakucchanda, Konākamuni, and Kāśyapa — had done before him. This continuity is theologically significant: nāgas serve as bridges between Buddhas across cosmic time, their long lifespans allowing them to be living witnesses to multiple dispensations of the Dharma.

---

## Nāgārjuna and the Prajñāpāramitā

The most philosophically consequential nāga episode occurs at the intersection of history and hagiography. Nāgārjuna (c. 2nd century CE), founder of the Madhyamaka school, is said to have retrieved the Prajñāpāramitā sūtras from the nāga realm, where they had been entrusted since the time of Śākyamuni Buddha. Traditional sources hold that the teachings were conferred upon Nāgārjuna by Nāgarāja, King of the nāgas, who had been guarding them at the bottom of a lake (Norbu, cited in Beer, _Encyclopedia of Tibetan Symbols and Motifs_, Shambhala, 1999).

Robert Mayer (Oxford Centre for Hindu Studies) has argued that this guardianship model has its roots in pre-Buddhist Indian culture — the _nidhiśāstras_, Sanskrit texts on tantric treasure discovery, describe a widespread belief in nāga guardianship of sacred objects (Mayer, cited in Scheible, "Nāgas and Relic Treasures in the Mahāvaṃsa," Oxford Treasure Seminar Series, 2024). Walser, in _Nagarjuna in Context_ (Columbia University Press, 2005), discusses the hagiographic and historical dimensions of this transmission account.

Nāgārjuna's very name — _nāga_ (serpent) + _arjuna_ (bright, white) — encodes this connection. This transmission structure is also the conceptual foundation for _terma_ in the Nyingma tradition of Vajrayāna — hidden treasure teachings encoded in matter and released by realized beings at the appropriate historical moment.

---

## Vajrayāna — Nāgas as Terma Guardians

In Vajrayāna Buddhism, the nāga's role as custodian of hidden knowledge reaches its fullest development. The _Mañjuśrīmūlakalpa_ describes great nāga kings brought in the hundreds of thousands to hear the Dharma. Mayer has pointed to Sanskrit _nidhiśāstras_ — medieval Indian texts on tantric treasure hunting — in which experts revealed or discovered sacred objects hidden in temples, religious statues, and sacred sites, guarded by nāgas, yakṣas, and other territorial deities (Mayer, cited in Scheible, 2024).

Beer's _Encyclopedia of Tibetan Symbols and Motifs_ (Shambhala, 1999) discusses the association of nāgas with cintamani wish-fulfilling jewels and the broader role of nāgas as protectors of potent sacred objects in Tibetan iconography.

---

## Dual Nature — Protection and Retribution

Across all schools, nāgas embody a consistent duality. The _Encyclopedia of Buddhism_ (encyclopediaofbuddhism.org) notes: "Some nāgas are Dharma protectors, but they can also bring retribution if they are disturbed. They may likewise fight one another, wage war, and destroy the lands of others by causing lightning, hail, and flooding." Nāgas are said to be only malevolent toward humans when mistreated — they are susceptible to mankind's disrespectful actions in relation to the environment. This positions the nāga as feedback loop: a mythological encoding of the principle that ecological harm generates consequence.

---

## Nāgas, Water, and Ecology

The nāga's association with water is among its most consistent attributes. Nāgas govern rivers, lakes, monsoons, and subterranean aquifers. They bring rain and fertility, but also floods and drought. Gombrich, in _How Buddhism Began_ (Athlone Press, 1996), discusses the pre-Buddhist substrate of nature-deity veneration that Buddhism absorbed, within which nāgas as water-sovereigns were already deeply established. The Pali Mahā Parinibbāna Sutta's record that nāgas guarded the Buddha's relics connects this ecological sovereignty to the ongoing vitality of the Dharma in the world.

## The Serpentine Energy Perfected

### Etymology as Doctrine

The Pali commentary tradition applies _nāga_ as an epithet to the Buddha and to arahants. The etymology offered is _āgum na karotī ti Nāgo_ — "one who does no wrong is a Nāga" (Mahāniddesa 201). This is a negative formulation that encodes a positive reality: the nāga-nature, associated with heat, venom, passion, and elemental force, does not disappear in the awakened being. What disappears is the capacity for _āgum_ — transgression, wrongdoing, the misuse of force. The energy remains; what has been perfected is its direction.

### The Nāga Saṃyutta and Moral Texture

The Nāga Saṃyutta (Saṃyutta Nikāya 29) contains fifty suttas describing the nature, origins, and qualities of nāgas. The Buddha describes four modes of nāga birth (egg, womb, moisture, spontaneous) and correlates nāga quality with moral conduct. The hierarchy of nāga states maps to degrees of virtue. The most exalted nāgas are those who observe the Uposatha vows.

When the Bodhisatta is born as a nāga king in the Jātaka literature, he practices _sīlapāramitā_ — the perfection of moral conduct — as a nāga. The most dangerous body imaginable, the one whose breath is poison and whose glance can kill, practices total non-harm. The inner logic of the epithet: the nāga who has become a true nāga — in the sense of _āgum na karotī_ — is the one in whom the full force of serpentine power has been turned entirely toward virtue.

### The Nāga in the Bhavacakra

At the center of the wheel of samsara (_bhavacakra_) in Tibetan Buddhist iconography, three animals emerge in an endless cycle: a pig (ignorance), a bird (craving), and a snake (aversion). The snake is one of the three root poisons, _dveṣa_. According to Tenzin Wangyal Rinpoche, the five afflictive energies that drive samsara are themselves primordially the five pure lights — they become the five poisons if we remain deluded, or the Five Wisdoms and the Five Buddha Families if we recognize their purity (cited in _Encyclopedia of Buddhism_, Five Wisdoms entry). The snake in the hub of the wheel and the nāga who wraps around the awakened Buddha are the same energy. The difference is recognition.

### Caṇḍālī, Tummo, and the Inner Serpent Fire

The most technically elaborated Buddhist account of serpentine energy perfected is tummo (_gtum mo_; Sanskrit: _caṇḍālī_), inner fire — the foundational practice of the Six Yogas of Nāropa. Miranda Shaw (_Passionate Enlightenment_, Princeton University Press, 1994) establishes that kuṇḍalinī yoga and Vajrayāna tummo "both take their origin from the Mahasiddhas who were active in India from the 8th century to the 12th century." Dr. Pasang Yonten Arya (2006) describes tummo as comparable to the "serpent fire" — Sanskrit _kuṇḍalinī_ / _caṇḍalī_ (New World Encyclopedia, Tummo entry). Judith Simmer-Brown identifies tummo as the practice Nāropa called "the pillar of the path" (_Lion's Roar_, 2024).

### The Tantric Doctrine of Transformation

The Vajrayāna tantric position, articulated in texts including the _Hevajra Tantra_ and the Six Yogas of Nāropa, is that the kleshas — the poisons of the passions — are the five wisdoms in disguise. They are not renounced but transmuted: their energy transformed within the subtle body into the luminous nectar of enlightened awareness. This is the formal ground of the claim that the serpentine energy is not suppressed but perfected.

Svātmārāma's _Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā_ (15th century) states it from the yogic side: kuṇḍalinī "bestows liberation on yogins and bondage on the ignorant." Arthur Avalon (Sir John Woodroffe), in _The Serpent Power_ (Ganesh & Co., 1918), defines the energy philosophically as "consciousness: the power of matter to know itself." The _Gheranda Samhita_ (6:16) describes kuṇḍalinī as "having the form of serpent" coiled in mūlādhāra: "The _jīvātman_ is there like the flame of a lamp." The individual soul and the serpentine energy share the same seat.

### The Nāga's Seven Coils and the Seven Chakras

Mucalinda's seven coils around the meditating Buddha correspond, in the yogic subtle body framework, to the seven major chakras. Abhinavagupta (10th c., Kashmir Shaivism) describes two primary movements of kuṇḍalinī — upward ascent toward liberation and downward movement toward contraction — as the fundamental polarity of embodied awareness. Mucalinda's seven coils suggest the full integration of energy at every level of the subtle body; his seven-headed hood flowering above the still, seated Buddha is the awakened serpentine force as canopy and mandala.

### The Bhūridatta Jātaka and the Perfection of Non-Harm

The Bhūridatta Jātaka offers the most concentrated image of this perfection. The Bodhisatta as nāga king practices the Uposatha on an anthill in the human realm — fully exposed, utterly capable of destroying those who torment him, choosing complete restraint. The serpentine energy and the power of destruction are present at their full magnitude. What occurs is perfect orientation. The passions are contained in a field of such precision that they generate merit. The poison becomes medicine; the venom, offered freely, becomes a teaching on non-retaliation.
