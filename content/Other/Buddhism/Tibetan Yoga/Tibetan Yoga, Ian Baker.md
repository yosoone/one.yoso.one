## 1. The book and the exhibition

This book, _Tibetan Yoga: Principles and Practices_, has been a work in progress for years. As Desmond said, I had the great privilege of spending many years — decades — living in Nepal and travelling extensively in the eastern Himalayas and Tibet, during which I was immersed in these practices. I was also able to document people doing practices that have conventionally been considered secret; I'll go into some of the reasons for that, and why the situation is changing.

The result is this book, which has been a passion of mine. As Desmond also said, there was an exhibition at the Wellcome two years ago called _Tibet's Secret Temple: Body, Mind and Meditation in Tantric Buddhism_.[^1] The book was originally meant to come out at that time, but because of the all-consuming nature of the exhibition it was delayed, and also because of new material I was able to put into it. It has now come out in the form you'll see today.[^2]

So the question for a Founders' Day at the Buddhist Society would be: what does Tibetan yoga have to do with Buddhism? That is what I would like to explore in this introduction. What are the principles and practices of Tibetan yoga that in some way differ, and where are the continuity and discontinuity within Buddhism as a whole?

## 2. The three turnings of the wheel

As I'm sure you're aware, there were traditionally three turnings of the wheel of doctrine.[^3] First, the **Theravāda**, the tradition of the elders, based on Pali texts composed centuries after the passing of Śākyamuni Buddha. Centuries after that came the birth of the **Mahāyāna**, the Great Vehicle, which emphasized the way of the bodhisattva, with a renewed emphasis on compassion and wisdom. And then the third turning of the wheel, the **Vajrayāna** — the so-called lightning path, the vajra path, the indestructible way — in which buddhahood could be achieved within a single lifetime rather than over many lifetimes.

It was this revolution within Buddhism, occurring roughly from the sixth century onward through the emergence of the Buddhist tantras, as opposed to the pre-existing Buddhist sutras, that gave rise to the practices that are the subject of this book.

People often ask where the continuity lies between these three traditions, or whether they are in fact a kind of syncretic tradition at this point. _\[Microphone adjustment.]_ So the question becomes: what is the relationship between the so-called Hīnayāna or Theravāda, the Mahāyāna, and the Tantrayāna or Vajrayāna, as it is also known?

It's important to frame the talk in this context. The Vajrayāna methods — the so-called tantric Buddhist methods — were considered the skilful means used _within_ the Mahāyāna to achieve the results of the bodhisattva path more quickly and efficiently than they could be in the traditional way described in the Buddhist sutras.

## 3. Thread and weave: sutra and tantra

There is an etymological link in Sanskrit between the sutras and the tantras. The word _sūtra_ shares a root with the English word "suture": it is a thread that ties things together. _Tantra_ is a weaving together.[^4] So rather than the single thread of the sutras, it is the warp and the weft — a non-dual, integrated tapestry woven from the threads of pre-existing Buddhist tradition. There is a richness and vitality to the Vajrayāna that is quite distinct from the earliest forms, the more renunciatory model, of early Buddhism.

## 4. From renunciation to integration

In that renunciatory model, the path was based on the Four Noble Truths, as Desmond spoke about this morning: the recognition that the essential, inescapable dissatisfactions of life serve as a reason to renounce the conventional aspirations we might otherwise feel compelled toward out of desire for fulfilment.

The Mahāyāna then liberated that from itself and said: no, it's not about trying to get off the wheel of life; it's about making a vow as a bodhisattva to be continuously reborn, life after life, until all of samsara is emptied of suffering and all beings are enlightened. A rather Sisyphean project — but it created a sea change in the way Buddhism was presented. It was about working with compassion and engagement in the world rather than renouncing it in any existential way. It still renounced, of course, the psychological dispositions that keep us in a cycle of discontent.

Vajrayāna took that even further. Based on the Mahāyāna view of wisdom and compassion and the active cultivation of those qualities, it turned around, in a certain sense, the whole idea of the Four Noble Truths — which is also a slight mistranslation. They were the four truths taught to the noble ones, those who had taken up a life of renunciation.[^5] It therefore made sense to reflect on life as filled with suffering, _dukkha_, because by reinforcing that you reinforced your existential decision to renounce it. This was a teaching given to renunciants, not to lay people in general. That is something we need to remember as we bring Buddhism into the twenty-first century: the context in which particular iconic teachings of the Buddha were given — who the audience was, and what the intention was.

_Dukkha_ is a very interesting word. It has been translated in many ways — existential pain, suffering, discontent, dissatisfaction — but I am told (I'm not a Sanskrit specialist) that it also refers to the axle of an oxcart that is out of joint.[^6] So _dukkha_ is something a farmer 2,500 years ago would relate to very well: the axle out of kilter, the wheels suddenly out of their groove and no longer aligned with the path, everything slightly off. In that sense it is something we can relate to across times, cultures and geographies.

In the Vajrayāna view, life is not all about suffering. Life can be blissful — _**sukha**_, the etymological opposite — and it is only when we fall from the view that life is full of endless possibility that we suffer. The path then becomes one of aligning ourselves with the inner core of our being, which is beyond suffering.

That is what the so-called **completion-stage yogas** of Tibetan yoga are about. These are very dynamic meditative and yogic practices that differ from the earliest Buddhist teachings, which focus on introspective observation of the breath and of thoughts. Instead they take a dynamic, interactive relationship with body, breath and mind. Rather than just observing the breath, you control it, experiment with it, interact with it. This is the dynamism of the Vajrayāna path, said to bring enlightenment within a matter of years rather than lifetimes, and it gave rise to the colourful iconography we associate with Tibetan Buddhism.

## 5. The mahāsiddhas and the tantric revolution

What is interesting in Tibet is that all the lineages we know — the **Nyingma**, the **Kagyu**, the **Sakya** — trace their original teachings to _mahāsiddhas_, great adepts, such as Virūpa on the right _\[slide]_, the founding figure of the Sakya tradition. These were wild yogis who lived and taught outside any institutional monastic setting, although many had been in such settings before. Their teachings were about breaking away from the institutional thought and practice that monasteries had come to embody, at least in their view.

So there is a flavour of early Indian Vajrayāna, before it reached Tibet, that was rather radical in many respects and not always compatible with monastic culture. There were great debates historically over the introduction of the tantras into Buddhist monastic settings. When these teachings crossed the Himalayas into Tibet from the eighth century onward, they often met mixed reactions from Tibetans themselves. And because of the interest in establishing Buddhism as the basis of a theocratic state, some of the more radical and antinomian aspects of tantra were not compatible either. So we sometimes get what look like awkward constructs — a celibate monastic community meditating upon deities in sexual union, and so on — which are hard to understand unless one sees how these radical teachings had to be reinterpreted to fit a monastic setting.

What I want to do in this talk is give a general overview of this radical tradition, which is how it started out. In the colourful mandala on the left _\[slide]_ you see the deity **Hevajra**, literally the "joyous vajra" — _He_ can be read as an exclamation, a "hail" — an invocation of adamantine reality. The palace of the deity is really just the nature of one's own mind, its self-transcendent, ego-transcendent state, represented as a palatial dimension of pure interconnectivity.

In early Buddhism we talk about renunciation based on the fact that everything is impermanent. In Vajrayāna, that impermanence — which has a negativity bias built into it — is seen instead as the perpetual renewal that impermanence implies. Early Buddhism is, in a sense, the glass half empty; Vajrayāna is certainly the glass half full.

## 6. King Indrabhūti and the _Guhyasamāja Tantra_

The basis of the Buddhist tantras can be summarized in the opening story of the _Guhyasamāja Tantra_, generally considered the earliest of the main Buddhist tantras. King Indrabhūti, from the land of Oḍḍiyāna on the north-western frontier of India, comes to the Buddha with his retinue:

> King Indrabhūti and his retinue made many offerings, asking, "Please teach us, and those like us, a method for liberation from the suffering of the life cycle." The Master, knowing his mind, said, "Go forth from home into homelessness, and there is a way." The king, again making offerings, replied, "Since passionate beings like us cannot renounce the five objects of sense desire, please teach us a method for liberation in which one does not have to renounce those things." The Master transformed his appearance into that of a universal emperor and emanated the thirty-two-deity mandala of the Secret Assembly. He initiated the king and his retinue, taught them the tantra, and entrusted it to them.

This is the story of King Indrabhūti according to Pema Karpo in the sixteenth century, based on the transmission of the _Guhyasamāja Tantra_.[^7]

And here is Guhyasamāja the deity _\[slide]_: a transformation of the image of a Buddha into a composite male–female figure, multi-armed and multi-headed, representing the transformation — the apotheosis — of the human condition into a state of ecstatic, self-transcending, noetic bliss. This is a different way of presenting the Buddhist teachings, one that emerged from the sixth century onward.

The scholar Christian Wedemeyer, trying to understand this tradition, said that the ultimate practice of tantric Buddhism is to become a buddha oneself by finding the enlightened being within, rather than seeking communion with buddhas through meditations on consecrated statues and the like — to cultivate a personal divine identity.[^8] That gives us a sense of what lies behind these deities.

## 7. The six yogas and their antecedents

We have the so-called six-yoga traditions: one attributed to the female mahāsiddha **Niguma**, and the other, best known, to the male mahāsiddha **Tilopa**.[^9] _Tibetan Yoga_ is based on the six-yoga system, which is about finding liberation in all experience rather than through renunciation — liberation in every activity between birth and death and in all the intermediate states in between.

We see this anticipated in pre-Buddhist texts such as the _Maitri Upaniṣad_:

> Mind alone is samsara. Strive to purify your thoughts. What one thinks, one becomes: this is the eternal mystery.[^10]

Samsara here means the world in which we are discontented. All of these practices — Buddhist, beyond Buddhism, or pre-Buddhist — work with the mind: how do we transform our thoughts to reach a liberated state, as opposed to a contracted and singularized one?

The _Maitri Upaniṣad_ also anticipates some of the language of the six yogas, particularly _tummo_, the inner heat:

> Like a lamp stirred by a subtle breeze, the one who dwells among the gods blazes up. The one who knows this blazing knows unity and duality; that one comes to the single abode.[^11]

So we see different languages in which a new way of practice began to emerge — not based on a rejection of life and materiality, but on a radical engagement with it.

## 8. The _Kālacakra_ and the central channel

The last of the great Buddhist tantras is the _Kālacakra Tantra_. It most likely emerged in the tenth century — newer scholarship suggests the early eleventh[^12] — and it is about recognizing that the whole cosmos lies within the body. The body, through its receptor points, the chakra system — the gateways through which we are interpenetrated with the universe as a whole — becomes the mode of practice.

As we were discussing before the talk, the new middle way is the **central channel**, through which all six yogas are performed. It is the core of human psychophysical anatomy, the thread that ties together top and bottom, and it correlates in contemporary terms with the hypothalamic–gonadal axis of the body.[^13] It is the _axis mundi_ of the human body, around which everything is centred, and it is also the basis of the tantric mandala. We see a wonderful example on the right from the Kālacakra system _\[slide]_: it's almost like looking down on the body from above, with the central channel at the centre and body, speech and mind expanding in concentric rings to the ends of the universe.

The practices were based on _pratyāhāra_, as we have it in the yogic tradition[^14] — turning the senses inward to open an inner efflorescence of consciousness beyond the sensory gates. This is symbolized by the peacock feather below this figure _\[slide]_, who closes his sense doors in order to open the interior receptor sites within the human body–mind.

## 9. The yoginī tantras and the feminine

We also have the **yoginī tantras**, which emerged as the final efflorescence of the Buddhist tantras. There were the so-called father tantras, mother tantras and non-dual tantras; the mother tantras are also called yoginī tantras. They represent a radical phase of Buddhist tantra in India **\[unclear]**, deeply influenced by and interactive with **tantric Śaivism**. Many techniques central to the six-yoga system within Vajrayāna have direct correlates in pre-existing Śaiva tantric practices — some of the most radical ones for shifting consciousness.[^15]

It is important to recognize, in the history and evolution of Buddhism, that it was always in creative interaction with contemporaneous traditions. With Vajrayāna, the influence of the Śaiva tantras — based very much on the idea of the yoginī, the female energies within the body and within nature — was very strong.

The book tries to bring back some of this. Of the eighty-four mahāsiddhas who were canonized, only four are women, but when you look into the biographies of the other 80 male mahāsiddhas, their teachers were for the most part female.[^16] Tilopa, for example, had **\[unclear]** as his female teacher, and Saraha had the arrowsmith's daughter, after which he said, "Only now am I truly a monk; only now have I woken up to the true meaning."

So the imprint of feminine energy into what had been a strongly patriarchal tradition, through the yoginī tantras, represents a sea change — one that, to be honest, slowly got covered up when tantra was re-monasticized in Tibet for theocratic reasons. Part of what I tried to do in the book is bring back a little of this: the patriarchal attempts to control an energy that can never be controlled, because it is an innately creative force — and this is where the inspiration for tantric Buddhism came from.

We see that in some of the earliest images, from temples such as the sixty-four-yoginī mandala temple at Hirapur in Odisha,[^17] and on the right, a transformed form of Kālī and Cāmuṇḍā **\[names uncertain]** _\[slide]_. This energy of the beautiful and the horrific, of the wrathful feminine, is very strong.

## 10. Oḍḍiyāna, the Swat Valley and Dionysus

Something I touch on only briefly in the book, and hope will be explored further: it is generally held that the Buddhist tantras emerged from a realm called **Oḍḍiyāna**, on the north-western frontier of India, now recognized as the **Swat Valley** in greater Gandhāra.[^18] This was part of the ancient Silk Road, a place of great interaction between East and West long before the emergence of the Buddhist tantras.

Interestingly, it is also in the Swat Valley that **Dionysus** — the great leopard-riding god of divine intoxication in the Hellenic tradition — was said, in some versions of his story, to have been born and raised by the maenads, by the yoginīs.[^19] He is, of course, a wine-drinking deity, and there is remarkable archaeological evidence in the Swat Valley of a wine culture going back well before the very extensive Buddhist civilization that developed there.[^20] That civilization thrived until the fifth and sixth centuries, when it fell to early invasions, with the destruction of some 1,400 to 1,500 Buddhist monasteries in the area. The monks left and presumably moved further up the valleys, where they interacted with literally wine-growing cultures that saw themselves as descendants of Dionysus. This is only beginning to be explored.

We also have some of the earliest images of mahāsiddhas from this area of Oḍḍiyāna, though much has been destroyed, of course, by the Taliban. So this is a vanishing link. Here, in an early rock carving from Oḍḍiyāna _\[slide]_, we see the Buddha standing below and an erotic Dionysian scene above — almost the seeds of later tantric iconography. An argument can be made that Vajrayāna Buddhism emerged in part through an interface between Hellenic, Dionysian traditions and the Buddhist traditions then thriving on the Indo-Iranian frontier.

Here, on a second-century gilded plate from Gandhāra _\[slide]_, is Silenus, the teacher of Dionysus, with his drinking horn, being served wine by a consort holding a bowl — exactly the same imagery you see in a fifteenth-century painting at Pelkor Chöde Monastery in Gyantse, Tibet.[^21] This link between the siddhas and the Dionysian tradition is something that could really bear fruit.

We see **Padmasambhava**, also known as **\[unclear]**, the great master from Oḍḍiyāna, and **Cakrasaṃvara**: traditions that trace themselves to this north-western frontier. There we can see some of the seeds of what Tantrayāna was really about — a shift from a renunciatory, existential orientation to one of integration; from recognizing life as perennial discontent, _dukkha_, to one of potential _sukha_, bliss.

## 11. The charnel grounds

We see this imagery also in the radical depictions of tantric practice in the **charnel grounds**, early places of tantric Buddhist practice in India, which also form the outer edges of a Buddhist mandala. One almost has to pass through a dimension of horror, terror and ecstasy before entering the sacred realm. We see that in a _Hevajra Tantra_ cremation-ground scene of sex, death and transformation _\[slide]_ — radical iconography, much of it borrowed from the Śaiva tradition — and in images of tantric feasts, some completely antinomian.

As Ronald Davidson wrote in his book on early esoteric Buddhism in India, the institutionalization of siddha literature by the more conservative siddha and monastic communities relied heavily on the most developed hermeneutical strategies Buddhism had ever seen — all with limited success.[^22] What that tells us is that there was an awkward assimilation of the radical teachings of tantric Buddhism into monastic institutional communities.

## 12. The body as the locus of practice

The mahāsiddha **Saraha**, from the eighth century, considered the founding figure of the mahāsiddha traditions, said:

> I have seen in my wanderings many temples, shrines and monasteries, but none are as blissful as my own body.[^23]

The human body became the new locus of tantric Buddhist practice. The mahāsiddha **Lakṣmīṅkarā** of Oḍḍiyāna, also said to be from the eighth century, said:

> The wise first establish enlightened perception. Secondly, they meditate unwaveringly on empty presence. Thirdly, with intuitive wisdom, they do whatever is to be done with spontaneity, modesty and grace.[^24]

You can't really do much better than that in expressing what we are trying to achieve: a sacred vision, enlightened view; meditation on emptiness, the relative nature of all phenomena; and then engaging with wisdom and compassion in whatever needs to be done. And this came from a female mahāsiddha.

There was also the female mahāsiddha Niguma, and Nāropa, who were most known for establishing the so-called six-yoga system within tantric Buddhism, in which the body becomes the locus of practice. This is already anticipated in a sutra attributed to the Buddha: "If the body is not mastered, the mind cannot be mastered. If the body is mastered, the mind is mastered."[^25]

Here we see different ways in which the energetics of the body have been depicted over time. Many of these are explained in the book — depictions of the energy body, the vajra body, the subtle body[^26] — but essentially it is the transpersonal dimension of our own anatomy. Once cultivated, as in the figure on the left _\[slide]_, with three channels running from the pelvis to the crown of the head, it brings out the buddha nature we see on the right. The Buddha here refers not to the historical figure but, in this long-haired form, to the yogic potential of all humans.

Many core practices of the six-yoga system focus on the central channel and are anticipated in Śaiva tantras such as the _Kubjikā Tantra_ — here the goddess Kubjikā Devī, Tripurasundarī _\[slide]_.[^27] These were in a sense pre-Buddhist, or pre-Vajrayāna, yogic systems clearly assimilated into the Hevajra, Cakrasaṃvara and other systems.

We see them in temples in India such as the sixty-four-yoginī temple in Odisha on the left _\[slide]_, which are essentially architectural mandalas. It is still a mystery exactly how these were used ritually, but they are inscribed, in this case, with sixty-four yoginīs in various yogic poses — dance postures or symbolic ones — transformational fields for practitioners of this early tradition.

## 13. Yogis and monks in Tibet

In early Tibetan wall paintings we also see the distinction between monastic practice, on the right with a monk, and the yogi on the left, with the distinctive meditation belt, long hair and ornaments _\[slide]_. It is also well established that Padmasambhava, one of the great mahāsiddhas, who went to Tibet in the eighth century at the invitation of King Trisong Detsen, established two distinct lineages to be equally valued and propagated: the **red-robed monastic order** and the **white-robed, non-celibate yogic order**. Both were to have equal status, but historically the interests of a theocratic state favoured suppressing the yogic and giving more power and authority to the monastic.[^28]

Part of what the book does is explore some of that debate and bring more attention to the sidelined yogic traditions. In terms of interest in the West, most of us drawn to Buddhism are not necessarily drawn to becoming celibate monastics, but to finding out how — as King Indrabhūti asked of the Buddha in the _Guhyasamāja_ story — we can integrate these practices into everyday life and use them to expand our potential rather than contract the way we engage with life.

The _Hevajra Tantra_, for example, says about fifteen different times that **the yogi must always dance and sing**.[^29] This is a radical departure from earlier prescriptions against such activities in the early Vinaya. The Hevajra tantras were direct; they were confrontational with the accepted principles of early Buddhism.

## 14. A living tradition: the caves of Yeshe Tsogyal

I want to show a few slides of this as a living tradition in Tibet. This is the enlightenment cave of **Khandro Yeshe Tsogyal**, one of the consorts of Padmasambhava, who came to Tibet and established tantric Buddhism there in the eighth century.[^30] One can say that this cave, and her enlightenment through the practice of tummo, the inner heat, represents in a certain sense the beginning of the whole efflorescence of tantric Buddhism in the Tibetan world.

Here is a yogi of the white-robed, long-haired order _\[slide]_. We spent the night there with him, and as I was leaving he almost flapped his wings as if about to fly off. The cave is at about 17,000 feet — an incredible place.

In this vicinity there are yoginīs such as the dreadlocked Ani **\[name unclear]**, who spent nine years practising in a cave, and **\[unclear]**, whom you saw in an earlier image. This represents the yogic tradition we hear so little about, because it is not happening in the monasteries; it is happening in remote caves far from the places we would think of as iconic for Tibetan Buddhism.

Here again is that lama on the right, with Ani **\[name unclear]** in the centre _\[slide]_, as we climbed to bring him to the cave where he was going to stay in long retreat, and where we all stayed overnight, performing rituals on the way to the local protector spirits. So there is this integration of Tibetan shamanic tradition with Vajrayāna Buddhism from India — an incredibly rich, fertile world, radical in its movement from introspection to experimentation.

Looking up at the top peak in the centre _\[slide]_ is where the enlightenment cave of Yeshe Tsogyal is located. On the right, that little hermitage is where the woman considered the contemporary emanation of Yeshe Tsogyal was based until a few years ago; she has since moved to a higher sanctuary because she felt too many people were coming to visit her. So this is where the real yogic practice of Tibet is happening — beyond what we would normally see or have access to.

## 15. Milarepa and the early Kagyu

**Milarepa**, in the eleventh century, is considered the great tantric yogi of Tibet. When we look at the early lineages of the Kagyu, for example — which has an incredibly rich monastic tradition — the first lineage holders were hardly monastic; none of them were celibate. We have Tilopa, then Nāropa, then Marpa, Milarepa and Rechungpa. It was only through **Gampopa**, Milarepa's other heart son, who had been a Kadampa monk, that the transmission of Nāropa's six yogas was, in a certain sense, appropriated into a monastic context.[^31]

Milarepa is said to have said:

> The six yogas are the culminating instructions of the whispered lineage. No other path or method can be found that is superior to these.[^32]

The "whispered lineage" here means the secret Vajrayāna path.

## 16. _Tummo_, the inner fire

The first of the six yogas is _**tummo**_. _Mo_ means feminine and _tum_ means fierce, so it is literally "the fierce feminine," but it refers to the primal heat associated with the goddess. _Tummo_ translates the Sanskrit _**caṇḍālī**_, the flaming female goddess described in the _Hevajra Tantra_. It is as if the middle way is transformed from the avoidance of extremes into an anatomical analogue of emptiness within the human body — drawing divisive energies and distractions into the luminous core of our own being.

The yogic belt, the _gomthag_, that the yogi on the right is wearing across his shoulder _\[slide]_ marks someone practising the inner tantric way, based on the central channel as the locus of transformation. Here is an anatomical depiction on the left, and on the right the classic posture for bringing the winds into the base of the central channel, four fingers' breadth beneath the navel. There is also a secondary tummo posture, described as the "three-legged pot stove," which Milarepa in his own life story described as the one he eventually favoured: the first posture induces the heat, and this one stabilizes and maintains it.[^33]

Already in the _Hevajra Tantra_:

> Caṇḍālī blazes upward from the navel. She incinerates the five buddhas. _HAṂ_ is burnt, and the moon at the crown melts.[^34]

What does this mean? In one of the earliest tantras the language is about incinerating the five buddhas: the idea that the Buddha is something other than our own awakened nature is to be burnt away. It is like the Zen saying, "If you meet the Buddha on the road, kill him."[^35] It is a radical interiorization of Buddhist practice, moving into somatic experience — dynamic practices that directly engage the physiological potential of the human condition rather than observing it. It is not about seeing that things arise and pass away, not getting attached, and leaving it behind. It is about bringing experience to its full efflorescence.

With that comes **bliss**, and bliss by itself is non-objectifying. Bliss is the apotheosis of desire. Desire — if that is the definition of _dukkha_ — always implies an object; bliss has no desire, because it is a state of absolute fulfilment. In a state of bliss there is no space for desire to manifest. This is why so much of the language of the Vajrayāna is about cultivating bliss: then _dukkha_ is taken care of.

Tilopa, the teacher of Nāropa, said in his oral instructions on the six yogas:

> The yogic body is a network of energy channels, coarse and subtle, intersecting the energy fields, the chakras, which should be brought under control. The method begins with physical exercises. The energy winds are drawn inward, expanded, retained and dissolved. There are two side channels, the central channel, the _avadhūtī_, and the four chakras. Flames rise from the caṇḍālī fire at the navel; a stream of nectar drips down from the syllable _HAṂ_ at the crown, invoking the four joys.[^36]

We see the same language, slightly varied, as in the _Hevajra Tantra_. Tilopa said the teaching of tummo he gave to Nāropa was the essential extract of the entire _Hevajra Tantra_. In the six yogas, Tilopa is taking the key extracts of very complicated tantras: tummo from the _Hevajra_; consort practice from the _Hevajra_ and _Cakrasaṃvara_; clear-light yoga **\[source tantra unclear]**; and dream yoga from the _Guhyasamāja_. I won't repeat them all here, as they are in the book, but essentially this is the song of the yogas' joy: working with energies of the body we normally don't tune into.

If you summarized the thread that connects all six yogas, it is about finding that thread — the often-forgotten core of our being, which has nothing to do with us in an ego-individualized sense. It is the transpersonal essence of our own being. It is **ego dissolution through ecstasy rather than renunciation**.

We see that depicted very vividly in the Lukhang murals,[^37] in the iconography of the subtle yogic body — the chakras and channels — and here in a practitioner from eastern Tibet of the long-haired order _\[slide]_, who takes a very different set of vows from a monastic.

Nāgabodhi, in his elucidation of the summary of the five stages, describes inner heat as the basis of the blisses that arise — the tantric equivalent of the four _jhānas_ of early Buddhism, the transcendent states of meditative absorption. In Vajrayāna, the four jhānas are represented as the four blisses. To read the end of the passage: this "induces the four blisses, and thus induces the experience of **\[unclear]** clear-light consciousness that arises together with the inner bliss."[^38]

Here is the Sixteenth Karmapa, the Karmapa before the current one, demonstrating some of the postures, using a meditation belt to bring about the inner transformation of the yogic body _\[slide]_; Khyentse Rinpoche **\[identification uncertain — possibly Dilgo Khyentse Rinpoche]**, slightly thinner at the end of his life, demonstrating the tummo posture with a meditation belt; and a yogi from Tashi Jong in the classic posture, drying a meditation shawl dipped in ice water, to demonstrate the heat these practices generate.[^39] Although this is considered a secret tradition, we also have historical images of it, taken very purposefully and openly by masters of the tradition.

## 17. _Trulkhor_, the magical movements

We also see manuscripts presenting the yogic exercises that precede the practice of inner fire. We saw them in the twenty-three postures of the Lukhang wall paintings — one set of many. These _\[slide]_ are by Pema Lingpa, from the fifteenth century, but there are many sets, ranging from 108 postures down to five, done to open the channels of the subtle body so that the inner fire can blaze forth. _\[Slides out of order.]_

Later manuscripts, here from the eighteenth century, show yogic postures that engage the core of the body. Here is one being practised, engaging the abdominal or pelvic fire. This is a classic position, the peacock, from haṭha yoga, but it had a different application within the so-called _**trulkhor**_, or magical movements, done as a prelude to rather radical exercises in the Tibetan Buddhist tradition.[^40]

Trulkhor often involves what are called _**bebs**_, great drops, as this practitioner in Bhutan is doing _\[slide]_: you leap into the air, ideally into full lotus, before landing back on your meditation seat. All of this is to drive the energy — the fire — opening the core of the body. Here again is that practitioner in a wonderful flying pose, and another yogi in Bhutan.

They say that if you do this without a perfect _**bumchen**_ — the "great vase," the _kumbhaka_ — where you are able to hold the energy, you risk harm, because the whole point is to keep opening the space of the spine, not compress it. Without a perfectly held vase breath you can compress the spine and do real damage to your inner physiology and outer anatomy. With the perfectly held breath, the drop drives extraordinary energy — that blazing fire — right up through the core of the body.

There were also other radical methods, such as the **vajra wave** (_dorje balab_ **\[spelling uncertain]**), in which the master or oneself uses the meditation belt to stabilize the posture and then, with the breath held out, presses very specific points on the sides of the neck near the carotid arteries to induce a state of noetic bliss. Without the meditation belt it is easy to faint and fall over. When I first discussed this practice with the Dalai Lama, showing him that image, he said that yes, enlightenment can come — but so can death. It is not to be practised at home.

As you can see in the text at upper right _\[slide]_, there are texts within the secret tradition, beautifully illustrated, showing how body and mind are prepared for these more contemplative practices based on inner visionary experience. Here again we see the female transmission in Tibet, with Ani **\[name unclear]** and one of her disciples on the right, and holders of the same tradition at Tashi Jong in north-western India many years ago.

## 18. Karmamudrā and the clear light

Here is tummo depicted in the Lukhang murals _\[slide]_. I'll go a little quickly, because my hour is almost up and I want to leave time for questions.

Tummo is always said to be the foundation of the six yogas — the most important of them all, because it opens the central channel. Even before that come the trulkhor, the magical movements — five to 108 in number, according to different traditions — which use physical yoga to prepare the body to open this stream of noetic awareness within the core of our being.

Within the non-monastic tradition, _**karmamudrā**_, consort practice, enhanced the fires of tummo and was considered a path in itself. This was not a convenient truth within monastic communities in Tibet and tended to be sidelined. There were different sets of the six yogas, but the original ones taught by both Niguma and Tilopa included karmamudrā.[^41] Tantric iconography is full of it: the bell and vajra represent the polarities of wisdom and compassion, male and female, matter and spirit, emptiness and form.

We saw before the enlightenment cave of Yeshe Tsogyal, and places such as the Lion Fortress **\[place name unclear]**. After her practice there, she went to India and was instructed by Padmasambhava to find her consort, Atsara Sale; she brought him back to this cave, and they practised there in union. So right at the heart of the tradition we see the male–female partnership in consensual union, beyond all the divisive things usually associated with it, as fundamental.

The outcome of that is the third of the six yogas, the **luminosity of being** — _**ösel**_, literally "clear light." This clear light of consciousness is also the clear-light body: essentially pure luminosity. There is a whole chapter on it in the book, called "The Infinite Brightness of Being," on how clear-light practice is done in both waking and dream states, and how its locus is at the heart, as in this early thirteenth-century manuscript _\[slide]_. It is all about transcending the wheel and cycle of existence.

Here are images of a **dark retreat**, also in the Lukhang murals _\[slide]_, showing how this luminosity can be achieved, paradoxically, through retreats in total darkness, in which the inner luminosity of mind emerges simply because of deprivation from external sources of vision. Inner visions begin to arise.[^42]

In the Lukhang murals we also see composite images. The Dalai Lama explained this particular one as combining tummo and _**phowa**_, the ejection of consciousness — almost a composite of all six yogas in a single image. We see fire rising from the central channel; the melting of the _HAṂ_, the small white syllable to the left of the yogi's head; and the transference of consciousness to a buddha realm at the upper left, bypassing the _bardo_ — the hallucinatory interim world that arises after death for someone who has not familiarized themselves with the nature of mind and its apparitional dimensions.

## 19. The heart centre

So we see the idea of centres within the body presented in different ways. Today we have new technologies and new understandings of human physiology and consciousness, but all of this comes back to the eternal refrain of the **heart centre**: the _nyingthig_, the heart-essence tradition within **Dzogchen**, the Great Perfection.

So much of the dialogue between neuroscience and Tibetan Buddhism has focused on the brain, although it is increasingly evident how many neurons there are in the heart,[^43] and there is growing interest among neuroscientists in how to measure what is happening there. When we take a top-down approach and measure only the brain, without attending to the heart, we miss the whole point — because so much of what the Buddhist tradition, Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna in particular, is telling us is that it is all about heart-centred awareness, not brain-centred awareness.

This is why tummo is so important. With tummo we shift out of the head and down to the enteric nervous system — the gut, the viscera[^44] — and that fire brings the illumination of the heart. Rather than top-down, it is bottom-up. It brings back, as the Vajrayāna did, a **visceral reality** as opposed to the virtual reality so often found in the way tantric practices have necessarily been interpreted and practised in monastic settings — through visualization rather than existential enactment — because enactment goes against the Vinaya, the early prescriptions of Buddhist practice, which has its own route to enlightenment. We are talking here simply about the tantric yogic tradition, which is why I emphasize it.

## 20. Dzogchen and the body

Here are the visionary practices that arise, and contemporary male and female practitioners, in this case in Bhutan _\[slide]_. I'll close with different kinds of practice undertaken specifically in nature: meditations on the five elements, merging one's consciousness with the flow of water to engage the flow of awareness within one's own subtle body; ecstatic dance and ecstatic movement.

Dzogchen, which we tend to think of as a purely mind-only practice, in its original form has an incredibly sophisticated physical yogic practice as its induction — its _**ngöndro**_, the preliminary foundational practices — which are extremely physically demanding. The book has a whole chapter on this. The core practice is the held, isometric **vajra posture** demonstrated here _\[slide]_: you hold it until the body collapses, get up, and hold it until it collapses again, until mind and body are completely exhausted and the mind simply enters its natural state.[^45] Here are different depictions of that pose and its function.

So, as in haṭha yoga, we use the body to access different dimensions of consciousness — in different ways, places and contexts, presented through wall paintings, manuscripts and details of the visionary states that can arise. Some of the art in the Lukhang murals, from around 1700, seems almost to anticipate both prehistoric cave art and twentieth-century modern art by Kandinsky and others: so-called **entoptic phenomena**, grids and swirls appearing against a blue field.[^46]

So again there is a dialogue between science — new understandings of the physical body and its energetic substratum — and these extraordinary depictions in Tibetan Buddhist art of how we engage a sense of being that is interconnected with all things, not limited, in which we free ourselves from egoic identification.

## 21. Closing: Mahāmudrā and Tilopa's six words

To close with the purpose of all this: as Tilopa said, all six yogas come down to **Mahāmudrā**, often translated as the "great gesture," essentially a synonym for Dzogchen, the Great Perfection — the great coalescence of all these practices into one thing.[^47] The six yogas that Tilopa transmitted to Nāropa came down to six vital points:

> Let go of what has passed.
> Let go of what may come.
> Let go of what is happening now.
> Don't try to figure anything out.
> Don't try to make anything happen.
> Relax, right now, and rest.[^48]

And to close on a note from the great Zen pilgrim Bashō, from _The Narrow Road to the Deep North_: "Do not seek to follow in the footsteps of the wise; seek what they sought."[^49]
