# Kūkai, the Letter A, and the Dragon Girl

A research note from one session's reading. Only primary texts, critical editions, university-press scholarship and scholar-edited reference works are cited. Anything not yet checked against the source is listed under **To review**.

---

## 1. The Cosmos as Scripture

For [[Kukai|Kūkai]] (空海, 774–835), the cosmic Buddha Dainichi Nyorai (大日如来, Mahāvairocana) does not stand behind the world. The world _is_ his body and his speech. Kūkai's term for this is _hosshin seppō_ (法身説法, "the Dharma-body preaches"). In the _Benkenmitsu nikkyōron_ (弁顕密二教論, _Distinguishing Exoteric and Esoteric Teachings_) he makes it the mark that separates [[Esoteric Buddhism|esoteric]] from exoteric Buddhism.[^1]

The poetic image of this idea is a couplet from 「遊山慕仙詩」(_Yūsan bosen shi_, "Wandering the Mountains, Longing for the Immortals"), the opening poem of the 『性霊集』(_Shōryōshū_, Collection of Spirit and Soul):

> 山毫点溟墨　乾坤経籍箱

"The mountains are my brush, dipped in the ink of the dark sea; heaven and earth are the case that holds the sutras."[^2]

**Note.** The common English paraphrase, "a sutra painted by brushes of mountains and ink of oceans," is a gloss. In the original, heaven and earth are the sutra _case_.

## 2. _Shōji jissō gi_ — the Four-Line Verse

The 『声字実相義』(_Shōji jissō gi_, The Meanings of Sound, Word, and Reality) is the doctrinal core of the idea. It is usually read with the _Sokushin jōbutsu gi_ (即身成仏義) and the _Unji gi_ (吽字義) as a trilogy.[^3] Its centre is a four-line verse, which Kūkai himself divides into four topics:[^4]

| Line | Reading | Translation | Theme |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 五大皆有響 | _godai ni mina hibiki ari_ | The five great elements all have resonance. | Sound (声) |
| 十界具言語 | _jikkai ni gongo o gusu_ | The ten realms are fully endowed with language. | True and false letters |
| 六塵悉文字 | _rokujin kotogotoku monji nari_ | The six sense-objects are, without exception, letters. | Inner and outer letters (字) |
| 法身是実相 | _hosshin wa kore jissō nari_ | The Dharma-body is reality. | Reality (実相) |

**Key points**

- **The five elements** (地水火風空: earth, water, fire, wind, space) have an exoteric meaning, the physical elements, and an esoteric one, the seed-syllables _A VA RA HA KHA_ that make up the Buddha's body. The elements are the substance of sound, and resonance is its function.
- **The ten realms**, from hell to buddhahood, all speak. The difference between "false" speech and _shingon_ (真言, true word) is whether its source in the Dharma-body is known.
- **The six sense-objects** (六塵, "six dusts") are read as script. Visual form gets a further verse of its own, 顕形表等色, which divides it into colour, shape and movement.[^5] This is the doctrinal root of the brush-and-ink image.
- **The Dharma-body is reality.** The copula 是 identifies them. There is no hidden Buddha behind appearances.

The grammar builds the argument. The totalising words 皆 ("all"), 具 ("fully endowed") and 悉 ("without exception") leave nothing outside the claim.

## 3. The Letter A

_A_ (阿, Siddham 𑖀) is the root syllable of [[Shingon]].

- **It is linguistically primary.** _A_ is the first sound of the open mouth and the first letter of the Sanskrit syllabary. It is also inherent in every consonant (_ka, kha, ga_…).
- **It carries the meaning _ādyanutpāda_** (阿字本不生, "originally unborn"). The _Mahāvairocana Sutra_ (大日経) gives this gloss.[^6] "Unborn" does not mean created before time. It is another name for emptiness: nothing arises with its own fixed substance.
- **In the _Shōji jissō gi_,** the single syllable _A_ holds the whole triad. The voiced sound is 声, the letter naming it is 字, and its meaning, the unborn, is 実相.[^7] The fourth line of the verse therefore closes on the root letter.
- **In practice,** _A_ is Dainichi's seed-syllable in the Womb Realm [[Mandala|mandala]] (胎蔵界). _Ajikan_ (阿字観, A-syllable contemplation) is meditation on a golden _A_ on a lotus within a moon disc.[^8]
- **Its origin is open.** Shingon tradition credits Kūkai with bringing _ajikan_ to Japan. Richard Payne notes that no materials clearly showing its development before Kūkai have been found or analysed, and that some researchers think the use of _A_ as a meditation device began as a Chinese or Japanese mistranslation of the _Mahāvairocana Sutra_.[^20]

## 4. Can Animals Be Buddhas?

The answer depends on the tradition.

- **Early Buddhism: not in an animal birth.** The Vinaya tells of a [[Nagas|nāga]] that took ordination disguised as a man. When it was discovered, the Buddha ruled that animals may not be ordained, because they cannot grow in the Dharma and discipline.[^9] The _Jātaka_ tales, however, show the future Buddha in many animal lives on the path to buddhahood.[^10]
- **Mahāyāna: Buddha-nature in all beings.** The _Nirvana Sutra_ teaches that all sentient beings have Buddha-nature (一切衆生悉有仏性).[^11] The Chan kōan of Zhaozhou's dog makes the question itself the practice.[^12] See [[Zen]].
- **Japan: grasses, trees and lands.** Medieval Japanese Buddhism extended buddhahood to the insentient, in the formula 草木国土悉皆成仏 (_sōmoku kokudo shikkai jōbutsu_, grasses, trees and lands all become Buddha).[^13] Kūkai's _Unji gi_ is an early source for this view.[^14]
- **Kūkai's own reading, through the verse.** The animal realm is one of the ten realms that are "fully endowed with language." In this framework an animal is already a letter of the Dharma-body. What it lacks is not buddhahood itself but recognition of it, which is the difference between "false" and "true" letters.

## 5. The Dragon Girl — _Lotus Sutra_, Chapter 12

**Where it is.** The episode is in Kumārajīva's 『妙法蓮華經』(_Miaofa lianhua jing_, Lotus Sutra), chapter 12, 提婆達多品 (the Devadatta chapter).[^15] See [[Sutras]].

**The story, in outline.**

1. Mañjuśrī reports that the eight-year-old daughter of the nāga king Sāgara (娑竭羅龍王女，年始八歲) aroused the mind of awakening "in a single instant" (於剎那頃).
2. The bodhisattva Prajñākūṭa (智積) doubts that anyone could awaken in a moment, when Śākyamuni needed countless kalpas.
3. Śāriputra objects: 「女身垢穢，非是法器」 ("a woman's body is defiled; it is not a vessel for the Dharma"). He lists the five obstacles (五障) that bar a woman from becoming Brahmā, Śakra, Māra, a wheel-turning king, or a Buddha.
4. She offers the Buddha a jewel worth a whole trichiliocosm, and he accepts it at once. She then says her buddhahood will come faster than that.
5. The assembly sees her suddenly become male (變成男子). She goes to the Stainless World (無垢世界) in the south and attains perfect awakening on a jewelled lotus.

**Its reception.**

- The main traditional readings are about **gender** and **sudden buddhahood**, not animal birth. Śāriputra objects to her female body. The text calls her 龍女, a nāga girl, and never says she is an animal.
- The "change of sex" became a point of dispute. Ryūichi Abé argues that the _Heike nōkyō_ (平家納経) frontispiece of this chapter, which shows no transformation, rejects the gender-biased popular reading.[^16] Yoshida Kazuhiko's study is the standard treatment in English.[^17]
- **The chapter is textually layered.** The _Kaiyuan shijiao lu_ (開元釋教錄, 730) records that the Devadatta chapter was translated separately in the Southern Qi, from a Sanskrit text brought from Khotan, and later added to Kumārajīva's version.[^18] In the Sanskrit, the episode sits within the stūpa chapter (chapter 11).[^19]

## Corrections Made During the Session

- "A sutra painted by brushes of mountains and ink of oceans" is a paraphrase. The original makes heaven and earth the sutra case.
- An earlier claim that the dragon girl's story "was often read as proof that no form of birth is excluded" is **withdrawn**. No academic source was found for it. Her story was read chiefly in terms of gender and sudden awakening.
- A figure for the number of Noh plays that use 草木国土悉皆成仏 came from a non-academic source and has been dropped.
