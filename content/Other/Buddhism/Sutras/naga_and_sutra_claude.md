# Nāga and Sūtra

A survey of nāga appearances across the canonical literature of Buddhism — Pali, Sanskrit, Tibetan.

---

## The Nāga as Textual Presence

The nāga is among the most frequently appearing non-human beings across all strata of Buddhist canonical literature. Rather than occupying a single text or tradition, the nāga threads through Theravāda suttas, Vinaya narratives, Mahāyāna sūtras, Jātaka stories, and the Tibetan Kangyur — shifting role and register depending on context, while maintaining recognizable attributes: protector, witness, questioner, keeper of hidden teaching, and ecological sovereign.

The sheer range of textual appearances suggests that the nāga functioned as a living conceptual tool across the tradition's development — a being whose liminal status (animal yet wise, dangerous yet devoted) made it capable of holding theological questions that more firmly classified beings could not.

---

## Theravāda Pali Canon

### Mucalindasutta (Udāna 2.1)

The earliest and most foundational nāga text in the Pali Canon. Set in the fifth week after the Buddha's enlightenment, it records the encounter between the meditating Buddha and Mucalinda, the nāga king who dwells beneath a lake.

The Udāna passage (trans. Ireland, Access to Insight; trans. Bhikkhu Ānandajoti):

> "At that time the Lord sat cross-legged for seven days experiencing the bliss of liberation. Now it happened that there occurred, out of season, a great rainstorm and for seven days there were rain clouds, cold winds, and unsettled weather. Then Mucalinda the nāga-king left his dwelling place and having encircled the Lord's body seven times with his coils, he stood with his great hood spread over the Lord's head, thinking to protect the Lord from cold and heat, from gadflies, mosquitoes, wind, sun, and the touch of creeping things."

When the storm passes, Mucalinda removes his coils, assumes the appearance of a youth, and stands with hands folded in veneration. The text ends with the Buddha's utterance on the bliss of seclusion, renunciation, and non-harm.

Theologically, the sutta encodes a principle that will recur throughout Buddhist literature: the natural world in its most powerful, potentially dangerous form — the great serpent — recognizes and spontaneously protects awakening. The nāga acts before any instruction is given.

### Mahā Samaya Sutta (DN 20)

The "Great Assembly" discourse brings together an enormous gathering of devas, nāgas, and other non-human beings who come to pay homage to the Buddha in Mahāvana. The sutta names the nāga kings present, including Virūpakkha — described elsewhere as the ruler of the Western Direction and lord of the nāgas. The enmity between nāgas and garuḍas is addressed: the Buddha protects the nāgas from garuḍa attack, and both groups subsequently take refuge in him.

This sutta is among the canonical sources for the nāga's inclusion in the _aṣṭasenā_, the eight classes of non-human beings who attend the Buddha's teachings (Buswell & Lopez, _Princeton Dictionary of Buddhism_, 2014, s.v. _aṣṭasenā_). The nāgas are present not as mythic backdrop but as members of the assembly.

### Himavanta Sutta (Samyutta Nikāya)

The Buddha uses nāgas as a structural metaphor for the gradual path. The nāgas depend on the mountains of the Himalayas for strength; when strong enough, they descend to small streams, then lakes, then rivers, and finally to the great ocean. The metaphor maps onto the practitioner's development through progressive stages of practice, pointing to the nāga's association with water as a figure of accumulation, depth, and eventual arrival.

### Four Modes of Nāga Birth (Samyutta Nikāya, trans. Bhikkhu Bodhi)

A brief but taxonomically significant passage:

> "Bhikkhus, there are these four modes of generation of nāgas. What four? Nāgas born from eggs, nāgas born from the womb, nāgas born from moisture, nāgas of spontaneous birth."

This mirrors the four modes of birth attributed to all sentient beings in Abhidhamma cosmology, placing nāgas within the same generative structures as humans, animals, and all other beings.

### The Vinaya — Pravrajyāvastu (Chapter on Going Forth)

The Vinayavastu _Pravrajyāvastu_ contains an extended passage on a nāga who takes the form of a brahmin youth and receives ordination. His true nature emerges in sleep; his coils appear. The Buddha delivers the ruling that only humans may receive ordination — not because the nāga lacks faith or aspiration, but because its identity is constitutively unstable in human terms.

The Vinaya (ii.109) records the four royal families of nāgas: Virūpakkhā, Erāpathā, Chabyāputtā, and Kanhagotamakā — evidence that nāga social structure was elaborated with care in early canonical literature.

## Jātaka Literature — The Bodhisatta as Nāga

The Pali commentarial tradition records that the Bodhisatta was born multiple times as a nāga king: Atula, Campeyya, Bhūridatta, Mahādaddara, and Saṅkhapāla.

### Campeyya-Jātaka (No. 506)

The Bodhisatta is born as Campeyya, a nāga king dwelling in the river Campā. Renouncing his palace and consort, he practices the Uposatha vows in human territory, where he is captured and used for entertainment. The story illustrates the power of precept observance and non-retaliation — _sīlapāramitā_.

### Saṅkhapāla-Jātaka (No. 524)

The Bodhisatta as nāga king submits to being seized and tormented by hunters while observing the Uposatha, without anger or retaliation. The virtue is patience (_khantipāramitā_) under extreme provocation — the nāga's natural ferocity fully transmuted into equanimity.

### Bhūridatta-Jātaka (No. 543)

Among the longest and most elaborate Jātakas. The Bodhisatta as nāga king Bhūridatta practices moral virtue with extraordinary commitment, offering his own body — skin, muscles, bones, blood — to those who demand it. The _Cariyāpiṭaka_ (pp. 85–86) identifies this Jātaka as an illustration of _śīlapāramitā_, the perfection of moral conduct. The _Maha-Prajnaparamita-Sastra_ commentary references the same parallel, noting the nāgas of such Jātakas are "practicing the uposathakamma and offer to those who want them their skin, their muscles, their bones and their blood."

These three Jātakas constitute a meditation on what complete non-harm looks like when inhabiting a body constitutionally capable of great harm. The nāga who could destroy with a glance or a breath instead practices total availability and patience — the perfections embodied in a form that exists in tension with them.

## The Mahāvastu

The Mahāvastu (Vinaya Piṭaka, compiled between 2nd century BCE and 4th century CE) records two nāga kings — Kāla and Mucilinda — interacting with the newly enlightened Buddha. Kāla extends an invitation:

> "Lord, former perfect Buddhas, the exalted Krakucchanda, Konākamuni, and Kāśyapa, lodged in my abode. Well would it be if thou, Lord, would take pity on me and also lodge in my abode."

The Exalted One dwells with Kāla for the fourth week after enlightenment. This passage is among the textual foundations for the understanding that nāgas serve as bridges across buddhic dispensations — their longevity allowing them to be living witnesses to multiple Buddhas.

## The Lalitavistara Sūtra

The _Play in Full_ (Lalitavistara), a Sanskrit biographical account of the Buddha's life accepted in Mahāyāna traditions, places nāga kings at the Bodhisatta's side before his enlightenment. Chapter 7 records that the nāgas stood on guard to protect the Bodhisattva; chapter 24 includes the encounter with Mucalinda (trans. Dharmachakra Translation Committee, 84000, v. 4.49.4, 2025). The sūtra integrates the nāga into the full arc of the Buddha's life — from conception through the events following awakening — as a consistent witness and guardian presence.

---

## The Lotus Sūtra (Saddharmapuṇḍarīka Sūtra) — The Nāga Daughter

Chapter 12 of the Lotus Sūtra, "Devadatta," contains one of the most theologically significant nāga episodes in Mahāyāna literature. The eight-year-old daughter of the nāga king Sāgara presents herself in the assembly and claims she has attained Buddhahood through her practice of the Lotus Sūtra.

Śāriputra challenges her: a female body is "defiled and unfit to be a Dharma vessel." She responds directly:

> "Spotless, bright, and of unfathomable light is that ethereal body, adorned with the thirty-two characteristic signs, pervading space in all directions... I have obtained enlightenment according to my wish; the Tathāgata can bear witness to it; I will extensively reveal the law that releases from sufferance." (Lotus Sūtra, Ch. 12, trans. Kern)

She then manifests in male form and attains full Buddhahood in the assembly.

The passage has generated extensive commentary across all Mahāyāna cultures. In Nichiren Buddhism, the Dragon Girl's instant enlightenment became the primary scriptural basis for affirming women's capacity for Buddhahood in this very life — a direct counter to the _henjo nanshi_ doctrine that required women to first be reborn as men. This is the Lotus Sūtra's most explicit statement on the universality of Buddha-nature: it is a nāga — formally classified as an animal, and female — who provides the demonstration.

## The Avataṃsaka Sūtra and Sāgara

The _Flower Garland Sūtra_ (Avataṃsaka) names Sāgara, the great nāga king, as the sovereign who presides over the world's supply of rain. Sāgara is listed among the twenty-eight guardians of the thousand-armed Avalokiteśvara — a connection that places the oceanic nāga in direct relationship with the bodhisattva of compassion (Buswell & Lopez, _Princeton Dictionary of Buddhism_, 2014, s.v. Sāgara).

## The Questions of the Nāga King Sāgara (Sāgaranāgarājaparipṛcchā, Toh 153–155)

The Tibetan Kangyur contains three sūtras under this title, distinguished by length. The Dergé Kangyur preserves them in the General Sūtra section (Toh 153, 154, 155). The medium-length version (Toh 154) takes place in Sāgara's court deep in the ocean — a rare instance of the Buddha himself traveling to the nāga realm to deliver a teaching.

The discourse addresses the ten virtues and ten non-virtues in their causal relationship to rebirth and ethical consequence. The framing — the Buddha entering the underwater palace of the nāga king — reverses the usual spatial structure of Dharma transmission and places the nāga realm at the center of the teaching event.

## Pratyutpanna Sūtra

One of the earliest Mahāyāna texts. Chapter 13 includes nāga references in connection with the direct encounter with Buddhas through samādhi practice. The nāga's association with hidden wisdom and depth — the subaqueous realm, the underground palace — resonates with the sūtra's teaching on accessing Buddhas through sustained contemplative absorption.

---

## Prajñāpāramitā Sūtras — The Nāga as Guardian of Transmission

The Prajñāpāramitā texts carry the nāga's imprint at the level of transmission history. The traditional account — that the Perfection of Wisdom teachings were entrusted to the nāgas by Śākyamuni and later retrieved by Nāgārjuna from the nāga realm — situates the nāga as custodian of the most profound philosophical teaching in Mahāyāna. Robert Mayer has argued that this guardianship model has roots in pre-Buddhist Indian culture — the _nidhiśāstras_ describe a widespread belief in nāga guardianship of sacred objects (cited in Scheible, "Nāgas and Relic Treasures in the Mahāvaṃsa," Oxford Treasure Seminar Series, 2024). Walser (_Nagarjuna in Context_, Columbia University Press, 2005) examines the historical and hagiographic dimensions of the Nāgārjuna-nāga transmission.

---

## Kangyur Nāga Texts

Beyond the _Questions of the Nāga King Sāgara_, the Tibetan Kangyur preserves texts related to nāga ritual and relationship, including:

- **Bestower of Happiness and Glory** (_klu mchod pa'i cho ga bde legs dpal ster_) — a ritual for making offerings to nāgas, composed by Mipham Rinpoche
- **The Practice for the Nāga** — by Chogyal Namkhai Norbu (restricted)
- References in the **Mañjuśrīmūlakalpa** to nāga kings brought in the hundreds of thousands to hear the Dharma

These texts indicate that the relationship with nāgas in Vajrayāna is not merely mythological but a living ritual engagement with the beings who govern water, earth, and the vitality of ecosystems.

---

## Nāga as Epithet — The Awakened One

The word _nāga_ is used as an epithet for the Buddha and for arahants. The Pali commentary tradition gives the etymology: _āgum na karotī ti Nāgo_ — "one who does no wrong is a Nāga" (Mahāniddesa 201, Pali Canon). The Buddha is _Nāga_ in the sense of one who has fully transcended transgression — the serpentine energy not suppressed but perfected. This usage dissolves the category boundary between being-type and quality-of-being: to become a nāga in the highest sense is to become faultless.
