TJōdo Shinshū, also known as Shin Buddhism or True Pure Land Buddhism, is one of the most widely practiced branches of Buddhism in Japan. Here's an overview of the Honganji school within Jōdo Shinshū:

## Origins and Founder

Jōdo Shinshū was founded by Shinran (1173-1263), a former Tendai monk, during the Kamakura period\[1]. Shinran's teachings were strongly influenced by the concept of Mappō, which held that humanity's ability to practice Buddhist teachings effectively had deteriorated\[1].

## Core Beliefs

The central doctrine of Jōdo Shinshū revolves around:

1. Reliance on Amida Buddha's Other Power (tariki) rather than self-power (jiriki)
2. Attainment of shinjin (often translated as faith or entrusting)
3. Recitation of the nembutsu (Namu Amida Butsu) as an expression of gratitude rather than a practice for gaining merit\[4]

## The Honganji School

The Honganji school is the largest branch of Jōdo Shinshū. Key points about Honganji include:

1. **Origin**: It developed from a modest temple built at the site of Shinran's mausoleum in Kyoto\[2].

2. **Early Development**: Kakushinni, Shinran's daughter, dedicated the property as a permanent mausoleum in 1277, which later became known as the Hongwanji (Temple of the Primal Vow)\[2].

3. **Growth**: Under the leadership of Rennyo (1415-1499), the eighth monshu (spiritual leader), Honganji gained significant power and influence\[3].

4. **Split**: In the late 16th century, the Honganji split into two major branches:

   - Nishi Honganji (Western Temple): Established in 1591
   - Higashi Honganji (Eastern Temple): Established in 1602\[3]

5. **Modern Era**: During the Meiji Restoration, the Honganji actively worked to protect religious freedom and modernize its practices\[2].

## Practices and Scriptures

Jōdo Shinshū followers:

- Recite the nembutsu (Namu Amida Butsu)
- Place hands together in gassho as a symbol of oneness and gratitude
- Study the Three Pure Land Sutras and the writings of Shinran\[4]

## Global Presence

The Honganji school has spread beyond Japan, with temples and followers in various countries, including the United States\[4]. It continues to adapt to modern contexts while maintaining its core teachings and practices.

Jōdo Shinshū, particularly the Honganji school, remains a significant force in Japanese Buddhism, blending traditional teachings with efforts to remain relevant in contemporary society.
