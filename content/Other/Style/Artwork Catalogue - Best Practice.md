# Artwork Catalogue — Best Practice

> **How this note relates to the others.** This one is about _the field_: what the museum and artist-legacy communities have settled on, why, and where to read further. [[Artwork Cataloguing]] is about _the house_: exactly how to write a dimension, an accession number, a medium line in this vault. Read this for the reasoning; use that one at the keyboard.

---

## 1. Why a catalogue exists

Four purposes, and they pull in different directions. Being clear which one a given field serves prevents the record from becoming an undifferentiated pile.

**Identification and recovery.** If a work is stolen, lost in transit, or destroyed, what remains is the record. Law-enforcement agencies have long held that documentation is the decisive factor — objects that were never photographed and adequately described are rarely recovered or returned.[^1] This is the purpose that justifies the dullest fields, and the one most often skipped by artists.

**Provenance.** The unbroken chain of custody from studio to present owner. For a living artist this is easy and costs nothing; reconstructed decades later it is expensive and often impossible. Every sale, gift, loan and return is a link, and the link is only free to record at the moment it happens.

**Exhibition and loan.** Curators, registrars and insurers need the same facts in the same order. A record built to museum conventions can be sent to a gallery unedited; one built ad hoc has to be rewritten each time.

**Legacy.** The Joan Mitchell Foundation, which has worked on artist legacy for close to fifteen years, frames documentation not as administration but as the mechanism by which a body of work survives its maker.[^2] An undocumented oeuvre does not become a contested oeuvre — it becomes an invisible one.

---

## 2. The standards landscape

Six standards matter. They are not alternatives; they operate at different levels.

| Standard | Level | What it gives you | Maintained by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Object ID** | Floor | Nine categories — the minimum to identify a stolen work | ICOM (from the Getty)[^3] |
| **CDWA** | Structure | ~540 categories, a small **core** subset marked as the minimum to identify and describe | Getty Vocabulary Program[^4] |
| **CCO** | Content | How to format what goes in each field | American Library Association[^5] |
| **VRA Core** | Structure | Enforces the work / image distinction | Visual Resources Association[^5] |
| **Spectrum** | Procedure | 21 collections-management procedures, from object entry to disposal | Collections Trust[^6] |
| **Getty Vocabularies** | Terminology | AAT, ULAN, TGN, CONA — controlled terms for materials, names, places, works | Getty Research Institute[^7] |

**How they nest.** CDWA says _which fields exist_. CCO says _how to fill them_. The Getty Vocabularies say _which words to use_. Spectrum says _what to do and when_. Object ID says _what you must have at minimum_. VRA Core keeps the work and its photograph from being confused.

**What a single artist actually needs.** Object ID in full, always. The CDWA core where it applies. Getty AAT terms for materials, so a future reader knows what "sumi" is. Spectrum only for the handful of procedures that arise — acquisition, loan, condition checking, rights. The rest is institutional machinery for institutions.

---

## 3. The minimum record

Object ID defines nine categories, arrived at by consensus between museums, police, customs, the art trade, insurers and appraisers.[^8] They are the floor, not the target:

1. Type of object
2. Materials and techniques
3. Measurements
4. Inscriptions and markings
5. Distinguishing features
6. Title
7. Subject
8. Date or period
9. Maker

Plus three actions: **take photographs**, **write a short description**, and **keep the record somewhere secure and separate from the work**.

That last point is the one artists reliably get wrong. A catalogue stored only in the studio burns with the studio. One artist in the CALL programme describes exactly this — a house fire that left the archives "in total disarray and lost."[^9] The record needs to live somewhere the work does not.

---

## 4. The working rule

The single most useful operational principle in the artist-legacy literature, from the CALL workbook:

> **Number — shoot — enter data. Before the work leaves the studio.**
>
> Place the inventory number on the work, photograph it, enter the data.[^10]

Everything else in this note is elaboration. A work that leaves the studio unnumbered, unphotographed and unrecorded may never be fully recoverable as a record, because the moment of best access has passed — the work is no longer in your hands, in your light, with your memory of it fresh.

The corollary is that the catalogue is maintained continuously, not in retrospective campaigns. The Foundation's own observation is that documentation is an ongoing process rather than a project with an end, and that different bodies of work will sit at different stages indefinitely — a complete inventory of paintings alongside no records at all for works on paper is the normal condition, not a failure.[^11]

---

## 5. Principles

**The record outlives the object.** Works are sold, given away, damaged, destroyed. Numbers are never reused and records are never deleted — a destroyed work keeps its entry, with the destruction recorded as an event. The catalogue is a history, not an inventory of current holdings.

**A blank field is a question; an explicit negative is an answer.** `signature: unsigned` means checked. An empty `signature:` means nobody has looked. Years later the difference is unrecoverable, so write the negative wherever the question has actually been asked.

**The work is not the image of the work.** VRA Core exists largely to enforce this.[^5] A photograph of a painting is a separate object with its own rights, its own metadata, and its own decay. For time-based work the distinction becomes urgent rather than pedantic, since the carrier fails.

**Describe before interpreting.** The descriptive fields answer _what is it_ — a reader who has never seen the work should be able to identify it from them alone. Meaning, intention and context belong in the label and concept sections, where they can be revised without corrupting the identification.

**Use controlled terms where they exist.** The Getty Art & Architecture Thesaurus has a term for most materials and techniques.[^7] Using it does not mean writing like a database; it means that when you write "kozo" you mean what everyone else means by it, and a glossary in the record explains it to anyone who does not.

**Photograph before description, not after.** Object ID's ordering is deliberate: images do the identifying work that words cannot.[^1]

## 6. Numbering

Museums number by year, source and sequence. The CALL advice to artists is deliberately looser — define a system that is simple and makes sense to you, and then apply it without exception.[^10] The second half matters more than the first.

Requirements a workable system meets:

- **Unique and never reused**, including after a work is destroyed
- **Sortable** — so the catalogue has a natural order
- **Stable** — a number is not a classification, and does not change when a work is reassigned to a different series or the series is renamed
- **Physically applied** to the work, or to its verso, mount, or storage housing

The vault's form is in [[Artwork Cataloguing#3 Accession numbers]].

## 7. Photography

The minimum set per work:

- **Overall**, straight on, even light, no keystoning, colour target in frame where colour is critical
- **Verso** for anything on paper
- **Details** — signature, seal, distinguishing features, any existing damage
- **Installed view**, where the work has one

File names carry the accession number so that image and record cannot become separated. Store at least one copy off-site: the same logic as keeping the written record away from the studio.

Distinguishing features deserve their own frame. In recovery terms they are what separates one work from a similar one — an irregularity in the paper, a repair, a particular fault in the mount.

---

## 8. Provenance

Record every transfer as it happens, with date, parties and nature of the transfer. Four events cover most cases:

| Event | Record |
|---|---|
| Completed | Date, studio |
| Sold | Date, buyer, gallery if any, price |
| Gifted | Date, recipient, occasion |
| Loaned and returned | Dates out and back, borrower, condition on return |

Provenance is the field where the cost of delay is highest, because the information is trivially available at the moment of transfer and irrecoverable a decade later. It is also the field that determines whether a work can be sold, lent or authenticated later.

## 9. Time-based media

Video, audio, software and performance carry problems the object-based standards were not designed for. Tate's definition of _time-based media_ covers film, slides, video, audio, performance and software — works that have "the dimension of time."[^12]

Three consequences, treated at length in [[Artwork Cataloguing#8 Time-based works]]:

- **Duration is a dimension**, recorded where a physical work records size.
- **The carrier fails.** Record the master format and every migration. Migration is the normal life of the work; an undocumented migration is how a work quietly becomes a different one.
- **The work exists only when installed.** The Guggenheim's position is that a media work's behaviours and limits of variability can only be established on the occasion of its installation.[^13] The display specification is therefore part of the work's identity.

Pip Laurenson, who built Tate's practice in this area, argues that much of a time-based installation is _under-determined_ — left to be settled by whoever installs it — and that artists should specify their work as "thickly" as they can, while accepting that the installation will always exceed the specification.[^14] The question that must be answered explicitly for each work is whether the display equipment is constitutive or merely a carrier, because that determines whether a future installer may substitute.[^15]

---

## 10. Rights

Three separate things, often conflated:

- **Copyright in the work** — retained by the artist unless assigned in writing. Sale of a work is not sale of its copyright.
- **Copyright in the photograph** of the work — belongs to the photographer unless assigned.
- **Reproduction permission** — what a third party may do, which is a licence granted under the above, not a transfer of them.

Record all three per work, and record the terms of any licence granted, with its date and scope. Spectrum treats rights management and reproduction as distinct procedures for this reason.[^6]

---

## 11. Legacy

The Joan Mitchell Foundation's _Career Documentation for the Visual Artist_ is the standard free guide, covering inventory creation, digital asset management, bibliography, preservation planning and career mapping.[^16] Their _Estate Planning for Visual Artists_ workbook covers the legal side — intellectual property, wills, trusts, artist-endowed foundations.[^17]

The practical point for a catalogue in progress: the fields that serve legacy are the same fields that serve everything else. There is no separate legacy record to build later. There is only this record, kept or not kept.
