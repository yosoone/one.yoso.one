## Kāmasūtra and karmamudrā

**Steve James:** Many people are familiar, at least indirectly, with the Kāmasūtra. In the popular mind, the Kāmasūtra _is_ tantric sex — many hours of lovemaking, multiple orgasms or sublimated orgasms, and so on. In your chapter "Clarifying Confusion," you emphasise that karmamudrā is not the same as the Kāmasūtra. Could you explain the difference between these two categories of teaching?

**Nida Chenagtsang:** They are different teachings, but they can have a similar base. For me, the Kāmasūtra is sex education. In the West you had the sexual revolution, and at the beginning of the last century Sigmund Freud and other great psychologists and psychiatrists wrote about sex and opened the subject. Sex became a more open subject — part of our education system.

So in the ancient Indian tradition, the Kāmasūtra \[the speaker says "karmamudrā" here] is one of the oldest forms of sex education.[^kamasutra] Some people think the Kāmasūtra is only about positions; others think it's more spiritual because there are temples connected with it. I really see it as a very precise education system — probably one of humanity's first sex education texts. In sexual intercourse, positions are important, but there's also intimacy, understanding, sharing the experience together, the orgasm of love, and respecting each other. These things are really important. So the Kāmasūtra is a basic sex education book, and I think it's very important.

Karmamudrā is more advanced. We should not get stuck at the physical level, the physical orgasmic part. We have to go beyond that, and that part is not mentioned in the Kāmasūtra. The Kāmasūtra is more physical — the physical positions and physical experiences. Karmamudrā has the physical part too, but in the end we have to go beyond it.

Both talk about mindful sex. The Kāmasūtra talks about how important it is to be mindful, and karmamudrā is exactly the same. But the Kāmasūtra probably doesn't talk about starting from self and partner — your experience and your partner's experience. In karmamudrā there is a self and a partner, and there is complete union, and that union transcends the self. From the self you reach the state of selflessness.

Put simply: in Buddhism we talk about emptiness, voidness and selflessness. We talk about a self, but we have to reach a state free from self. That's why attachment is considered dangerous — when we are attached to ourselves, it can cause all kinds of problems, pain and suffering. So we cannot get stuck with the self. What we can do is use this self to reach selflessness. That's why karmamudrā, compared to the Kāmasūtra, is much more profound.

_Karma_ here refers to the physical body \[the Tibetan _las_, "action"], and _mudrā_ is your karmic partner. When you have a partner, you transcend the self, because now there are two — you cannot think only of one. You unify these two, you and your partner, and that generates joy or bliss, which helps us transcend the self. Once we transcend the self, we also transcend the partner. So it leads directly to the non-dual state. That's why karmamudrā is very profound, and the Kāmasūtra doesn't go that far.

The great Tibetan master Ju Mipham said that once you become an expert in the Kāmasūtra, karmamudrā is much easier for you. So he presents the Kāmasūtra as the first stage and karmamudrā as the second. I like that expression.[^mipham]

## Karmamudrā, tummo and the Six Yogas

**Steve James:** Karmamudrā is often presented as part of the Six Yogas of Nāropa, and sometimes as the means to move from tummo to the illusory body practices — I'm thinking of Tsongkhapa's presentation.[^tsongkhapa] You also learned karmamudrā from Ani Ngawang _\[surname unclear]_, presumably in the context of the Six Yogas. It seems that with Aku Lhamo the teachings were given on a more ad hoc basis rather than as part of a complete system. Is that fair to say?

**Nida Chenagtsang:** The experiential part, yes. When you mention the Six Yogas — normally in the Six Yogas we don't talk directly about karmamudrā. In the Yuthok Six Yogas it's very clear. First there are two yogas for daytime, tummo and illusory body. Then two yogas for the night, dream yoga and clear light yoga. Then two yogas for the moment of death and afterlife, phowa and bardo. Those are the six yogas. In the Yuthok system it's very clear: once you are trained in the six yogas, you practise the two mudrās, karmamudrā and mahāmudrā. In many other texts this is not expressed so clearly.

What we need to know, put simply, is that first you train in tummo, and tummo is the preparation for karmamudrā. You have to train well so you can have the experience of tummo and blissful heat: you generate heat, you melt the _thigle_, and you experience that thigle as bliss. This experience is very similar to an orgasmic or pre-orgasmic state. Before orgasm, the heart beats fast, body temperature rises, and joy or pleasure intensifies. That feeling is very close to tummo.[^tummo]

Normally you do tummo more mentally — you generate the heat mentally. So it's a perfect preparation for karmamudrā. When the tummo fire melts the thigle and you have these experiences, there is also a physical training: _tsalung trulkhor_. You should do lots of physical exercises too. Not all tummo is the same — some is more mental, some is more breathing and breath-holding, and some is more physical, like trulkhor. In any case, in the Six Yogas we say tummo is the cornerstone, the foundation of the other yogas. So it's very clear that once you are highly trained in tummo, if you continue, your next step is karmamudrā.

Within karmamudrā there are two types: karmamudrā itself, with a physical partner, and _jñānamudrā_, with an imaginary partner. My teacher Ani-la gave me the teachings, but in her own practice she does jñānamudrā. She taught me the jñānamudrā system.

In the Tibetan tradition we have the scholar style and the style of the great _mahāsiddhas_. The mahāsiddha style is experiential; the scholar style follows the text. According to the texts I received from Ani-la, that's the classical, official training. But the experiential part, the actual practice, comes from Aku Lhamo.

Today the actual karmamudrā is not taught publicly. One reason is that sex is seen as taboo everywhere, even in the Tibetan tradition. Even today some great masters, when they reach the karmamudrā part of a text, say, "That's not important now," or "You don't need that now," or "That's not for the public," and they skip it.

For instance, Tilopa's Mahāmudrā teaching, the _Song of the Ganges_.[^tilopa] He teaches very clearly about Mahāmudrā, the realisation of the ultimate nature of mind — a perfect Mahāmudrā teaching. But at the end he says: if you don't understand Mahāmudrā, practise karmamudrā. And in his karmamudrā instruction he says very clearly that we have to melt our thigle: it descends, you hold it, you reverse it, you spread it. He actually uses these four words, and he says this practice will help you understand the nature of Mahāmudrā.

What I'm saying is that most Mahāmudrā teachers today teach Mahāmudrā, but when they come to karmamudrā they skip it — "It's an enhancement practice," "We don't need it." I think that's really a pity. Different great masters express the relationship between Mahāmudrā and karmamudrā in different ways. I mention Tilopa's view because it's essential: he says if you get Mahāmudrā you don't need karmamudrā, but if you don't get Mahāmudrā you need karmamudrā.

The Yuthok Nyingthig system is also very clear. Yuthok talks about karmamudrā with tsalung, yogic exercises, and he mentions two types: karmamudrā for those trained in advanced tsalung trulkhor, and karmamudrā for those not trained in advanced physical yoga. He also says this type of practice is for young people and those with more sexual desire who want to practise it. But some people, because of their vows — they are celibate monks or nuns — cannot practise it. Or they are too old, or they have sexual dysfunction, or from birth they don't experience sex as joyful or pleasurable, whether because of trauma or physical conditions. He says that in these cases you practise \[Mahāmudrā — the speaker says "karmamudrā"].

That's why I like these two views together. Tilopa says: try Mahāmudrā; if it doesn't work, go to karmamudrā. Yuthok says: focus on karmamudrā; if somehow you cannot practise it, practise Mahāmudrā. Put these two systems together and it really makes sense.

Today, of course, karmamudrā is a secret practice — but exactly like Mahāmudrā. Mahāmudrā is secret too, yet many great masters teach it publicly and we find many Mahāmudrā books, commentaries and translations everywhere. When it comes to karmamudrā, people say, "Karmamudrā is secret." Actually karmamudrā is no more secret than Mahāmudrā. They are equally secret — _chakchen_ and _lekyi chakgya_ \[the Tibetan names for mahāmudrā and karmamudrā].

Karmamudrā is not taught publicly partly because of the sex taboo, which I think is one of the main reasons. The other is that many people think that to practise karmamudrā you must be highly trained in the yogic exercises, tsalung trulkhor — and now everybody says nobody is highly trained or qualified, so nobody is allowed to teach or practise karmamudrā.

In some traditions it's true that you must first train in tummo and the yogic exercises, and once you are completely ready — able to hold your energy, draw it up, hook it back, reverse it — you are ready for karmamudrā. That is the classical way. But it's also true that there is another part of karmamudrā that is purely experiential and doesn't require all this. That's why Yuthok distinguishes karmamudrā for tsalung-experienced practitioners and for non-tsalung-experienced practitioners. I really like the way he made that clear.

After Yuthok, other great masters talked about karmamudrā in different ways too. One great master from my hometown of Rebkong, _\[name partly unclear — rendered in the captions as "Kamla Namkha Gyatso"]_, a great and crazy yogic master, wrote a great text on karmamudrā. In it he talks about the Ati Yoga style of karmamudrā — the direct experience of karmamudrā, not connected with tsalung trulkhor. So even if you are not highly trained in trulkhor, you can still practise karmamudrā in a very experiential way. In my book I quoted one of these teachings. Aku Lhamo first mentioned and taught this to me. At that time I just knew the experiential way it goes; I hadn't read or gone into those practices. Later, in my own practice, I realised that this part is very important.

The reason I put it in my book is to let people know that a highly qualified master, great in both the Mahāmudrā and the Ati Yoga or Dzogpa Chenpo traditions — respected by masters of all schools — actually taught this direct, experiential karmamudrā practice. Otherwise some people say that without tummo it becomes just a kind of formula, and many people say the karmamudrā tradition is lost: "We had it, but we don't talk about it now." We cannot say, "Ancient people had sex, and we can't have it."

Even Je Tsongkhapa — as you know, one of the most brilliant Tibetan tantric Buddhist scholars — was very precise and systematic when he taught tantra, quoting great Indian masters exactly. In one of his teachings on tummo he says there are different systems, and some are the mahāsiddha, great-yogi style. For those, he says, you don't need to prove everything in a philosophical or theoretical way. These were great masters who achieved enlightenment, and there is a reason they presented the teachings in a simple, direct way — not confirming every word with "because Nāgārjuna said this, because Candrakīrti said this, because the Buddha said this, because this tantra said that."

Some people love this intellectual part; they want to know everything precisely, what's right and what's wrong. For them, a great master like Je Tsongkhapa's tantric teachings are perfect. But for others — we are not that intelligent, and what matters to us is practice and experience, not running after who is right and who is wrong. The mahāsiddhas didn't say, "This is right because Nāgārjuna said it." They did the practice, had experiences, and knew how to transmit the teachings. If we understand these two ways of teaching and transmitting, we don't need to struggle and fight so much about who's right and who's wrong.

## Vajrolī mudrā and the purpose of advanced practices

**Steve James:** In your book you make the distinction between karmamudrā practices that require a grounding in tsalung trulkhor and those that don't. **Nida Chenagtsang:** In the Yuthok Nyingthig, karmamudrā is clearly part of thigle training. With tummo and illusory body we train our channels (_tsa_) and our energy (_lung_). Once we have trained the body as channel and the energy as mantra or lung, then we train the mind, and there we are talking about thigle. Thigle is a kind of essential fluid, and according to tantra it's connected with the mind. When we experience joy and bliss, it increases; when we experience sadness and dullness, it decreases. So it's very important to work with thigle.

We have the _dzé kyi thigle_ — substantial thigle, _bindu_ in Sanskrit, something more physical. In my book I say that, in my view, these substantial thigle are hormones — the love hormones and happy hormones.[^hormones] These are used especially for people with desire and attachment. We have many kinds of desire and attachment in life, but sexual desire is one of the most powerful. So for people with lots of sexual energy, we talk about substantial thigle — chemical substances responsible for our mood and feelings of joy, happiness and bliss. I'm convinced the tantric texts are talking about the happy and love hormones.

Then we have _ngak kyi thigle_, mantra thigle — chanting mantras — used more for angry people. We are always talking about the three mental poisons: attachment, anger and ignorance. Substantial thigle is for desire, mantra thigle for anger, and _lung gi thigle_, wind or energy thigle, for ignorance. I didn't put this part in my book, and I think it's a little bit of a pity; I should have made thigle a little clearer. In the book I was talking about the thigle of substances, which is closely connected with desire.

The nature of that thigle is bliss — _dewa_ in Tibetan, _sukha_ in Sanskrit, in ordinary terms the feeling of pleasure. There is a white one and a red one, which we can call the essence of solar and lunar energy, the essence of the sun and the moon. Men and women — one has the sun and one the moon. With this practice we mix solar and lunar energy, and within ourselves we can complete ourselves.

There are male and female deities in union. We think there are two deities, but actually they are the expression of one deity manifesting as two. We see ourselves as single, but in tantra we are already in union: one part is solar energy, one part is lunar energy. When we feel separated from others or from our partners, not in union, that is the problem.

I think Indian hatha yoga talks about this too: _ha_ and _tha_, sun and moon, and _yoga_ means union — so hatha yoga means the union of sun and moon.[^hatha] That's exactly what we talk about in tummo: the union of solar and lunar energy. You increase the solar energy, which melts the lunar energy — the fire melts the butter, the butter drips into the fire, and the forces unify completely. Heat increases and bliss increases. That's the basic theory of tummo. The more you increase the solar energy, the more the lunar energy melts. Why do we burn and melt? The more you melt, the more you burn, the more they unify completely. I don't practise hatha yoga, but if you explain the name theoretically, it's the unification of solar and lunar energy — and that is tummo practice, which is also the foundation of karmamudrā.

So men and women — one has the moon and one the sun. We put them together, bring them up, and spread them through the body. We can also mix our own sun and moon, since each of us has solar and lunar energy. If we do only that solo practice, it's called jñānamudrā. We don't need a physical partner, only an imaginary or meditative partner, and we mix our own solar and lunar energy and become complete. Then you're not alone, not single anymore. You're free from loneliness, free from sadness, and you reach bliss by yourself. Many meditators get this. In this part of practice we call it the completion or perfection stage. If you reach that level, solar and lunar are completely mixed, and you have perfected yourself.

So perfection doesn't come externally; it is our inner quality. You can perfect your spiritual journey by yourself — you don't need to chase others to perfect yourself. In ordinary life some people say, "I can't find a partner. Without a partner I'm not happy." They think the partner gives them perfection. Physically, that's true: when we are with our partner, in love, it gives the perfection of that moment. That's why in karmamudrā we need to find a physical partner. But with jñānamudrā it's not always the case that we need a partner to be happy, and that alone we're unhappy. Some people can find this inner partner, their own bliss, their own completeness or perfection. I think it's very beautiful how this practice is presented both ways, as karmamudrā and jñānamudrā.

[^palden]: Palden Lhamo (_dpal ldan lha mo_, Śrī Devī) is the principal female dharma protector of Tibetan Buddhism; Gönpo (_mgon po_) is the Tibetan name for Mahākāla. _Kyab_ (_skyabs_) means "protection" or "refuge," so _Lhamo Kyab_ and _Gönpo Kyab_ mean "protected by Lhamo" and "protected by Gönpo."

[^jangbu]: Dorje Tsering Chenaktsang, pen name Jangbu, is a Tibetan poet, novelist and filmmaker from Rebkong, widely regarded as one of the leading contemporary Tibetan writers.

[^gendun]: The captions render the author as "Kendam"; the likeliest reading is Gendün Chöphel (1903–1951), whose _Treatise on Passion_ (_'dod pa'i bstan bcos_, completed 1938) was the best-known Tibetan work in the Kāmaśāstra genre. English: Gedün Chöpel, _Tibetan Arts of Love_, trans. Jeffrey Hopkins with Dorje Yudon Yuthok (Ithaca, NY: Snow Lion, 1992). For the author's life and context, see Donald S. Lopez Jr., _Gendun Chopel: Tibet's Modern Visionary_ (Boulder: Shambhala, 2018).

[^kamasutra]: Vātsyāyana, _Kamasutra_, trans. Wendy Doniger and Sudhir Kakar (Oxford: Oxford World's Classics, 2002). Doniger and Kakar's introduction presents the text as a broad treatise on pleasure, courtship and social life, not primarily a manual of positions — broadly supporting the speaker's "sex education" framing. It is not a tantric text, which is the distinction the speaker draws.

[^mipham]: Ju Mipham Namgyal Gyatso (1846–1912) composed his own Kāmaśāstra treatise, which Gendün Chöphel explicitly positioned his _Treatise on Passion_ in relation to. The specific statement attributed to Mipham here is as reported by the speaker and has not been checked against the text.

[^tsongkhapa]: Tsongkhapa's main Six Yogas commentary, _A Book of Three Inspirations_, is translated in Glenn H. Mullin, _Tsongkhapa's Six Yogas of Naropa_ (Ithaca, NY: Snow Lion, 1996). See also Glenn H. Mullin, _The Practice of the Six Yogas of Naropa_ (Ithaca, NY: Snow Lion, 2006).

[^tummo]: Two physiological studies of tummo are relevant: Herbert Benson et al., "Body Temperature Changes during the Practice of g Tum-mo Yoga," _Nature_ 295 (1982): 234–236; and Maria Kozhevnikov et al., "Neurocognitive and Somatic Components of Temperature Increases during g-Tummo Meditation," _PLoS ONE_ 8, no. 3 (2013): e58244. Both measure heat generation; neither studies bliss or sexual arousal, so the speaker's comparison with pre-orgasmic states is experiential rather than tested.

[^tilopa]: Tilopa's _Ganges Mahāmudrā_ (_phyag rgya chen po gang gā ma_), sung to Nāropa, is a foundational Kagyu Mahāmudrā text. Its closing lines do address karmamudrā. The four terms the speaker paraphrases — descending, holding, reversing and spreading the drops — are a standard completion-stage sequence in Tibetan tantric literature.

[^vajroli]: The most thorough academic study is James Mallinson, "Yoga and Sex: What Is the Purpose of Vajrolīmudrā?," in _Yoga in Transformation: Historical and Contemporary Perspectives_, ed. Karl Baier, Philipp A. Maas and Karin Preisendanz (Göttingen: Vienna University Press, 2018), 181–222. Primary passages are translated in James Mallinson and Mark Singleton, _Roots of Yoga_ (London: Penguin Classics, 2017).

[^hormones]: The speaker's identification of "substantial thigle" with hormones is his own interpretive bridge, not a traditional claim. On the neurochemistry of sexual response and bonding, see Barry R. Komisaruk, Carlos Beyer-Flores and Beverly Whipple, _The Science of Orgasm_ (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins University Press, 2006); and Larry J. Young and Zuoxin Wang, "The Neurobiology of Pair Bonding," _Nature Neuroscience_ 7 (2004): 1048–1054.

[^hatha]: The "ha = sun, tha = moon" reading is a later esoteric etymology. Jason Birch shows that in early sources _haṭha_ means "force," referring to forceful methods: Jason Birch, "The Meaning of _haṭha_ in Early Haṭhayoga," _Journal of the American Oriental Society_ 131, no. 4 (2011): 527–554.

[^abuse]: For scholarly treatment of sexual misconduct scandals in Western Buddhist communities, including Tibetan lineages, see Ann Gleig, _American Dharma: Buddhism Beyond Modernity_ (New Haven: Yale University Press, 2019).

[^consort]: On the historical and contemporary role of the tantric consort (_gsang yum_) and its ethical ambiguities, see Holly Gayley, "Revisiting the 'Secret Consort' (_gsang yum_) in Tibetan Buddhism," _Religions_ 9, no. 6 (2018): 179. The host's attribution of the Yogini Project is given as spoken and has not been verified.

[^lelung]: Lelung Zhepé Dorje (1697–1740), a Gelug-trained lama and Nyingma treasure revealer who wrote candidly about his own sexuality. His birth in 1697 places his writing in the early eighteenth century, not the seventeenth as the speaker says.

[^porn]: The "ninety-nine percent" figure is hyperbole. A US survey found that 73 percent of teens aged 13–17 had viewed online pornography: Common Sense Media, _Teens and Pornography_ (San Francisco: Common Sense Media, 2023). On links between pornography and sexual dysfunction, the evidence is contested: compare Brian Y. Park et al., "Is Internet Pornography Causing Sexual Dysfunctions? A Review with Clinical Reports," _Behavioral Sciences_ 6, no. 3 (2016): 17, which argues for a link, with Goran Landripet and Aleksandar Štulhofer, "Is Pornography Use Associated with Sexual Difficulties and Dysfunctions among Younger Heterosexual Men?," _Journal of Sexual Medicine_ 12, no. 5 (2015): 1136–1139, which found no robust association.

[^microchimerism]: The study referred to is almost certainly William F. N. Chan et al., "Male Microchimerism in the Human Female Brain," _PLoS ONE_ 7, no. 9 (2012): e45592, from the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Research Center in Seattle (not the UK). It found male DNA in the brains of some women, but the authors attributed this mainly to cells from a male fetus during pregnancy (fetal microchimerism). It did not show that intercourse or ejaculation transfers DNA to the brain. The speaker's reading goes beyond what the study supports.

[^pairbond]: The host does not name the study. The most frequently cited source for this claim is Nicholas H. Wolfinger, "Counterintuitive Trends in the Link Between Premarital Sex and Marital Stability," Institute for Family Studies, 6 June 2016, which found an association between more premarital partners and higher divorce rates. The data are correlational and do not establish that the number of partners itself impairs bonding.

[^trauma]: For contrasting clinical perspectives on body-based trauma healing, see Bessel van der Kolk, _The Body Keeps the Score_ (New York: Viking, 2014), which supports somatic approaches; and David A. Treleaven, _Trauma-Sensitive Mindfulness_ (New York: W. W. Norton, 2018), which documents how meditation can re-traumatise survivors without appropriate safeguards. Body-based practice with survivors of sexual abuse is generally recommended only alongside qualified trauma-informed care.

[^nyingma]: On Mahā, Anu and Ati Yoga as the three inner tantras of the Nyingma system, see Dudjom Rinpoche, _The Nyingma School of Tibetan Buddhism: Its Fundamentals and History_, trans. Gyurme Dorje and Matthew Kapstein (Boston: Wisdom Publications, 1991).

[^proverb]: The Chinese idiom 抛砖引玉 (_pāo zhuān yǐn yù_), "throw a brick to attract jade" — to offer one's modest contribution so that others will respond with something better.

[^prostate]: Jennifer R. Rider et al., "Ejaculation Frequency and Risk of Prostate Cancer: Updated Results with an Additional Decade of Follow-up," _European Urology_ 70, no. 6 (2016): 974–982. In this US cohort of about 32,000 men, reporting 21 or more ejaculations per month was associated with a lower risk of prostate cancer than reporting four to seven. The finding is an association, not a prescribed target.

[^pois]: The clinical term is postorgasmic illness syndrome (POIS). It was first described in Marcel D. Waldinger and Dave H. Schweitzer, "Postorgasmic Illness Syndrome: Two Cases," _Journal of Sex & Marital Therapy_ 28, no. 3 (2002): 251–255.

[^spermatogenesis]: The "seven days" reflects Tibetan medical theory of the seven bodily constituents (_lus zungs bdun_), in which regenerative fluid is the final refinement of nutrition, as set out in the _Four Tantras_ (_rgyud bzhi_). Biomedically, a full cycle of sperm production takes roughly 64–74 days, even though sperm are produced continuously: Carl G. Heller and Yves Clermont, "Spermatogenesis in Man: An Estimate of Its Duration," _Science_ 140 (1963): 184–186.

[^urges]: The _Four Tantras_ list natural urges that should not be suppressed — including urination, defecation, sneezing, coughing, vomiting and seminal emission — and describe the disorders that follow from suppressing them. The same principle appears in Āyurveda as _vegadhāraṇa_.
