[[Japan]] | [[_Shinsei]] | [[9 Placement and Direction|The Scroll]]

A working reference for the Japanese hanging scroll: its anatomy, the proportional system that governs it, the mounting formats and what each one is licensed to carry, and the practice of displaying, handling and storing it.

---

## What a kakejiku is

A _kakejiku_ (掛軸, hanging roller; also _kakemono_ 掛物, hanging thing) is a painting or piece of calligraphy — the _honshi_ (本紙, main sheet) — mounted onto a flexible backing of silk and paper so that it can be hung, rolled, and stored. The mounting is not a frame. It is a second work, made by a different hand, that sets the register of the first.

The form arrived in Japan from China with Buddhism, used initially for devotional images.[^1][^2] It gained a secular life in the late Kamakura period through [[Zen]] ink painting (_suibokuga_ 水墨画), and became central in the Muromachi period with the development of the _tokonoma_ (床の間, display alcove) and the tea gathering.[^1] Edo-period contact with Ming mounting technique and the availability of Nishijin brocade refined it further.[^1]

Two structural facts follow from its being rolled rather than stretched:

- Everything in it must tolerate repeated rolling. This governs the choice of paper, paste, and fabric.
- Everything in it is **replaceable**. A scroll is periodically taken apart and remounted; the honshi is the only permanent element.[^3]

Read from the top down, a full _yamato_ mounting stacks: **hassō → ten → chūmawashi (upper) → ichimonji (upper) → honshi → ichimonji (lower) → chūmawashi (lower) → chi → jikugi**.[^4][^5]

| Part | Reading | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| 本紙 | honshi | The work itself, on paper (紙本 _shihon_) or silk (絹本 _kenpon_) |
| 一文字 | ichimonji | Narrow horizontal strips directly above and below the honshi; the highest-grade fabric in the mounting |
| 中廻し | chūmawashi | The middle band framing the honshi on all four sides; the choice that most determines how the mounting reads |
| 柱 | hashira | Pillars — the vertical members of the chūmawashi, flanking the honshi |
| 天 / 地 | ten / chi | Heaven and earth — the outermost upper and lower panels, in the plainest fabric |
| 風帯 | fūtai | The pair of narrow bands hanging down over the ten, dividing it roughly into thirds |
| 露 | tsuyu | Dew — the small decorative thread tufts at the lower tips of the fūtai |
| 八双 | hassō | The half-round wooden bar at the very top |
| 鐶 | kan | The fittings on the hassō through which the hanging cord passes |
| 掛緒 | kakeo | The flat hanging cord |
| 巻緒 | makio | The flat cord that binds the scroll when rolled |
| 軸木 / 軸棒 | jikugi / jikubō | The lower roller — its weight is what holds the scroll flat |
| 軸先 | jikusaki | The end-caps on the roller: wood, lacquer, ceramic, horn, ivory |
| 筋 | suji | Hairline fabric strips, 1.5–3 mm, used as dividing lines in plainer formats |

Two conventions worth noticing. Fabric quality **increases with proximity to the honshi** — plainest at ten and chi, finest at the ichimonji.[^6] And the mounting is designed to be read from a seated position, which is why the proportions are weighted upward.[^2]

## The proportional system

Sizes are not arbitrary. They cascade from a single small decision — the width of the ichimonji — outward. A common Japanese workshop rule, using the traditional _shaku_ (尺, about 30 cm) and _sun_ (寸, about 3 cm):[^7]

1. **Ichimonji:** upper and lower together about two sun (roughly 6 cm), split 2:1 or 3:2 in favour of the upper.
2. **Lower chūmawashi** = the combined ichimonji measurement.
3. **Upper chūmawashi** = twice the lower chūmawashi.
4. **Chi** = upper + lower chūmawashi combined.
5. **Ten** = twice the chi.
6. **Hashira** = the same as the lower chūmawashi, or slightly narrower.
7. **Fūtai width** = about one twenty-fifth of the total scroll width.

**Worked example.** Ichimonji 6 cm total (4 above, 2 below) → lower chūmawashi 6 cm, upper 12 cm → chi 18 cm → ten 36 cm. Mounting above the honshi: 4 + 12 + 36 = **52 cm**. Below: 2 + 6 + 18 = **26 cm**. The whole mounting resolves to a **2:1** top-to-bottom weighting — which is exactly the Kantō standard stated independently: the upper ichimonji twice the lower, the ten twice the chi.[^1][^2] Kansai practice runs slightly shorter at the top.[^1]

The rule is a starting point, not a law; mounters adjust to the work and the alcove.[^7]

### Standard finished sizes

| Name | Height | Width |
|---|---|---|
| 尺八幅 _shakuhachi-haba_ | 190 cm | 64.5 cm |
| 尺五幅 _shakugo-haba_ | 190 cm | 54.5 cm |
| 尺三幅 _shakusan-haba_ | 164 cm | 44.5 cm |
| 行灯 _andon_ | 140 cm | 54.5 cm |
| 尺幅 _shaku-haba_ | 140 cm | 35 cm |
| 尺幅横物 _shaku-haba yokomono_ | 135 cm | 64.5 cm |

**尺五幅 is the default** — the most common size by a wide margin.[^8] Size is chosen against the tokonoma, not against the artwork alone.[^8]

## Mounting formats

The formats are graded by the calligraphic hierarchy _shin–gyō–sō_ (真・行・草, formal–semi-formal–informal), the same scale that orders script styles and garden design.[^9][^10]

| Grade | Format name | Carries |
|---|---|---|
| 真 _shin_ | 仏表具 _butsu-hyōgu_, Buddhist mounting | Buddhist images, _myōgō_ (名号, sacred names), _chinsō_ (頂相) portraits, sutras[^10] |
| 行 _gyō_ | 大和表具 _yamato-hyōgu_, three-section mounting | Calligraphy, landscape, bird-and-flower, warrior subjects — the general-purpose format[^9][^10] |
| 草 _sō_ | 輪補表具 _rinpō_ / 茶掛表具 _chagake_ | Tea-room scrolls; the least formal register[^10] |

Each grade subdivides again — _shin-no-shin_, _shin-no-gyō_, and so on — giving eight variants in all, since there is no _sō-no-shin_.[^11][^9] When someone says _yamato-hyōgu_ without qualification they usually mean 行の行 (_gyō-no-gyō_).[^11]

**Other formats:**

- **仏表具 / 本尊表具** — the ten and chi fabric wraps around the _outside_ of the chūmawashi as a continuous surround (総縁 _sōberi_). This configuration is reserved for Buddhist and Shinto subjects and should not be used for secular work.[^1][^11]
- **丸表具 / 袋表具** (_maru-_ / _fukuro-hyōgu_) — Chinese in origin; one or two fabrics only, with or without _suji_ hairlines. Plain, and much used for literati work.[^9][^5]
- **明朝仕立て** (_minchō-jitate_) — the bag mounting with slim side panels added; for Chinese poetry and literati painting.[^9]
- **唐表具** (_kara-hyōgu_) — fewer fabrics with thin dividing lines; associated with Shinto subjects.[^9]
- **文人表具** (_bunjin-hyōgu_) — the literati simplification: some elements omitted, the middle section extended.[^1]
- **茶掛 / 利休表具** — a narrowed chūmawashi for the tea room; the hashira run five _bu_ (about 1.5 cm) or less, markedly narrower than a standard mounting.[^1][^12]

**Multiples.** Scrolls are also mounted as sets — 双幅 (pairs), 三幅対 (triptychs, for example Kannon, gibbon and crane), 四幅対 (the four seasons), and up to 十二幅対.[^2]

## Materials and construction

**The honshi is lined, not glued to a board.** _Urauchi_ (裏打ち, backing) applies successive layers of thin _washi_ to the reverse with wheat-starch paste, and it is the structural heart of the object.[^3] The layers, from the work outward:

| Layer | Reading | Role |
|---|---|---|
| 肌裏 | hadaura | First lining, directly against the honshi |
| 増裏 | mashiura | Intermediate reinforcing layer or layers |
| 総裏 | sōura | Final overall backing — replaceable on its own, without a full remount[^3] |

**Pastes.** Fresh wheat-starch paste (正麩糊 _shōfunori_) is strong and used where permanence is wanted; _furunori_ (古糊, aged paste, stored for years under controlled conditions) is weak and flexible, used for the layers that must stay supple and be reversible later. Aging it reliably outside a traditional workshop is notoriously difficult.[^3][^13] _Funori_ and _konnyaku_ are also in the repertoire.[^3]

**Papers.** _Mino_ and _misu_ papers for linings, _uda_ paper (宇陀紙, clay-loaded) for final backings, _kōzo_ (楮) for reinforcement strips.[^3]

**The trade.** The mounter is a _hyōgushi_ (表具師) or _kyōji_ (経師). _Kyō-hyōgu_ (京表具, Kyoto mounting) was designated a national traditional craft in 1997 and registered as a regional collective trademark in 2007.[^14] Kyoto mounting tends toward quieter, more harmonised silks; Edo practice historically favoured bolder combinations and slightly different proportions.[^15]

**Remounting** is routine maintenance, not damage, and does not in itself reduce a work's value when done with appropriate materials.[^15] Because rolling generates creases over time — and creases become losses — periodic professional inspection, roughly every five to 10 years under normal display use, is the standard counsel.[^15][^3]

## Commissioning a mounting

Practical notes for having your own work mounted — relevant to the scroll dimension of [[_Shinsei]]:

- **The work will be wetted.** Urauchi involves damp paste and lining paper applied across the entire back. Sumi on washi is ideal for this. Media that lift, bloom, or resist adhesion — heavy acrylic, thick gouache, anything with real impasto — are problematic. State the medium before the mounter starts.
- **Leave margin.** The mounting needs a border of the honshi's own support to grip. Agree the allowance with the mounter before finishing the painting, not after.
- **Format carries meaning.** The _shin–gyō–sō_ grading is a statement about the subject, and _butsu-hyōgu_ in particular is reserved for sacred subjects.[^1][^10] A formal Buddhist surround on a secular abstract is a legible error, not a neutral aesthetic choice.
- **Fabric selection is the mounter's decisive act.** Colour, weave and pattern are chosen against the work; the ichimonji gets the finest cloth.[^6][^15] Bring the actual piece, in the light it will hang in if possible.
- **Ask about the paste.** Whether furunori is used in the flexible layers is a real question about how the scroll will age and how easily it can be remounted a century from now.[^13]
- **Commission the box with the scroll.** The paulownia box and its inscribed lid (箱書 _hakogaki_ — signature, seal, title) are part of the object's identity; a scroll separated from its original box loses standing.[^6]

## Display

### Hanging

The tokonoma is not entered, which is why the _yahazu_ (矢筈, forked pole) exists: it lifts the kakeo onto the hook from outside the alcove.[^16]

1. Untie the makio and gather it up against the kakeo.
2. Lift the kakeo onto the hook with the yahazu.
3. Unroll slowly, supporting the weight with the other hand the whole way down. Never let it drop — the sudden load falls on the cords and the mounting.[^16]
4. Level it, then let the **fūtai fall free**.[^2][^16]

A _fūchin_ (風鎮, a weighted ornament hung from the jikusaki) is sometimes used to steady a scroll in a draught, but it adds tension and is not universal practice.

### Rolling and putting away

Roll it **while it is still hanging**, not on the tatami.[^16]

1. Start loosely from the bottom, holding both jikusaki, keeping slack rather than tension at first.
2. Guide the side edges in with the thumbs so they do not creep out.
3. Past the midpoint, take the weight in one hand.
4. **Never roll the fūtai into the scroll** — they crease permanently. Leave them hanging, then fold them flat along their existing creases once the roll is complete.[^16]
5. Slip the _makigami_ (巻紙, protective paper) partway in so the cord cannot impress the mounting.
6. Bring the makio to the front and wrap about three turns, rotating the scroll rather than passing the cord around it; keep the turns parallel with no gaps or overlaps. Finish by tucking a loop under the kakeo in a へ shape.[^16]
7. Into the box lying on its pillow supports, wider side down, lid seated flush.[^16]

### Season and occasion

Rotation is the point of the form. Typical pairings:

| Season / occasion | Subjects |
|---|---|
| Late winter | Plum in first flower |
| Spring | Cherry, warblers |
| Summer | Water, cool mountain scenes |
| Autumn | Maple, chrysanthemum, harvest moon |
| Winter | Snow scenes |
| Celebration | Crane and tortoise; pine, bamboo and plum (松竹梅 _shōchikubai_) |

Two to four changes a year is common practice — and, not coincidentally, good for the object.[^17][^15]

## The tea scroll

In the tea room the scroll outranks every other utensil. 『南方録』(_Nanpōroku_, Record of the Southern Temple) — the Edo-period record of Rikyū's teaching[^18] — puts it plainly:

> 掛物ほど第一の道具はなし。客・亭主ともに茶の湯三昧の一心得道の物也、墨蹟を第一とす。
>
> There is no utensil of the first rank like the hanging scroll. For guest and host alike it is the thing by which one enters the Way through the single-mindedness of tea; _bokuseki_ stand foremost.[^19]

The categories in use: _bokuseki_ (墨蹟, Zen monks' calligraphy), _ichigyōmono_ (一行物, single vertical lines), _yokomono_ (横物, horizontal scrolls), _shōsoku_ (消息, letters), _gasan_ (画賛, painting with inscription), plus _shikishi_ and _tanzaku_.[^19] What is revered is the _meaning_ of the words and the attainment of the person who wrote them — not the calligraphy as decoration.[^19]

Tea mountings are correspondingly restrained: narrow pillars, quiet cloth, the informal _sō_ register.[^12][^10]

## Care and conservation

| Factor | Guidance |
|---|---|
| **Light** | Museum practice for paintings _with textile mountings_: maximum 50 lux, and a cumulative exposure limit of about six months in any five years, with extended rest if exceeded. UV blocked entirely.[^20] |
| **Humidity** | Stable mid-range relative humidity, commonly given as 45–55%. Above about 70% brings mould and paste failure; very dry conditions cause embrittlement and delamination.[^15] Humidity is the scroll's chief enemy.[^21] |
| **Airing** | _Mushiboshi_ (虫干し, insect-airing) — unroll and air twice a year on clear days in spring and autumn. After a display in a humid season, dry it out on a clear day before boxing.[^21] |
| **Storage** | Paulownia (桐 _kiri_) box; a double box, kiri inside a lacquered outer, for better moisture buffering; _futomaki_ (太巻, a fat paulownia core fitted over the roller) to widen the roll radius and reduce creasing.[^2] |
| **Pest control** | Traditional insect incense, replaced regularly. **Not naphthalene** — it damages the jikusaki.[^2][^21] |
| **Hanging position** | Do not place valuable objects directly beneath a hanging scroll.[^21] |
| **Inspection** | Professional review every five to 10 years in normal use; remount when creasing or the sōura demands it.[^15][^3] |

The single most useful habit: **hang it for weeks, not years**. Continuous display is what destroys scrolls, and the seasonal rotation that tradition prescribes for aesthetic reasons is also the conservation regime.
