Japanese Buddhist thought, which assimilated into Japanese culture over centuries, has profoundly shaped the literature and haikus that feature cherry blossoms (

_sakura_). While no canonical Buddhist texts from India mention the blossoms, Japanese texts and poetry, particularly by monks and poets, use the flower as a powerful metaphor for the Buddhist concept of impermanence (_mujo_). 

Cherry blossoms in Japanese Buddhist literature

Monk Saigyō (1118–1190)

Saigyō, a revered classical poet, was deeply influenced by Buddhist philosophy, and his love for cherry blossoms became a central theme in his work. He viewed the transient beauty of the blossoms as a reflection of life's impermanence. 

**Famous poem:**

> _Negawaku wa / hana no shita nite / haru shina n / sono kisaragi no / mochizuki no koro_ 
>
> **Translation:**\
> "If only I might die / beneath the blossoms in spring / under the full moon / of the second month." 

- **Buddhist meaning:** This verse is a plea for a peaceful death under the cherry blossoms during the second month of the lunar calendar, which coincides with the traditional date of the Buddha's passing into nirvana. This aligns the poet's mortal end with a profound spiritual event, accepting death as a natural part of the cycle of existence.
- **Contradiction:** Some of Saigyō's poems also express an internal conflict between his attachment to the worldly beauty of the blossoms and his spiritual vow of detachment. This is typical of his work, which often grapples with these emotional and spiritual struggles. 

Zen monk Ikkyū Sōjun (1394–1481)

Ikkyū was a Zen master whose poetry often used unconventional and direct language to convey core Zen concepts. His take on cherry blossoms highlights the Zen idea that ultimate truth and beauty arise from emptiness. 

**Famous poem:**

> _Break open a cherry tree / and there are no flowers; / but the spring breeze / brings forth myriad blossoms._ 

- **Zen meaning:** This verse suggests that the potential for truth and beauty is not inherent in the physical object (the tree) but is a manifestation of a deeper, ephemeral process (the spring breeze). It relates to the Buddhist concept of non-attachment by showing that beauty is not possessed but arises spontaneously and dissolves. 

Haiku master Matsuo Bashō (1644–1694)

Bashō, a lay Zen Buddhist, masterfully incorporated Buddhist themes of nature and impermanence into his haikus. For Bashō, the transient beauty of the blossoms evoked the gentle, poignant sadness known as _mono no aware_. 

**Famous haikus:**

> _Toshi doshi ya / sakura o koyasu / hana no chiri_ 
>
> **Translation:**\
> "Year after year / they fertilize the cherry / the fallen petals." 

- **Buddhist meaning:** This haiku illustrates the cycle of life, death, and renewal. The falling petals are not lost but return to the earth, enriching the soil for new blossoms. This echoes the Buddhist cycle of rebirth, where endings enable new beginnings. 

> _Samazama no / koto omoidasu / sakura kana_ 
>
> **Translation:**\
> "How many, many things / they call to mind / these cherry-blossoms!" 

- **Buddhist meaning:** This verse perfectly expresses _mono no aware_, the bittersweet awareness of the impermanence of things. The blossoms' return each year triggers a flood of memories—of past springs, people, and events—creating a powerful mix of nostalgia and appreciation for the present. 

Contextualizing the verses

The verses by Saigyō, Ikkyū, and Bashō demonstrate the evolution of Japanese Buddhist thought on cherry blossoms:

- **Saigyō** reflects on the fleeting beauty in a way that suggests a bittersweet, almost romanticized, acceptance of impermanence.
- **Ikkyū** approaches it from a more direct, philosophical Zen perspective, emphasizing the deeper truth beyond physical appearance.
- **Bashō** embodies the aesthetic sensibility of _mono no aware_, capturing the emotional resonance of the flowers' transience.
