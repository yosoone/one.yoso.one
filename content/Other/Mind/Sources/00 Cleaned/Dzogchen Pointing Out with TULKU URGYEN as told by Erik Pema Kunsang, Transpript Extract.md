# Meeting Rinpoche in Retreat

_Cleaned interview transcript. Speaker labels, punctuation, and paragraphing added; filler words and false starts removed. Uncertain names and terms are marked in the footnotes._

---

**Speaker:** I had been in retreat up near Milarepa's cave in Yolmo,[^1] three days' walk north of the Kathmandu Valley. When I came back, I had learned enough Tibetan, both literary and spoken, that I could translate for Rinpoche.

One day he talked at length about the phrase _rangjung yeshe_[^2] and its meaning. I had learned what the words meant: _rang_ means "existing by itself," and _yeshe_ is some kind of state of mind that the buddhas have. So I said, "Yes, I know what it means."

He leaned forward and said, "Do you _really_ know what it means?"

I must have looked unsure, because I did not really know. I knew the superficial meaning, but not the real meaning that comes from experience. Then he said, "I think you need to see my father."

That was the first time I heard him say that his father was an expert on that level. He wrote a letter and told me, "Take this in your hand, otherwise the nun will not let you in to see him, because he is still in three-year retreat. But I think we can make one exception."

**Interviewer:** Wow. So Rinpoche was in three-year retreat, and yet you were able to meet with him. How special. What was that like? Please tell us.

**Speaker:** The nun guarding the gate, so to speak, lit a lot of incense and fumigated me all over, as if I were infected with the plague. Then I was allowed in, begrudgingly.

**Interviewer:** It reminds me a little of the story of Naropa meeting the dakini, who asks, "Do you know the meaning of this?" and then, "But do you _really_ know the meaning?" before sending Naropa to find Tilopa.[^3] How interesting. So you were allowed in after the fumigation process, and Rinpoche was there. Tell us what that meeting was like, if you could.

**Speaker:** I actually never wanted to talk about this to anybody. But Rinpoche has not just given me permission; he said, "You have to tell exactly what happened, so that it can be inspiring for other people." So this is the first time I am telling it publicly. I have written it down before, but I have never said it aloud.

I went in under the pretext of being the interpreter for an American friend, Bruce. He was sitting in front of me, and I was sitting right behind him, looking at the back of his neck. Rinpoche was in his meditation box in front of Bruce. When Rinpoche spoke, I had to lean around like this in order to see, because the ledge was quite narrow.

At some point Rinpoche was simply speaking to me, and he did not give me time to translate for Bruce. I do not know why, but that is how it happened. He said, "==You need to look this way, toward your own mind. You cannot see buddha nature by looking the other way==. We are always looking somewhere else. We listen to sounds, or we look at the scenery. It is always somewhere else." ^9ecbd6

==Then he said, "Stop that now. Let that which pays attention, which observes, which is aware, let that awareness look toward itself==." ^9f91b7

That struck a great fear in me. All of a sudden he jumped out of his meditation box and came over to where I was sitting. He put his face right in front of mine, this close, and at the same time he tapped me on the back of my neck and said, "Look here. Don't look at me. Look at the back of your head."

I thought, "The eyes cannot turn around. This is impossible." And he said, "Look here."

I started to laugh a little, not hysterically, but very shyly, and I tried to cover it up with laughing. He said, "Stop laughing. Look here," and he tapped me again on the back of the head.

All of a sudden everything fell apart. This mind that had been hiding from itself since before I was born, again and again evading the knowing of itself, completely collapsed. I almost could not speak. It lasted like that for three days. No words. Just a completely open mind in all directions.

In the scriptures they use the example of a flame on a windless night. Just a flame, totally quiet and lucid.[^4]

That was a strong blessing of his. He had that impact not just on me but on many people. He would continue until this thick coconut had broken open. That was his great quality, you could say. He wanted people to understand, and he would keep teaching until they did.

I have only translated for two people where he did not succeed in bringing them face to face with the nature of mind. It was not always in the same way, but there was always some glimpse.

**Interviewer:** What an incredible master. Thank you for sharing that. It is very inspiring; it actually gave me goosebumps. So this lasted for three days, you said?

**Speaker:** Yes. I was in that place for three days, and then it slowly tapered off and normal confusion took over again.

**Interviewer:** And you said that this mind was hiding from itself. This is one of the things you saw: that it is available, but hidden in a way, and that it is hiding itself. Is that what you said?

**Speaker:** Yes. You could call it self-perpetuating ignorance.

**Interviewer:** Self-perpetuating ignorance.

## Footnotes

[^1]: The audio transcription reads "mapus cave in yma." Yolmo (Helambu), north of the Kathmandu Valley, holds caves associated with Milarepa, which fits the "three days' walk north" description. This reading is an inference from context and should be checked against the recording.

[^2]: The audio transcription reads "Rong yes." The speaker's gloss, "existing by itself" (_rang byung_) and a state of mind of the buddhas (_ye shes_), matches _rangjung yeshe_ (Tib. རང་བྱུང་ཡེ་ཤེས་), usually rendered "self-existing wakefulness" or "self-arisen primordial wisdom." See Tulku Urgyen Rinpoche, _Rainbow Painting_, trans. Erik Pema Kunsang (Hong Kong: Rangjung Yeshe Publications, 1995), glossary.

[^3]: The interviewer refers to the traditional account in which a dakini appears to Naropa while he is studying at Nalanda and asks whether he understands the words or the meaning of the texts, prompting him to seek Tilopa. See Herbert V. Guenther, trans., _The Life and Teaching of Nāropa_ (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1963), 24–26.

[^4]: The image of a butter lamp undisturbed by wind is a standard simile for the stability and clarity of mind in Indian and Tibetan meditation texts; compare _Bhagavad Gita_ 6.19 and the śamatha instructions in Kamalaśīla's _Bhāvanākrama_.
