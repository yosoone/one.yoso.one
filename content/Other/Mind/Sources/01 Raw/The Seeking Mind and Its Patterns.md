# The Seeking Mind and Its Patterns

## 1. The problem stated

The seeking mind is not a moral failing but a structural one. It posits a gap between the seeker and what is sought, then expends itself maintaining the very gap it intends to close. Huangbo Xiyun (d. 850) formulated this as the impossibility of using mind to look for mind: the instrument of the search is identical to its object, so the search can only ever displace it.[^1] Dale Wright notes that the _Chuanxin fayao_ has a single doctrinal centre — One Mind, outside of which nothing exists — which is precisely why any movement toward it is a movement away.[^2]

Linji Yixuan (d. 866) gave the phenomenon its sharpest clinical description. Ruth Fuller Sasaki renders his diagnosis as the “ceaselessly seeking mind,” which he asks students to bring to rest — not because the goal is unworthy, but because students lack confidence in themselves and therefore run outside after something already present.[^3] His mockery of the pilgrim whose feet have flattened from tramping in search of a teacher is not anti-intellectualism; it is an argument about the topology of the search.

## 2. Terms and their range

Three registers describe the same phenomenon with different precision.

**Pali analysis.** The Second Noble Truth names _taṇhā_ (thirst, craving) as the origin of _dukkha_, and the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta specifies three channels: _kāma-taṇhā_ (craving for sense pleasure), _bhava-taṇhā_ (craving for becoming or continued existence), and _vibhava-taṇhā_ (craving for non-becoming, annihilation, escape).[^4] The third is the analytically valuable one for contemplatives: the wish to be rid of a state is itself a form of thirst. Ajahn Sumedho’s commentary makes the application explicit — the practitioner’s determination to eliminate anger, fear, or defilement is _vibhava-taṇhā_ operating under monastic cover, while the wish to be understood and appreciated as a practitioner is _bhava-taṇhā_.[^5]

**Chan/Zen analysis.** The East Asian tradition shifts the question from craving to topology. If buddha-nature is already the case — the _hongaku_ (本覚, original enlightenment) doctrine inherited from Tendai — then seeking is not merely inefficient but self-contradictory.

**Affective neuroscience.** ==Panksepp’s SEEKING system names a mesolimbic dopaminergic circuit that generates exploratory, appetitive, anticipatory states rather than consummatory pleasure.[^6] Berridge and Robinson’s dissociation of “wanting” (incentive salience, dopamine-dependent) from “liking” (hedonic impact, opioid-mediated, neurally fragile) is the empirical core: the system that pursues and the system that is satisfied are not the same system, and the first can be amplified without the second==.[^7] Attaining the object therefore does not terminate the seeking disposition; in pathological form, the disposition reorganises around ever more specific objects.[^8]

These three registers should not be collapsed. The neuroscience describes a mechanism; the Pali texts describe a soteriological structure; Zen describes a logical trap. The convergence is suggestive, not evidential.

## 3. A taxonomy of patterns

**Deferral.** The object relocates ahead of each arrival. Structurally this is not a failure of attainment but the correct output of a system whose function is anticipation rather than satisfaction.[^6]

**Acquisition.** States are collected rather than inhabited. Trungpa named this spiritual materialism: the ego annexing the path as a further possession, converting practice into ego-reinforcement.[^9]

**Reflexive measurement.** Continuous self-auditing (“am I progressing?”) instantiates the very division it hopes to resolve, producing two minds where there was one. This is Linji’s point about seeking outside — the outside is generated by the act of looking.

**Substitution.** The object changes while the grasping structure persists. Career, dharma, art, institution: the content is variable, the form invariant.

**Recoil.** Renunciation as disguised craving — _vibhava-taṇhā_. Its modern clinical cousin is spiritual bypassing, coined by John Welwood in 1984 for the use of spiritual practice to skip unresolved psychological material rather than integrate it.[^10] Welwood’s own description — using absolute truth to dismiss relative human needs — makes it a species of aversion rather than transcendence.

## 4. The counter-argument

The tradition does not, in fact, condemn seeking. The critique is directed at a misconceived seeking, and any reading that abolishes effort altogether misreads its sources.

Three pieces of evidence.

First, Dōgen. _Fukanzazengi_ opens with the objection in its strongest form: the Way pervades everything, the teaching is complete, the mirror is already clear — so why practise at all?[^11] His answer does not eliminate effort; it reverses its direction. The doctrine of _shushō-ittō_ (修証一等, practice and realisation as one), articulated in _Bendōwa_ (1231), holds that practice is not preparatory to realisation but is its full expression.[^12] Carl Bielefeldt’s textual work shows how carefully this position was constructed against both gradualist and quietist readings, and cautions against taking the hagiographic framing of Dōgen’s “great doubt” at face value.[^13]

Second, Kuoan Shiyuan’s Ten Oxherding Pictures (twelfth century). The sequence opens with searching, and the preface to the first image concedes the paradox directly: the ox has never gone astray, so why search?[^14] The answer given is estrangement, not absence. The herdsman must walk the whole sequence anyway — and the tenth image returns him to the marketplace, not to a summit.[^15]

Third, the Pali corpus itself distinguishes craving from wholesome aspiration. The desire for liberation and for others’ welfare is explicitly cultivated, which is why rendering _taṇhā_ simply as “desire” makes the Second Truth unusable.[^16]

## 5. Synthesis

The seeking mind is best understood not as an error to be deleted but as a directional problem. Its pathology is structural: it converts presence into an object, places the object at a distance, and then commits the organism’s appetitive machinery to closing a distance it has itself produced. The classical remedies do not counsel inertia — quietism is simply _vibhava-taṇhā_ with better vocabulary — but a change in what practice is understood to be _for_. When practice stops functioning as a means and starts functioning as expression, the same energy continues without generating the gap.

## 6. Open questions

- Whether the SEEKING/liking dissociation offers genuine explanatory purchase on _taṇhā_, or only an attractive homology. The methodological literature on neuro-Buddhist mapping is worth engaging before pressing the analogy further.
- How _shushō-ittō_ is operationalised outside zazen — in craft, ceremony, and bodywork, where the product is external and a client’s outcome is at stake.
- Whether the oxherding sequence is a developmental claim or a retrospective description, and what difference that makes to a teacher.
