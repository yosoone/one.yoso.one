# Review: Durkheim's Concept of _Mana_

A check of [[emile_durkheim_mana_concept_and_diagram|the mana note]], a Gemini Notebook synthesis, against Swain's 1915 translation and the scholarship since. The page references mostly hold. The problems are a few ideas credited to the wrong author, and a century of criticism the note leaves out.

## What holds up

The page references were checked against a searchable scan of the 1915 edition.[^2] These are right:

- Book II, chapter 6, "The Notion of the Totemic Principle, or Mana, and the Idea of Force" (188–204).
- Chapter 7, "Origin of the Idea of the Totemic Principle or Mana" (205–239).
- Book III, chapter 3, on imitative rites and causality (from 351; the section on causality from 362).
- Codrington's definition of _mana_, which Durkheim quotes on page 194.
- _Arungquiltha_, which Durkheim also spells _Arunkulta_: the Arunta force that "brings death" (197).

The four-stage account of how _mana_ arises is a fair summary of chapter 7.

## Corrections

1. **"Strategic misrecognition" is Bell's term, not Durkheim's.** Bell takes "misrecognition" from Bourdieu. On the pages the note cites for it, Durkheim argues that religion "expresses something real" and that "the believer is not deceived." He does call the experience a kind of "delirium," but a well-founded one.[^3] A better label for stage 4 would be "the group's force is felt as the totem's," cited to 219–226.
2. **In Australia there is no single, universal _mana_.** The note's summary and diagrams show one force running through everything. Durkheim says each clan's force stays separate, and that "the idea of a single and universal mana could be born only at the moment when the tribal religion developed above that of the clans."[^4]
3. **The phrase "homo duplex" isn't in this book.** It comes from Durkheim's 1914 essay on the dualism of human nature. _Elementary Forms_ says "man is double" and discusses the soul in Book II, chapter 8.[^5]
4. **Manitou:** Durkheim only names it in a list. The note's description of it isn't his.[^6]
5. **Physical and moral force:** Durkheim discusses this on 188–191, not 191 and 218.[^7]
6. **Section 4** leaves out Durkheim's clearest statement. He calls this force "the first form of the idea of force," and says "the idea of force is of religious origin."[^8]
7. **Mauss:** "Techniques of the Body" does not appear to discuss _mana_. Mauss's argument about _mana_ is in his and Hubert's _General Theory of Magic_ (1904), which Durkheim draws on. The essay could not be opened to rule this out.[^9]
8. **Bell:** her book mostly argues _against_ the split between belief and rite that runs from Durkheim through later ritual theory; it does not "apply" him. The Hawaiian passage on prophets (_kaula_) and priests could not be found in her book. The book dates from 1992; 2009 is a paperback reissue.[^10]
9. **"Demonstrates" overstates it.** This is Durkheim's argument, and it has been heavily criticised (next section).
10. **Vault format:** the note has no frontmatter, no backlinks and no `AI: true`. It uses author–date citations instead of Chicago footnotes.

## What the note leaves out

### Academic critique

- **The word itself.** Keesing argues that _mana_ in Oceanic languages is mainly a verb meaning "be efficacious, work." Codrington and the anthropologists who followed him turned it into a substance.[^11] Firth made the same point in 1940.[^12]
- **The word's origin.** Blust traces Proto-Oceanic \*_mana_ back to forces of nature such as thunder and wind.[^13]
- **Theory.** Lévi-Strauss read _mana_ as a "floating signifier."[^14]
- **The Australian evidence.** Stanner found Durkheim's sacred–profane split too simple for Aboriginal religion.[^15]
- **Pacific scholars today.** _New Mana_ includes Native Hawaiian scholars such as Noenoe K. Silva and Ty P. Kāwika Tengan on _mana_ as political authority and sovereignty.[^16]

### Scientific work

Collective effervescence has been tested:

- Four studies found that emotional synchrony in gatherings strengthens identity and positive feeling.[^17]
- Fire-walkers and their relatives watching showed synchronised heart rates; unrelated spectators did not.[^18]
- A meta-analysis of 50 studies found consistent effects.[^19]
- A 2023 review finds Durkheim's account of gatherings "remarkably supported" by recent results.[^20]
- Collins develops effervescence into the everyday "emotional energy" of ritual interaction.[^21]

None of this tests Durkheim's larger claim that religion, or the idea of force, _comes from_ these gatherings.

### Spiritual and Indigenous sources

For how _mana_ is lived and taught, read Māori and Hawaiian authors in their own words:

- Māori Marsden, _The Woven Universe_.[^22]
- Hirini Moko Mead, _Tikanga Māori_.[^23]
- Pukui and Elbert, _Hawaiian Dictionary_.[^24]

### Threads for practice

- **Tattooing.** Durkheim covers totemic tattoos, and argues that "tattooing should be the most direct and expressive means by which the communion of minds can be affirmed."[^25]
- **Buddhism.** Durkheim counts Buddhism as a religion without gods, whose sacred things are "the four noble truths."[^26]
- **Shinto.** Orikuchi Shinobu 折口信夫 may have used マナ (_mana_) to explain _tama_ (魂, spirit) and 外来魂 (_gairaikon_, soul that comes from outside). This is a lead only; the source has not been read.[^27]

### Translations

Karen E. Fields's 1995 translation is now the standard English one.[^28] The Japanese translation is by 山﨑亮 (2014).[^29]
