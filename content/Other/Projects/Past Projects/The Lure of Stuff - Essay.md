# The Lure of Stuff — Twenty Years On

_Based on the installation work with Victoria Whatmore (2005), her original poem, and the question: what has changed?_

---

> _We're told what to eat_
> _Told what to wear_
> _The lure of stuff_
> _Is always there._
>
> _The lure of stuff_
> _Is nothing but greed_
> _The adman has told us_
> _What we need._
>
> — Victoria Whatmore, 2005

---

## The Adman Is Gone. Something Worse Replaced Him.

In 2005, the enemy had a face. The adman — that knowing, slightly cynical figure in a suit — stood between you and your own desire, whispering what you needed. He worked in agencies, commissioned campaigns, bought space in magazines and time on television. He was powerful, but he was legible. You could see the billboard. You could turn off the TV. The manipulation was external, and its mechanisms were visible enough to mock, resist, or at least identify.

Twenty years later, the adman is gone. What replaced him has no face, no suit, no office. It is an algorithm — a system of recommendation, attention-capture, and behavioral prediction so deeply woven into the architecture of daily life that most people no longer experience it as advertising at all. They experience it as themselves. Their preferences. Their feed. Their needs.

This is the mutation the poem could not have anticipated, and it is the mutation that makes it more relevant now than when it was made.

---

## The Gap Within, Quantified

The installation framed consumerism around an abyss — something hollow within us that the economy both creates and exploits. The gap. The increasing gap. In 2005 that framing was poetic and political; in 2026 it is also, grimly, technical. The gap has been measured, mapped, and monetised with a precision the adman could only dream of.

Behavioral data now serves multiple purposes for modern monopolies — training machine learning systems to automate, control, and predict our behavior, placing us in a permanent comparison world and facilitating the commodification of the self. The gap within us is no longer simply exploited; it is _modelled_. Every moment of dissatisfaction, every late-night scroll, every purchase that fails to satisfy and leads to another — all of it feeds the system that learns you faster than you learn yourself.

Attention now functions as a scarce, tradable asset, with algorithmic dynamics that compress consumption into seconds. The attention economy — a phrase that barely existed in 2005 — has its own futures markets, its own measurement standards, its own rate of return on the hollow feeling that follows every unneeded acquisition.

The poem said the adman tells us what we need. The algorithm does not tell. It shows. It shows you, drawn from the residue of your own behavior, the precise reflection of whatever you have been reaching for — and then offers it to you, slightly out of reach, again and again.

---

## From Broadcast to Mirror

What changed structurally between 2005 and now is the direction of the message. The adman broadcast outward — he spoke to a demographic, a market segment, a presumed desire. The algorithm reflects inward. Complex, data-driven algorithmic systems act as a proprietary invisible hand, making use of immense amounts of consumer information to efficiently match supply with demand — but once a platform establishes dominance, it extracts ever more time and attention from users by controlling the flow of attention itself.

The billboard told you to want something you might not want. The feed tells you that you already want it — and the evidence is your own click history, your own pause time, your own pattern of return. Resistance is harder when the manipulation wears your own face.

This is the deeper logic of _The Lure of Stuff_ that only becomes fully visible from twenty years out: the poem named the external mechanism — the adman, the lure, the told — but the mechanism has since moved inward. The lure of stuff is now largely indistinguishable from the self that feels the pull.

---

## The Stuff Has Changed Too

In 2005, stuff was still largely physical — clothes, gadgets, cars, the objects piling up in the installation's ticker of excess. The abyss was filled with things you could hold.

Now stuff includes attention itself. The product is the scroll, the notification, the next video. Variable reward schedules identical to slot machines, combined with infinite scroll that eliminates natural stopping points, keep us in a perpetual state of cognitive switching — never quite present, never quite focused. The lure of stuff has become the lure of the next stimulus, the next signal, the next proof that something is happening. Stuff no longer needs to be physical to hollow you out.

And underneath it, the original poem's diagnosis holds with even more force. The greed is still there. The need is still manufactured. But the factory has moved inside.

---

## What the Work Knew That It Didn't Know It Knew

Looking back at _The Lure of Stuff_ from 2026, what strikes me is how accurately the installation captured the _structure_ of the problem — the loop between need, manufactured desire, acquisition, and the return of the gap — without yet being able to name how completely that loop would be automated.

The stock ticker running consumer data. The war. The corporate greed. The connection to our own manufactured needs. These felt, in 2005, like an indictment of a system you could stand outside of and critique. The installation gave you the distance to see it.

That distance is what has been most successfully eliminated in the twenty years since. The system learned that critique, too, is attention. That outrage keeps you scrolling. That the gap can be filled, temporarily, with the feeling of having noticed the gap.

The poem ends with the adman telling us what we need. In 2026, we tell it ourselves — every time we engage, react, reach for the phone in the quiet moment, fill the silence with the next thing. The adman got smart enough to let us do his work.

---

## The Question the Work Leaves

If stuff is a response to an abyss, and the abyss has now been engineered into the architecture of daily life — what is the answer?

Not, I think, more critique. The critique has been absorbed. Not minimalism as aesthetic, which became its own consumer category. Not digital detox as retreat, which became a wellness product.

Something quieter. The work of returning, repeatedly, to what actually satisfies — not as a philosophy but as a daily practice of attention. What the Japanese call _ma_: the charged quality of the interval. What the contemplative traditions call _presence_. The deliberate cultivation of a self that knows the difference between fullness and the feeling of filling.

_The Lure of Stuff_ pointed at the problem from the outside. Twenty years on, the work is to cultivate, from the inside, something the algorithm cannot model — because it leaves no data trail.
