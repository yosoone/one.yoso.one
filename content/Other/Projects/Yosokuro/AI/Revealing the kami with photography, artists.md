[[Yosokuro Index]]

There's a paradox baked into your question that actually shapes the best work on this theme: in Shinto, the kami aren't meant to be seen. The _shintai_ sits hidden inside the sanctuary, the inner halls stay closed, and a sculpture housing a deity is kept out of view. So nobody really photographs a kami head-on. The strongest photographers instead circle around _kehai_ (気配) — the felt presence or trace of something unseen — and find ways to register that rather than the thing itself.

A few artists who do this especially well:

**Yukihito Masuura** is the most literal answer. He took kehai — which he glosses as "the invisible indication" — as the guiding principle of his use of light, first while photographing Michelangelo's sculptures and the Louvre's collections, then turning to Shinto architecture. Since 2006 he has documented the _Sengu_, the cyclical rebuilding of Ise Jingū (every 20 years) and Izumo Taisha (every 60), and he's the only photographer granted access to that reconstruction at Izumo. Tellingly, he shot mostly at night, on the reasoning that since the gods are not to be seen, he went when they were invisible. His shrine series is titled _Kami no Miya_.

**Tokihiro Sato** comes at it through pure light. In his _Photo-Respiration_ (or "breath-graph") series, he makes long exposures while walking through a landscape, drawing with a flashlight at night or flashing sunlight back at the lens with a mirror by day; the lights record as traces of his presence while his moving body vanishes entirely. The forest scenes in particular read as enchanting and pagan, evoking a Japanese animism — nature with its own sentience. It's about as close as photography gets to picturing a presence without a body.

**Rinko Kawauchi**'s _Ametsuchi_ ("heaven and earth") is the one you may feel closest to where you are. She anchored it on Mount Aso's volcanic landscape, a historic site of the Shinto field-burning ritual _yakihata_, and interleaved it with constellations and tiny human figures lost in vast terrain. One of its threads runs right through Miyazaki: alongside the Aso burning, she filmed the Shiromi Kagura, a theatrical Shinto dance ceremony in Miyazaki Prefecture, plus prayers at the Western Wall and laser drawings on a planetarium ceiling. The whole series is described as a veneration of the invisible world carried forward from the distant past — using ritual as a junction between the spiritual and the real.

**Hiroshi Sugimoto** engages the sacred more architecturally and is a serious collector of Shinto and Buddhist antiquities. His site-specific show _The Descent of the Kasuga Spirit_, staged inside the 8th-century Kasuga-Taisha shrine in Nara, drew directly on Japan's spiritual past and present — and he's said that the deeper he explores Shinto and Buddhist art, the more it exposes the shallowness of contemporary art. His earlier _Sea of Buddha_ and his Enoura Observatory work sit in the same territory of sacred time.

Worth adding **Masao Yamamoto**, whose tiny, hand-aged prints don't name Shinto but carry a quiet animist charge — the sacredness of small things — that fits the same lineage.

One note, since you're in Miyazaki: you're sitting in arguably the richest place in the country for living kami-presence — Takachiho's all-night _yokagura_, the Shiiba and Shiromi traditions, the Amaterasu cave myth itself. The kagura that Kawauchi traveled to film is essentially in your backyard. If you're thinking about making your own work rather than just looking, that's the thing these artists chase across Japan, already happening around you.
