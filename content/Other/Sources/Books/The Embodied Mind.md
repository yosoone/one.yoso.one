### The argument in brief

The book asks whether cognitive science and lived human experience can reshape each other. Its answer runs in five steps:

1. **Circularity.** The scientist who studies the mind is inside what she studies (ch. 1). Mindfulness/awareness is proposed as a disciplined way to examine experience (ch. 2).
2. **No self.** Cognitivism treats mind as symbol processing (ch. 3). A search for a self in the five aggregates finds none (ch. 4).
3. **Coherence without a self.** Emergence and connectionism (ch. 5) and the twelve links, read as "codependent arising" (ch. 6), explain how habit gives life coherence without a self.
4. **No ground outside.** The Cartesian anxiety demands a fixed ground in the world or the mind (ch. 7). Enaction replaces representation: cognition brings forth a world (chs. 8–9).
5. **Worlds without ground.** Nāgārjuna's middle way makes groundlessness explicit (ch. 10). Chapter 11 turns to nihilism, Nishitani Keiji, ethics and compassion.

Thompson's 2016 introduction states the double idea at the centre of the book: "The other idea was that of _dependent origination_—that all phenomena arise dependent on causes and conditions, and therefore all phenomena lack substantial being." (p. xxi) The first idea was not-self.[^3]

### Points relevant to dependent origination and emptiness

The numbers match the gold markers in the diagram.

#### 1. Not-self in the aggregates (ch. 4)

Chapter 4 follows the early Buddhist analysis of the person into five aggregates and finds no self in any of them. This is the analysis that Madhyamaka later extends from persons to all phenomena. _Not yet read in full._

#### 2. The twelve links at any scale (ch. 6)

The authors gloss _pratītyasamutpāda_ as "codependent arising" and read the links as mutually conditioning:

> The Buddha was said to have discovered on the eve of his enlightenment not only the momentariness of the arising of the aggregates but also the entire edifice of causality—the circular structure of habitual patterns, the binding chain, each link of which conditions and is conditioned by each of the others—that constitutes the pattern of human life as a never-ending circular quest to anchor experience in a fixed and permanent self. (p. 110)

They decline to choose between the three-life and moment-to-moment readings: "The circle is an analytic structure that can be used to describe events of any duration from a single moment to a lifetime or, in the Buddhist view, to many lifetimes." (p. 111)

They place the point of freedom between craving and grasping:

> Craving is an extremely important juncture in this chain of causality. Up to this point, the links have rolled off automatically on the basis of past conditioning. At this point, however, the aware person can do something about the situation: he can interrupt the chain or he can let it go on to the next link (grasping). (p. 114)

Brasington puts the break one link earlier, between feeling and craving.[^4] The two positions can be compared in [[Dependent Origination and Emptiness]], section 1.

#### 3. The Cartesian anxiety as the two extremes (ch. 7)

> The anxiety is best put as a dilemma: either we have a fixed and stable foundation for knowledge, a point where knowledge starts, is grounded, and rests, or we cannot escape some sort of darkness, chaos, and confusion. Either there is an absolute ground or foundation, or everything falls apart. (p. 140)

The authors trace both extremes to one grasping:

> At first, the grasping mind leads one to search for an absolute ground—for anything, whether inner or outer, that might by virtue of its “own-being” be the support and foundation for everything else. Then faced with its inability to find any such ultimate ground, the grasping mind recoils and clings to the absence of a ground by treating everything else as illusion. (p. 143)

And they name the way out in Buddhist terms:

> By progressively learning to let go of these tendencies to grasp, one can begin to appreciate that all phenomena are free of any absolute ground and that such “groundlessness” (sunyata) is the very fabric of dependent coorigination. (p. 144)

This is the Kaccānagotta Sutta's pair of extremes, "exists" and "does not exist" (SN 12.15), restated for modern philosophy. The link is Claude's reading; the authors cite Madhyamaka, not the sutta.

#### 4. Enaction as mutual conditioning (chs. 8–9)

> Consequently, cognition is no longer seen as problem solving on the basis of representations; instead, cognition in its most encompassing sense consists in the enactment or bringing forth of a world by a viable history of structural coupling. (p. 205)

Their short formula: "Question 1: What is cognition? Answer: Enaction: A history of structural coupling that brings forth a world." (p. 206)

Mind and world condition each other here, much as consciousness and name-and-form lean on each other like two sheaves of reeds in SN 12.67. That comparison is Claude's; the authors themselves refuse to identify enaction with Madhyamaka (point 5). _Chapter 8, including the colour case study, is not yet read._

#### 5. Nāgārjuna: emptiness as lack of intrinsic nature (ch. 10)

The authors state their debt directly: "In fact, it is one such important tradition, the Madhyamaka, which has served as the basis for our thought in this book." (p. 219)

On what Nāgārjuna claims:

> Nagarjuna’s point is not to say that things are nonexistent in an absolute way any more than to say that they are existent. Things are codependently originated; they are completely groundless. (p. 223)

Their summary of his argument ends: "We now have a context for understanding emptiness with respect to codependent origination: all things are empty of any independent intrinsic nature." (p. 224)

They insist that the argument is exact: "It is a very precise argument, not just a general handwaving that everything is dependent on everything." (p. 225) This is a useful check on Brasington's SODAPI, alongside Anālayo's critique of "interdependence" in [[Dependent Origination and Emptiness]], section 4.

On the two truths: "Ultimate truth (paramartha) is the emptiness of that very same phenomenal world." (p. 226) And: "By now it should be obvious that the distinction between the two truths, like the analysis of the Abhidharma, was not intended as a metaphysical theory of truth." (p. 226)

They warn against turning any concept into a new ground, their own included: "Any conceptual position can become a ground (a resting point, a nest), which vitiates the force of the Madhyamaka." (p. 228) And: "We offer the vision of enactive cognitive science and of evolution as natural drift neither as a claim that this is the only way science can be done nor as a claim that this is the very same thing as Madhyamaka." (p. 228)

**One claim to qualify.** "Theravada Buddhism does not teach sunyata." (p. 220) That holds for emptiness in the Madhyamaka sense. It does not hold for emptiness as such: the Pāli discourses teach that the world is empty of self (SN 35.85) and describe a meditative entry into emptiness (MN 121).[^5]

**Nāgārjuna's date.** The authors place his work in roughly the first half of the second century CE, noting that not all scholars agree (p. 220). Westerhoff dates him c. 150–250 CE; Brasington gives c. 100 CE.

#### 6. Emptiness and compassion (ch. 11)

> Thus sunyata, the loss of a fixed reference point or ground in either self, other, or a relationship between them, is said to be inseparable from compassion like the two sides of a coin or the two wings of a bird. (p. 246)

Just before this: "It is said that the full realization of groundlessness (sunyata) cannot occur if there is no warmth." (p. 246) The chapter also describes compassion that follows no rules and responds to each situation (pp. 247–48). This connects to [[Compassion Altruism and Empathy|Compassion, Altruism and Empathy]].

#### 7. Thompson's 2016 correction

In the new introduction Thompson writes: "Specifically, I no longer accept three of the rhetorical and argumentative strategies on which we relied." (p. xxii) The three are:

- **Phenomenology as a failed project** (ch. 2). He now treats Western phenomenology as a living movement (p. xxii).
- **Buddhist philosophy as derived from meditation.** "The second strategy is our depiction of Buddhist philosophy, specifically the Indian Buddhist Abhidharma school and the writings of the Madhyamaka (middle way) philosopher Nāgārjuna, as based on meditation or as deriving from meditative experience. I now see this idea as being simplistic and inaccurate." (p. xxiii) He calls the assumption a typically Buddhist modernist claim (p. xxiii).
- **Mindfulness as inner observation** of a mental stream (ch. 4), following Hubert Dreyfus's criticism (p. xxiv). The better view, also in the book: "According to this conception, mindfulness practices should be understood as skillful ways of enacting certain kinds of embodied states and behaviors in the world, not as inner observation of an observer-independent mental stream." (pp. xxiv–xxv)

**For citing the book:** use it for enactive cognitive science and for its account of Madhyamaka. Be careful using it for how Buddhist meditation and philosophy developed historically.
