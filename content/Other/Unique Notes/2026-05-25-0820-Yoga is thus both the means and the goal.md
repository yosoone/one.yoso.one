# Claude

The idea that yoga is both the means and the goal is one of the most profound insights in its philosophy. Here's what that means:

The word itself points to this duality. The word "yoga" comes from the Sanskrit word yuj, meaning "union" or "to join." ==Union is simultaneously what you practice and what you arrive at==.

Yoga is the practice and the destination. Yoga is the goal and the practice, the means and the end. Every tradition within yoga points back to this same circularity — the path and the destination are made of the same substance.

==The goal is a state of mind, achieved through cultivating that same state==. According to Patanjali's Raja Yoga, the main goal of yoga is Chitta Vritti Nirodaha — a calm, clean, waveless mind. Anything done to calm and clean the mind can be called yoga. In other words, the quality of mind you're trying to reach is also the quality you must bring to the practice itself.

==The ultimate aim is pure consciousness== — which is already your nature. The ultimate goal of yoga is a sustained state of pure awareness called Moksha or Samadhi — the transcendence of the mind to realize the "true self." This experience of pure consciousness is understood to be our true nature.

The physical practice serves the larger whole. The goal of asanas (postures) is to still the body in order to enter a state of meditation. The postures are created to still the body in order to silence the mind — the postures alone are not enough.

All paths lead to the same place. Yoga is a vast science, rich in philosophy, steeped in diverse traditions, lineages, and practices — all ultimately leading to one goal: Yoga itself.

This is why the statement holds such depth: you don't practice yoga to get somewhere else. You practice yoga to become more fully what yoga already is — union, awareness, stillness.
